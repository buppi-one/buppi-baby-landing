**Quick answer:** at 20 weeks you are halfway through pregnancy, in the 5th month and the 2nd trimester. Your baby is **about 25.6 cm** from head to heel and weighs **about 330 g**, roughly the size of a banana. This is the time of the mid-pregnancy anatomy scan and, in England, the week the whooping cough vaccine is usually offered.

"Twenty weeks" means twenty completed weeks, from 20 weeks and 0 days to 20 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. The due date is set at 40 weeks, so there are about 20 to go. Converting weeks to months is approximate, which is why your care team always talks in weeks.

## How your baby is developing at 20 weeks

The jump in the measurements stands out: about 15 cm last week and about 25.6 cm now. Your baby did not grow ten centimetres in seven days. Week-by-week guides such as the NHS one measure from head to bottom until week 19, because the legs are curled up and hard to see. From week 20 the length is given from head to heel.

The weight of about 330 g is the median on the World Health Organization fetal growth charts, estimated by ultrasound. Your scan report has your own baby's measurements, and your care team interprets them.

At this stage the skin is covered in vernix, a white, greasy layer thought to protect it from long contact with the amniotic fluid. Your baby is more active every day, kicking, stretching, turning over and possibly sucking a thumb, which practises the sucking reflex needed for feeding after birth.

According to the NHS, first movements are usually felt between 16 and 24 weeks. Early on the feeling is like bubbles or a light flutter low in the belly. Many women only notice it after 20 weeks, especially in a first pregnancy. If you reach 24 weeks without feeling anything, tell your midwife or doctor.

## What is changing in your body

Around 20 weeks the top of the uterus reaches the level of your navel. From here on, fundal height, which is the distance in centimetres from the pubic bone to the top of the uterus, tracks gestational age quite closely, and that is why a midwife or doctor measures it with a tape at later appointments.

Common symptoms at this stage include:

- Leg cramps, often at night. Rubbing the muscle and pulling your toes up towards your shin usually helps. Stretching before bed and after long walks helps too.
- Twinges at the sides of the belly, usually from the ligaments that support the growing uterus being stretched. Pain that is severe or does not ease needs checking.
- Feeling hot, tired and sleeping in shorter stretches.
- More vaginal discharge, clear or milky white, which is expected in pregnancy. If it turns yellow or green, smells strong or itches, book an appointment. Watery fluid that leaks is something else and needs same-day care.
- Heartburn, a slower gut, gums that bleed easily, the occasional nosebleed and darker patches on the face.

Not every woman gets all of these, and intensity changes from one pregnancy to the next. Appetite often goes up as well. Even so, the NHS points out that there is no need to eat for two and that, in general, extra calories only come into the picture in the third trimester. Your midwife or doctor follows your weight over time and gives advice that fits you.

## Antenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ by country, so here is what applies in England and what applies in the US.

In England, the NHS plans at least 10 appointments for a first baby and at least 7 if you have given birth before. After the 16-week appointment, the next routine ones are at 25 weeks (first baby) and 28 weeks, so around week 20 the main appointment is the scan. The 20-week screening scan is offered to everyone, usually between 18 and 21 weeks, and can be done up to 23 weeks in some cases. The sonographer looks closely at your baby's bones, heart, brain, spinal cord, face, kidneys and tummy, checks growth and looks at the placenta. It takes around 30 minutes, you can bring someone with you, and you may be asked whether you want to know the sex. If the baby is not lying in a good position, you may be asked back for a second attempt. No scan can find everything, and a result that needs a second look is a reason to talk it through with your team before drawing conclusions.

In the US, the timing of the anatomy scan is set by your ob-gyn or midwife. International ultrasound guidance (ISUOG) places the routine mid-pregnancy scan between 18 and 24 weeks.

The whooping cough vaccine is where the two countries differ most. The NHS usually offers it around week 20; it can be given from week 16 and works best before week 32. In the US, the CDC and ACOG recommend it between 27 and 36 weeks, as early in that window as possible. Both repeat it in every pregnancy. The antibodies you make cross the placenta and help protect your baby in the first months, before their own vaccines start. The flu vaccine is recommended at any stage of pregnancy in both countries.

The next milestone is the glucose test at 24 to 28 weeks. In the US it is a routine test for all pregnant women, and it may be done earlier if you have risk factors. In England it is only for women with a higher chance of gestational diabetes.

## What to do this week

1. If your scan is not booked yet, sort it out now. In England, tell your midwife or GP if you are between 18 and 23 weeks and have not had it.
2. Ask about the whooping cough vaccine: now in England, and at which visit it is planned in the US.
3. Keep moving. The general recommendation is about 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, starting with 10-minute walks if you have not been active. Some situations call for restrictions, so check with your care team what suits you.
4. Start pelvic floor exercises, which help prevent leaks when you cough or sneeze. Your midwife or a pelvic health physiotherapist can teach you the technique.
5. In the UK, ask your midwife or doctor for the maternity certificate (MAT B1), which is issued from 20 weeks and is needed to claim maternity pay and benefits.

> Get checked without waiting for your next appointment if you have vaginal bleeding, fluid leaking from the vagina, severe or persistent abdominal pain, cramps or contractions that come at regular intervals, a fever, pain or burning when you pee, a severe headache that does not go away, changes in your vision or sudden swelling of your face, hands or legs. A severe headache, vision changes and sudden swelling can be signs of high blood pressure in pregnancy, which can appear from 20 weeks. If you already feel your baby move every day and notice the movements have slowed or stopped, that is also a reason to be assessed. Call your midwife or maternity unit in the UK, or your ob-gyn or labor and delivery unit in the US, and use emergency services if you cannot reach them.
