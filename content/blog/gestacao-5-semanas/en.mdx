**Quick answer:** at 5 weeks you are at the start of the 2nd month and in the 1st trimester. The embryo is **about 2 mm long**, the size of a sesame seed, and the heart starts to form and to beat around now. This is the week many women find out they are pregnant, and the practical next step is booking your first antenatal appointment.

"Five weeks" means five completed weeks, from 5 weeks and 0 days to 5 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. Ovulation usually happens about two weeks after that date, so conception was around three weeks ago. That is why the count already says five weeks when your period is only recently late.

## How your baby is doing at 5 weeks

At this stage the baby is still called an embryo. According to the NHS week-by-week guide, the nervous system is developing and the brain and spinal cord are taking shape quickly. The heart starts to form and beats for the first time around this week, which does not yet mean it will show on a scan.

The embryo already has some blood vessels of its own, and some of them will make up the umbilical cord. The placenta is being formed now too. It will carry oxygen and nutrients to the baby and take waste away. The NHS also describes the first outlines of the face.

The nervous system forming in these first weeks is the reason folic acid comes up so early in pregnancy advice: it plays a part in that process. Which supplement to use and for how long is something to settle with whoever looks after your pregnancy.

## What is changing in your body

A late period is usually the first sign. Home pregnancy tests are most reliable from the first day of a missed period. A positive result is almost always right. A negative one is less reliable, especially if you tested early or your cycles are irregular: test again a few days later, and see your doctor if it is still negative and your period has not come.

The most common signs at this point include:

- Extreme tiredness and sleepiness, a frequent complaint in the first trimester.
- Sore, more sensitive breasts.
- A sharper sense of smell, a metallic taste in your mouth and going off foods you used to like. The smell of hot food, perfume or cleaning products can suddenly bother you.
- Nausea, with or without vomiting, at any time of day. It usually starts before 9 weeks, and not every pregnant woman gets it.
- Needing to pee more often.
- Mild cramps, a bit like period pains, and a milky white discharge.
- Mood that swings more than usual.

Light pink or brown spotting can happen in early pregnancy and does not always mean a problem. Even so, any bleeding in pregnancy should be checked by a health professional.

If nausea has already arrived, a few simple things help: rest, avoid the smells and foods that set it off, eat something dry such as toast or a plain biscuit when you wake up, have small and frequent meals, choose cold food when the smell of hot meals bothers you, and sip fluids little and often. If none of that works, your midwife or doctor can assess whether you need treatment.

Finding out stirs up emotions, whether or not the pregnancy was planned. The NHS estimates that around 1 in 5 women feel anxious or stressed during pregnancy. Talking about it with someone you trust and with your care team usually helps.

## Antenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ by country, so here is what applies in England and what applies in the US.

In England, you can tell your GP or refer yourself directly to local maternity services as soon as you have a positive test. The first midwife appointment, called the booking appointment, takes place between 8 and 12 weeks and lasts around an hour. The NHS plans at least 10 appointments for a first baby and at least 7 if you have given birth before, with 2 routine scans. At the booking appointment your midwife works out your due date from your last period, goes through your health history and offers blood tests, including screening for infectious diseases.

In the US, call your ob-gyn or midwife's office now. The timing of the first prenatal visit is set by the practice, and blood and urine tests are ordered at that visit.

The first routine scan in England is the dating scan, offered at around 10 to 14 weeks. If you do not know the date of your last period or your cycles are irregular, that scan is what dates the pregnancy. With an embryo of about 2 mm, a scan done now may show very little and need repeating, which can make the wait harder. The NHS says that at week 6 the heartbeat can sometimes be picked up on a vaginal ultrasound. If your doctor asks for a scan earlier, there is a clinical reason for it.

The flu vaccine is recommended at any stage of pregnancy in both countries. The first appointment is a good moment to have your vaccination record checked.

## What to do this week

1. Book your first antenatal appointment and write down the first day of your last period, the date every calculation starts from.
2. Make a list of the medicines, supplements and skin or hair treatments you use and take it to the appointment. If you take a regular medicine or have a long-term condition, tell the doctor who manages it as soon as possible, without waiting for your first antenatal appointment, and do not stop anything on your own.
3. If you are not taking folic acid yet, speak to your midwife, doctor or pharmacist in the next few days about the supplement that suits you, without waiting for the appointment date.
4. Stop smoking and drinking alcohol, and cut down on coffee, tea and other sources of caffeine. If that is hard, ask your care team for help.
5. Look after the quality of what is on your plate without making portions bigger, because there is no need to eat for two.

Whether to share the news now is your decision. Many people wait until the end of the first trimester because most losses happen in that period. A review published in The Lancet estimated that about 15% of recognised pregnancies end in miscarriage. According to the RCOG, the UK's college of obstetricians, the most common cause of early loss is thought to be a problem with the embryo's chromosomes, and in most cases no reason is identified. Some women prefer to tell people early so they have support close by.

> Get checked without waiting for your appointment if you have vaginal bleeding, severe or persistent abdominal pain, pain that settles low on one side of your tummy, pain in the tip of your shoulder, feeling very dizzy or fainting, a fever, pain or burning when you pee, or vomiting with very dark urine, not peeing for more than 8 hours or no fluids staying down for 24 hours. One-sided pain, with or without bleeding, can be a sign of an ectopic pregnancy, which usually causes symptoms between weeks 4 and 12 and needs prompt assessment. In the UK, contact your GP or NHS 111, or your early pregnancy unit if you have its number; in the US, call your ob-gyn. Sudden severe pain, shoulder tip pain, feeling very dizzy, fainting or heavy bleeding mean calling 999 in the UK or 911 in the US straight away.
