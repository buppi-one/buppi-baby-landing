**Quick answer:** at 39 weeks you are in the 9th month, at the end of the third trimester, and your pregnancy has reached what is called full term. Your baby is **about 50.7 cm** long from head to heel and weighs **about 3.4 kg**, roughly the size of a small watermelon. Labour could start any day, so this is the week to know the signs and when to head to hospital.

"Thirty-nine weeks" means 39 completed weeks, from 39 weeks 0 days to 39 weeks 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. Your due date is set at 40 weeks, but the NHS notes that most women go into labour within a week either side of it.

## Your baby at 39 weeks

The length comes from the NHS week-by-week guide and is measured from head to heel. The weight is the median of the World Health Organization fetal growth charts. These are population references: 2 in 10 babies fall outside the 2.9 kg to 3.9 kg range, and a bigger or smaller baby on a scan does not, on its own, mean something is wrong. Your care team reads your results.

Under ACOG's classification, a pregnancy from 39 weeks 0 days to 40 weeks 6 days is full term. Before that, from 37 weeks to 38 weeks 6 days, it is early term. ACOG also points out that the brain and lungs keep maturing in these last weeks.

Your baby's skin, almost see-through a few weeks ago, now has a tougher layer. The NHS explains that it protects the organs better and helps with temperature control. On top of it, many babies still have vernix, a white, creamy coating that protects the skin. Some are born covered in it, others with almost none left.

Movements should not slow down at the end of pregnancy. According to the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (RCOG), they increase until about 32 weeks and then stay at that level until birth. With less room, each movement may feel different.

Pregnancies vary a lot at this stage. Only about 1 in 20 babies arrives on the due date, according to ACOG, and reaching 40 weeks with no sign of labour is common.

## Changes in your body

As your baby moves down into the pelvis, pressure low in your bump usually increases and back pain can get worse. The NHS explains that, as the baby drops, it starts pressing near your spine. Many women find breathing easier once the bump drops, but need to pee more often and sleep in short stretches.

Discharge may increase. The NHS says it should be thin, white and not smell much. A thick, sticky blob of mucus that is yellowish or slightly bloody is probably your mucus plug (the "show"), which sealed the cervix. It is one of the first signs labour is coming, though you could still have days to wait. Discharge with a strong smell, itching or soreness is not expected and is worth getting checked.

Braxton Hicks (practice) contractions may come more often. They are irregular, short and spaced out. In labour the pattern changes: contractions get stronger, longer and regular, and do not ease with rest.

The NHS also mentions sudden bursts of energy to tidy the house or wash baby clothes, known as the nesting instinct, along with the usual late-pregnancy complaints: heartburn, constipation, leg cramps, swollen hands and feet, and tiredness. Swelling that comes on suddenly, especially in the face, needs checking straight away.

If back or pelvic pain is getting in the way, mention it at your appointment. Do not take any medicine on your own: in pregnancy, only on the advice of your midwife or doctor.

## Prenatal care at this stage

In England, the NHS schedule includes an appointment at 38 weeks, one at 40 weeks for a first baby, and at 41 weeks if you have not given birth. They are a chance to check your blood pressure, your bump and your baby, and to go over the signs of labour. If you have not had the RSV vaccine, the NHS says it can still be given right up until labour: ask your midwife or GP.

In the US, ACOG explains that if pregnancy goes past the due date, testing to check the baby's wellbeing may be recommended from 41 weeks, and induction may be recommended at 41 weeks. Your ob-gyn will talk through the plan for your situation.

## What to do this week

1. Agree a plan for when contractions start. The NHS advice is to phone your maternity unit or midwife when contractions last at least 60 seconds and come every 5 minutes. If you have had a caesarean before, have a higher-risk pregnancy or live far from the hospital, your team may tell you to come in earlier: follow the plan you agreed with them.
2. Watch for your waters breaking. The NHS points out it may not be a gush; it can be a trickle. If you think it has happened, note the colour of the fluid and call your midwife or doctor even without contractions, because once the waters break the baby's risk of infection rises. If you cannot tell whether it is urine, discharge or fluid, get checked.
3. Keep your hospital bag, notes and documents by the door, and arrange who will drive you.
4. Keep up your pelvic floor exercises. The NHS describes them as pretending you are having a pee and stopping midflow.
5. Rest when you can. The NHS notes that babies do not always cry straight away and that a newborn's hands and feet can look blue at first, because circulation is still settling.

> Go to hospital without waiting for your next appointment if you have strong, regular contractions, fluid leaking from your vagina, vaginal bleeding or a fever. Call straight away if your baby is moving less than usual or the pattern of movements changes. A severe headache that does not go away, blurred vision or flashing lights, severe pain just below the ribs and sudden swelling of the face, hands or legs can be signs of high blood pressure and need immediate assessment. Contact your maternity unit or midwife in the UK, or your ob-gyn in the US. For a seizure, fainting, severe breathlessness, chest pain or heavy bleeding, call 999 in the UK or 911 in the US.
