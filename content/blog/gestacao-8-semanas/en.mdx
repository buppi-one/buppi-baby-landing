**Quick answer:** at 8 weeks you are at the end of the 2nd month and in the 1st trimester. Your baby is **about 16 mm** from head to bottom, roughly the size of a raspberry. This is when nausea tends to be at its most noticeable and, if your pregnancy care has not started yet, the time to book the first appointment.

"Eight weeks" means eight completed weeks, from 8 weeks and 0 days to 8 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. With a regular cycle, conception happened about two weeks after that date, so the embryo has been developing for around six weeks. Converting weeks to months is approximate, which is why your care team always talks in weeks.

## How your baby is developing at 8 weeks

Growth is fast: about 10 mm last week and about 16 mm now. The measurement runs from head to bottom because the legs are still very short and stay curled up. Weight is missing from the table because the reference charts used in this series only start at week 14.

According to the NHS week-by-week guide, the head, which was tucked down onto the chest, starts to uncurl a little. The arms are longer than the legs, since the upper body grows faster than the lower body. The legs are lengthening too, but the knees, ankles and toes have not formed yet.

The placenta is getting ready to take over delivering oxygen and nutrients and carrying waste away. To do that it grows small branches that anchor it to the wall of the uterus. In the coming weeks the embryo starts to be called a fetus.

You cannot feel your baby move yet, and that is still a long way off. First movements are usually felt between 16 and 24 weeks. The sex has to wait as well: it only shows on ultrasound later on, and you can usually ask at the mid-pregnancy scan.

## What is changing in your body

From the outside almost nothing shows. The NHS points out that it will probably be a few more weeks before you look pregnant, although bloating and bigger, sore breasts can already make clothes feel tight. A supportive non-wired bra and elastic waistbands tend to be more comfortable.

The most common symptoms at this stage:

- Nausea, with or without vomiting, at any time of day. It usually starts before 9 weeks and, for most women, is gone by about 14 weeks. For some it lasts until 16 to 20 weeks. Nausea that appears for the first time after 9 weeks may have another cause and is worth mentioning at your appointment.
- Tiredness and sleepiness at odd hours.
- Needing to pee more often.
- A metallic taste in your mouth, a sharper sense of smell and going off foods you used to like.
- Mild cramps, a bit like period pains.
- More discharge, clear or milky white, with no strong smell. Loose cotton underwear and avoiding perfumed soap may help prevent thrush.
- Headaches and mood swings.
- Thicker, shinier hair and darker patches on the face.

For nausea, the NHS suggests simple measures: rest, because tiredness makes it worse; eat something dry, such as toast or a plain cracker, before getting out of bed; have small, frequent meals of plain, low-fat food; choose cold food when the smell of hot meals bothers you; sip fluids through the day. Food and drinks containing ginger help some women. If none of this works, treatment exists and your doctor or midwife decides on it, so do not take anything on your own.

Not every woman gets all of these, and having no nausea does not point to a problem. This stage affects mood too. Anxiety and worry are common early on, and telling someone you trust and your care team is a good first step.

## Antenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ by country, so here is what applies in England and what applies in the US.

In England, tell your GP or refer yourself to your local maternity service as soon as you know you are pregnant. The first midwife appointment, called the booking appointment, usually takes place between 8 and 12 weeks and lasts around an hour. You go through your health and your options for care, and you are offered screening tests for infectious diseases and for conditions such as Down's syndrome. The NHS plans at least 10 appointments for a first baby and at least 7 if you have given birth before, with 2 routine scans. The first, the dating scan, is offered at around 10 to 14 weeks and gives a more accurate due date than counting from your last period.

In the US, the timing of the first prenatal visit and of the first ultrasound is set by your ob-gyn or midwife, so call the office once you have a positive test and ask when they want to see you.

On vaccines, the NHS recommends the flu vaccine at any stage of pregnancy. The whooping cough vaccine comes later: in England it is usually offered around week 20 and can be given from week 16, while in the US the CDC and ACOG recommend it between 27 and 36 weeks.

On supplements, the NHS advises folic acid until at least week 12. Follow what your own care team recommends.

## What to do this week

1. Book your first appointment if you have not yet. In England, contact your GP surgery or self-refer to your local maternity service. In the US, call your ob-gyn or midwife and check what your insurance plan covers and which tests need prior authorization.
2. Make a list of everything you use (medicines, vitamins, herbal teas, skin creams) and take it to the appointment. If you are treated for a long-term condition, tell your doctor you are pregnant and do not stop the treatment on your own.
3. Stop smoking and drinking alcohol, and cut down on coffee and other drinks with caffeine. If that is hard, ask your care team for support.
4. Plan your eating around the nausea: keep a plain cracker by the bed and carry small snacks in your bag.
5. Keep moving if you feel well. The general recommendation is about 150 minutes of activity a week, and you can start with 10-minute walks each day. Check at your appointment what suits you.

> Get checked without waiting for your next appointment if you have vaginal bleeding, even a small amount, severe abdominal pain or pain on one side, pain in the tip of your shoulder, severe dizziness or fainting, a high temperature, or pain or burning when you pee. The same applies if you are vomiting and cannot keep food or fluids down for 24 hours, have very dark urine or have not peed in more than 8 hours, feel weak or dizzy when you stand up, or have lost weight. Discharge that turns yellow or green, smells strong or itches needs an appointment. Call your midwife, maternity unit or early pregnancy unit in the UK, or your ob-gyn in the US. With very severe pain, heavy bleeding or fainting, call 999 in the UK or 911 in the US.
