**Quick answer:** at 33 weeks you are in the 8th month, in the third trimester. Your baby is **about 43.7 cm** long from head to heel and weighs **about 2.1 kg**, roughly the size of a pineapple. The bones are hardening, your bump may tighten now and then, and it is a good week to pack your hospital bag.

"Thirty-three weeks" means 33 completed weeks, from 33 weeks and 0 days to 33 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. The due date is set at 40 weeks, so there are about 7 to go.

## Your baby at 33 weeks

The length comes from the NHS week-by-week guide and is measured from head to heel. The weight is the median on the World Health Organization fetal growth charts. Both are reference values: in that study, 2 in 10 babies fell outside the 1.8 kg to 2.4 kg range, and a different estimate on your scan does not point to a problem on its own. Your prenatal care team is the one to interpret the result.

According to the NHS, the bones are hardening, except for the skull bones, which stay flexible and separate and, in the NHS description, only firm up at around 12 to 18 months of age. That give in the head makes the journey through the birth canal a little easier.

The NHS describes the brain and nervous system as fully developed this week, though maturing continues: the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) notes that the brain and lungs keep developing into the last weeks of pregnancy.

Between 33 and 36 weeks the baby may turn head-down, the most common position for birth. Some turn earlier and some later, and your team keeps track of the position at your late-pregnancy appointments.

A baby born now is premature. The World Health Organization classes babies born from 32 weeks to before 37 completed weeks as moderate to late preterm. Because the brain and lungs are still maturing, signs of labour at this stage need to be checked quickly.

Movements stay frequent. With less room the feeling changes, but the amount should not drop towards the end of pregnancy. The reference is your baby's usual pattern, and a clear drop in it is a reason to go in the same day, without waiting to see if it improves.

## What is changing in your body

Feeling very tired is common now: the NHS points out that you are carrying a few extra kilos. You may also feel a weight pressing on your pelvis, which the NHS says can be a sign that the baby is already head-down. If that heaviness comes with cramps or contractions at regular intervals, get checked.

Your bump may tighten for 20 to 30 seconds, in the NHS description, and then relax. These are practice contractions, or Braxton Hicks: irregular, spaced out and usually painless. If they become painful or start coming at regular intervals, call or go to your maternity unit or hospital. Before 37 weeks, period-like cramps, pelvic pressure, a constant dull ache low in your back, more discharge or a change in it, and leaking fluid are also signs of premature labour.

Other complaints the NHS lists for this stage:

- Backache and pains on the side of the bump, caused by the expanding womb. Pain that is severe or does not ease needs to be assessed.
- Heartburn, indigestion, constipation and piles.
- Leg cramps, feeling hot and dizziness.
- Swollen hands and feet. Swelling that comes on suddenly, especially in the face, needs immediate assessment.
- Leaking urine when you cough, laugh or sneeze, which pelvic floor exercises help to prevent.
- Urine infections, which are not an expected part of pregnancy and need treatment. Pain or burning when you pee and a fever are reasons to seek care.

If back pain is getting in the way of your day, bring it up at your appointment and do not take any medicine on your own: in pregnancy, only on the advice of your midwife or doctor.

Many women do not feel good about their body at the end of pregnancy, tired and in pain. If you feel low or anxious and it does not lift, talk to your midwife or doctor, who can point you to support.

How strong any of this is varies from one woman to the next.

## Prenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ by country, so here are England and the United States separately.

In England, the NHS schedule has an appointment at 34 weeks for everyone, and one at 31 weeks as well if this is your first baby. The RSV vaccine, which protects babies against bronchiolitis, is offered from 28 weeks in every pregnancy and can be given later, right up until labour. The whooping cough vaccine is usually given around 20 weeks and works best before 32 weeks, but it can still be given after that. If you have missed either, ask your midwife or GP. Group B strep is not routinely tested for in NHS pregnancy care.

In the United States, the whooping cough (Tdap) vaccine is given in every pregnancy between 27 and 36 weeks. The maternal RSV vaccine is a single dose between 32 weeks and 36 weeks and 6 days, during the RSV season, so you are now in that window: ask your provider whether it applies to you. Screening for group B strep is done for everyone between 36 weeks and 0 days and 37 weeks and 6 days.

The flu vaccine is recommended at any stage of pregnancy in both countries.

## What to do this week

1. Pack your hospital bag. Only 1 in 20 babies arrives on the due date, the NHS notes. Put in your birth plan and maternity notes, clothes and nappies for the baby, something loose and comfortable, nursing bras and breast pads, maternity pads and a wash bag.
2. Save in your phone, and write on paper too, the number and address of your maternity unit or hospital, your birth partner's number and the contact of whoever will take you there. The NHS warns that it is not safe to drive yourself when you are in labour.
3. Get the car seat and practise strapping it in and taking it out. The NHS suggests buying a new one and reminds parents that carrying a baby in your arms in the car is dangerous.
4. Talk to your employer. In the UK, the NHS notes that you can ask for a risk assessment of your workplace and attend antenatal appointments in paid time. Rules are different in the US, so check with your employer what applies to you.
5. Do your pelvic floor exercises. The NHS describes the movement as imagining you are having a pee and squeezing the muscles that would stop the flow.

> Get checked without waiting for your next appointment if you have vaginal bleeding, fluid leaking from your vagina, contractions that are painful or come at regular intervals, strong pressure low in your belly, a fever, or pain or burning when you pee. Call straight away if your baby is moving less than usual or the pattern of movements changes. A severe headache that does not go away, blurred vision or flashing lights, severe pain just below the ribs and sudden swelling of the face, hands or legs are signs of high blood pressure in pregnancy and need immediate assessment. Call your midwife or maternity unit in the UK, or your ob-gyn in the US. For a seizure, fainting, severe breathlessness, chest pain or heavy bleeding, call 999 in the UK or 911 in the US.
