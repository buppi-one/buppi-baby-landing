**Quick answer:** at 7 weeks you are in the 2nd month and the 1st trimester. The embryo is **about 10 mm** long from head to bottom, roughly the size of a blueberry, and the brain is growing faster than the rest of the body. For many women this is when nausea gets worse, and it is the week to arrange your first antenatal appointment if you have not done so yet.

"Seven weeks" means seven completed weeks, from 7 weeks and 0 days to 7 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. Conception usually happens around the second week of that count, so the embryo has been developing for about five weeks. Converting to months is approximate, which is why midwives and doctors talk in weeks.

## How your baby is developing at 7 weeks

At this stage the baby is still called an embryo. The week-by-week guide from the NHS, the UK's public health service, puts the length at about 10 mm from head to bottom.

The head is the most striking part. The brain is growing faster than the rest of the body, which gives the embryo a large forehead, and the NHS estimates that around 100 new brain cells form every minute. The spinal cord is taking shape quickly too.

The face is still a sketch: there are small dimples where the nose and ears will be, and the eyelids are starting to grow over the eyes. The buds that will become arms and legs begin to form the cartilage that later turns into bone. The arm buds are getting longer, and their flattened ends will become the hands.

Since last week, a vaginal ultrasound scan can sometimes pick up the heartbeat.

## What is changing in your body

Very little shows on the outside. The NHS says that in a first pregnancy you may not start to look pregnant until around week 12, and for many women it takes longer. Inside, your womb is already about the size of a lemon and there is more blood circulating than before you were pregnant. Over the course of pregnancy that volume rises by up to 50%, which can make you thirstier.

The symptoms reported most often this week:

- Nausea, with or without vomiting, at any time of day. According to ACOG, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, it usually starts before 9 weeks and for most women is gone by week 14.
- Tiredness and mood swings.
- Sore, more sensitive breasts.
- A sharper sense of smell, a metallic taste in your mouth and sudden changes in what you feel like eating.
- White, milky discharge. A strong smell, itching or a yellowish or greenish colour is a reason to be seen.
- Mild cramps, a bit like period pains. Severe pain, or pain with bleeding, needs to be checked.

Your sex drive can change as well, in either direction. Sex is generally considered safe in pregnancy unless your midwife or doctor has advised against it. If you have pain, bleeding or fluid leaking, stop and get checked.

For nausea, the NHS suggests eating small, frequent meals, choosing plain or cold food when the smell of hot meals puts you off, sipping fluids little and often, and resting, because tiredness makes nausea worse. There is some evidence that food and drinks containing ginger help. If none of that works, talk to your midwife or doctor: there are medicines they can prescribe, and that decision is theirs. Some women never feel sick at all, and having no nausea does not mean something is wrong.

## Antenatal care at this stage

Schedules depend on where you live, so here is how the two main English-speaking systems work.

In England, the NHS asks you to contact a midwife or GP as soon as you know you are pregnant. You can often refer yourself to your local maternity service online. The first long visit, called the booking appointment, usually takes place between 8 and 12 weeks. It lasts around an hour and includes the offer of blood tests and screening for infectious diseases. The NHS plans at least 10 appointments if this is your first baby and at least 7 if you have given birth before, plus 2 routine scans. The first of those, the dating scan, is offered at around 10 to 14 weeks.

In the United States, each practice sets its own date for the first prenatal visit, so call your ob-gyn's or midwife's office as soon as you have a positive test and ask when they want to see you.

In England, a scan at 7 weeks is not part of routine care, and in the US it depends on the practice. If you do have one this early, for example because of bleeding or pain, it is often done through the vagina and looks to confirm that the pregnancy is inside the womb, count the embryos and estimate how far along you are. If the embryo or the heartbeat cannot be seen clearly yet, the pregnancy may be less advanced than the dates suggested, and the scan is usually repeated. Sometimes the repeat scan shows that the pregnancy has stopped developing. The result is for your midwife or doctor to interpret.

Early pregnancy is also a good time to check your vaccinations. The NHS recommends the flu vaccine at any stage of pregnancy. The whooping cough vaccine comes later: in England it is usually given around 20 weeks and can be given from 16 weeks, and in the US the CDC recommends it between 27 and 36 weeks.

## What to do this week

1. Arrange your first antenatal appointment if you have not yet, and bring a list of the medicines and supplements you take. Do not start or stop any of them on your own.
2. Ask about pregnancy supplements. Folic acid is a nutrient involved in forming the baby's nervous system, and the NHS advises taking it until at least week 12, so it is still worth asking now if you have not started. Your midwife or doctor will tell you what to take and for how long.
3. Protect yourself from toxoplasmosis, which can be serious when caught in pregnancy. Cook meat thoroughly, wash fruit and vegetables well, wash your hands and utensils after handling raw meat, and wear gloves for gardening. If you have a cat, ask someone else to empty the litter tray, or wear gloves and wash your hands afterwards. Stroking a cat does not pass on the infection.
4. Avoid unpasteurised milk and dairy products, which can carry listeria bacteria. Check the label on cheese to see whether the milk was pasteurised.
5. Stop smoking, do not drink alcohol and cut down on coffee and other drinks with caffeine.

> Get checked without waiting for your appointment if you have vaginal bleeding, severe or persistent tummy pain (especially on one side), pain in the tip of your shoulder, dizziness or fainting, a fever, pain or burning when you pee, vomiting that stops you keeping fluids down, very dark urine or hardly any urine, or weakness and dizziness when you stand up. Pain on one side of your tummy, shoulder-tip pain and fainting can be signs of a pregnancy outside the womb (ectopic pregnancy), which needs immediate care. Bleeding in early pregnancy is not always serious, but it does need to be assessed. Call your midwife, GP or early pregnancy unit (UK) or your ob-gyn (US). In an emergency, call 999 or 911.
