**Quick answer:** at 4 weeks you are at the end of the 1st month and in the 1st trimester. The embryo is **about 2 mm long**, roughly the size of a poppy seed, and has just implanted in the wall of the womb. This is the week your period is late and a pregnancy test usually turns positive.

"Four weeks" means four completed weeks, from 4 weeks and 0 days to 4 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. You were not yet pregnant for the first two weeks of that count, so with a regular cycle conception happened about two weeks ago. If your cycle is irregular or you don't know the date of your last period, the first scan helps your care team work out how far along you are. There are about 36 weeks to go until your due date. Converting weeks to months is approximate, which is why midwives and doctors always talk in weeks.

## Your baby at 4 weeks

After fertilisation, the egg travelled down the fallopian tube, dividing as it went, and became a small ball of cells called a blastocyst. Between the end of week 3 and this week it buries itself in the wall of the womb, which is thick and full of blood vessels, and starts to receive nourishment from your blood. This process is called implantation.

The inner group of cells will form the embryo. The outer cells become the structures that protect and feed it, including the placenta, which later carries oxygen and nutrients to the baby. According to the NHS week-by-week guide, the embryo, about 2 mm long, already sits inside the amniotic sac, filled with cushioning fluid, and is attached to a yolk sac that nourishes it at this early stage.

Next week is the start of what is called the embryonic period, when the brain, spinal cord and heart begin to form. That is one reason antenatal care pays attention to folic acid so early: this nutrient helps form the nervous system and offers some protection against conditions such as spina bifida. Because this development starts so early, ask about supplements at your very first contact with a midwife, GP or doctor, without waiting for an appointment that may be several weeks away. Your care team decides what you should take and for how long.

## What changes in your body

Nothing shows on the outside. Inside, the hormone hCG, the one pregnancy tests detect, starts to be produced about six days after fertilisation and rises quickly in the first weeks. Together with the other pregnancy hormones, it is behind the symptoms of early pregnancy.

Many women feel nothing beyond the missed period, and that says nothing about how healthy the pregnancy is. When symptoms do appear, the most common are:

- Sore, tender or swollen breasts.
- Tiredness and feeling sleepy at odd times.
- Needing to pee more often.
- A metallic taste in your mouth, a sharper sense of smell and new food likes and dislikes.
- Mild cramps, a bit like period pains, and bloating.
- Very light spotting, which can happen at implantation. Even if it is slight, tell a midwife or doctor.
- A milky white discharge without a strong smell.

Nausea usually starts a little later, generally before 9 weeks. Not feeling sick yet is expected at this stage.

About the test: home tests are most reliable from the first day of a missed period, and a positive result is almost certainly correct. A negative one is less trustworthy, especially when the test is taken too early. If your period has not come and the result was negative, test again after a few days. If the second test is also negative and your period is still late, speak to your GP or doctor.

## Antenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ by country, so here is what applies in England and what is common in the United States.

In England, the next step after a positive test is to tell the NHS: contact your GP surgery or refer yourself to local maternity services. The first midwife appointment, called the booking appointment, usually happens between 8 and 12 weeks. You will be offered screening tests, including blood tests for infectious diseases. The NHS plans at least 10 appointments for a first baby, at least 7 if you have given birth before, and 2 routine scans.

In the United States, call your ob-gyn's or midwife's office once the test is positive to book your first prenatal visit. Practices set their own timing for that visit, so ask when they want to see you and what to do in the meantime.

A scan this early shows very little. In England the first routine scan is the dating scan, offered at around 10 to 14 weeks. A heartbeat can usually be seen on a vaginal ultrasound from around 6 to 7 weeks. An earlier scan is usually arranged when there is pain or bleeding, and at this stage it often has to be repeated some days later because there may be nothing to see yet.

## What to do this week

1. Arrange your first appointment and write down the first day of your last period, which is the basis of the whole count.
2. If you take any regular medicine, don't stop it on your own. Tell the doctor who prescribed it that you are pregnant so it can be reviewed. Don't start any new medicine either, including for pain or nausea, without checking with your midwife or doctor. Mention the pregnancy before any test or procedure, including at the dentist.
3. Stop drinking alcohol and smoking, and go easy on coffee, tea and other drinks with caffeine. If quitting smoking is hard, ask your midwife or doctor for support.
4. Watch what goes on your plate. Meat should be cooked through because of the risk of toxoplasmosis, and unpasteurised milk and dairy are off the menu. Your care team can go through the other food precautions. There is no need to eat for two: what counts is variety, with fruit and vegetables every day.
5. Keep moving if you feel well. The general recommendation in pregnancy is about 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, and 10-minute walks are a good start if you have not been active. If you have bleeding or pain, stop and get checked.

Whether to share the news now or wait for the first scan is your decision. Sex does not need to stop in an uncomplicated pregnancy unless your midwife or doctor has told you otherwise. If you notice bleeding, pain or fluid leaking, stop and get checked.

> Get medical help without waiting for your first appointment if you have vaginal bleeding, severe or persistent tummy pain, a fever, or pain or burning when you pee. Pain low down on one side of your tummy, pain in the tip of your shoulder, dizziness, fainting or looking very pale, with or without bleeding, can be signs of an ectopic pregnancy, when the embryo implants outside the womb. Symptoms tend to appear between the 4th and 12th week and need urgent assessment. Contact your GP, NHS 111 or an early pregnancy unit (UK) or your ob-gyn (US), and call 999 or 911 in an emergency.
