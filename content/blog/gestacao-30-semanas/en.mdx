**Quick answer:** at 30 weeks you are in the 7th month and the 3rd trimester, three-quarters of the way through. Your baby is **about 39.9 cm** long from head to heel and weighs **about 1.5 kg**, roughly the size of a cabbage. Their eyes can now focus. For you, sleep often gets worse and small efforts can leave you short of breath.

## How your baby is developing at 30 weeks

The 39.9 cm figure comes from the NHS week-by-week guide and runs from head to heel. The 1.5 kg weight is the median of the World Health Organization fetal growth charts. On those charts, 2 in 10 babies weigh less than 1.3 kg or more than 1.8 kg this week. A weight outside the range is one piece of information that your care team looks at alongside earlier scans, and it is not a diagnosis by itself.

What is new this week, according to the NHS, is that your baby's eyes can now focus. Vision keeps developing inside and outside the womb, and the same guide says babies only follow a moving object with their eyes at around 3 months old.

Many women want to know at this stage whether the baby has turned yet. They do not need to have turned yet. The NHS explains that babies twist and turn during pregnancy and that most are head down by the time labour begins. A baby who is bottom first (breech) on a scan done now does not decide how the birth will go. Position is usually checked again at around 36 weeks, and only then does your team discuss the options if the baby is still breech.

With less room, the kind of movement you feel may change, but movements should not become less frequent. According to the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (RCOG), they increase until around 32 weeks and then stay about the same until the birth. Your baby has periods of sleep, and what counts is the pattern you already know. If your baby is moving less than usual, call your maternity unit or ob-gyn the same day.

Under the WHO classification, a baby born between 28 and just under 32 weeks is very preterm, which is why regular contractions or leaking fluid at this stage mean calling your maternity unit or ob-gyn straight away.

## What is changing in your body

Sleep is the main topic of the NHS guide this week. You may have trouble falling asleep, and when you do, you may have disturbing dreams. The NHS puts these dreams down to hormones and to anxiety about the big changes ahead, and says that talking about them helps.

To sleep better, the NHS suggests relaxing before bedtime, avoiding tea, coffee and cola in the evening, and not napping in the late afternoon. After 28 weeks the safest position to fall asleep in is on your side, left or right, and a pillow between your knees makes it more comfortable. If you wake up on your back, turn onto your side and go back to sleep.

Your breathing changes too. Walking up the stairs without getting out of breath takes more effort now, and the NHS explains that your baby is pushing against your lungs. Severe breathlessness, or breathlessness with chest pain, is a warning sign and needs immediate care.

Other symptoms the NHS lists for this week:

- Backache, leg cramps and pains at the side of your bump.
- Heartburn, indigestion, constipation and piles (haemorrhoids).
- Swollen hands and feet, feeling hot and dizziness. Swelling that comes on suddenly is a warning sign.
- Headaches. A severe headache that does not go away is also a warning sign.
- Urine infections and vaginal infections, which need to be checked and treated by your care team. Pain or burning when you pee should be seen promptly.
- Bleeding gums, stretch marks and darker patches of skin on the face.

Not every pregnant woman gets all of these, and how strong they are changes from one pregnancy to the next.

Your bump may tighten now and then. Before 37 weeks, contractions that keep coming at regular intervals, period-type pains or an unusual backache need to be assessed by your maternity unit or ob-gyn.

When sleeplessness comes with feeling hopeless and losing interest in the things you used to enjoy, it can be a sign of depression, which also happens during pregnancy. Tell your midwife or doctor, because there is treatment and your care team will agree a plan with you.

There is no weekly weight gain that suits everyone. Your care team follows yours against your weight before pregnancy.

## Antenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ by country, so here is what applies in England and what applies in the US.

In England, the NHS appointments around now are at 28 weeks, at 31 weeks if this is your first baby, then at 34 and 36. The whooping cough vaccine can be given from 16 weeks, is usually offered around week 20 and is ideally given before 32 weeks. If you have not had it, speak to your midwife or a GP this week. The RSV vaccine is offered from 28 weeks in every pregnancy and can still be given later, up to labour. The NHS schedules two routine scans, at around 12 and 20 weeks, so a third-trimester scan depends on what your team needs to check. Group B strep is not routinely tested for in NHS pregnancy care.

In the US, the CDC and ACOG recommend the whooping cough (Tdap) vaccine in every pregnancy between 27 and 36 weeks, as early in that window as possible. The maternal RSV vaccine is a single dose between 32 and 36 weeks that is not repeated in later pregnancies. It is seasonal, so ask your provider whether it applies to your dates. Group B strep screening is done for all pregnant women between 36 and 37 weeks. Glucose screening is done at 24 to 28 weeks, so tell your ob-gyn's office if you missed it.

The flu vaccine is recommended during flu season, at any stage of pregnancy, in both countries.

Pre-eclampsia, a condition with high blood pressure that appears after 20 weeks, is more common in the third trimester. This is why your blood pressure matters at every check, and why the warning signs further down need immediate assessment.

## What to do this week

1. Check your vaccination record for whooping cough, RSV and flu, and book whichever one is due in your country.
2. Ask about antenatal classes. The NHS suggests asking your midwife or doctor, who may be able to recommend one, and inviting your partner to take part.
3. If you are in the UK, talk to your employer. The NHS notes that you can ask for a risk assessment of your workplace and attend antenatal appointments during paid work time.
4. Read about the stages of labour and write down your questions and preferences to go through with your care team.
5. Set up an evening routine with less caffeine, some time to relax and pillows for falling asleep on your side.

> Get checked without waiting for your next appointment if you have vaginal bleeding, fluid leaking from your vagina, contractions at regular intervals, severe tummy pain, a fever, or pain or burning when you pee. Call urgently too if your baby is moving less than usual or the pattern of movements changes. Do not wait until the next day. A severe headache that does not ease, blurred vision or flashing lights, severe pain just below the ribs and sudden swelling of the face, hands or legs can point to high blood pressure in pregnancy and need immediate assessment. Call your midwife or maternity unit in the UK, or your ob-gyn in the US. For a seizure, fainting, severe breathlessness, chest pain or heavy bleeding, call 999 in the UK or 911 in the US.
