Most of the time, it is normal. In the first months, crying is at its highest and holding is one of the things that calms it best. Later, around 9 months, your baby understands better that you can leave, and protests. It is worth looking into when the demand to be held comes with fever, pain, refusing to feed, crying that nothing calms, or a sudden change.

Knowing it's normal doesn't make the day easier. Spending hours without your hands is tiring, and you can ease that load without leaving your baby out.

## What to expect at each age

| Age | What usually happens | What the data say |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 3 months | Cries more, and holding calms | In a review of 28 diary studies, averages were 117 to 133 minutes of crying and fussing a day up to 6 weeks, and 68 minutes at 10 to 12 weeks |
| 4 to 6 months | Crying is usually less, and they notice more who is around | The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) reports that some babies already show separation anxiety at 4 or 5 months |
| 7 to 12 months | Wary of outsiders and protests when you leave | Stranger anxiety is expected in this phase, and separation anxiety gets stronger around 9 months |
| 12 to 18 months | Wants to explore, but returns to your arms when scared or tired | The peak is usually between 10 and 18 months, fading in the second half of the second year |

Every baby has their own pace, and temperament counts. These ages are a reference, not a deadline.

The same review shows that, on average, fussing drops a lot after 8 to 9 weeks. That doesn't tell you when your own baby will improve, but it helps to know the beginning tends to be the hardest part. The AAP describes colic crying as usually gone by 3 months, so talk to your pediatrician if it stays intense after that.

## Does holding spoil a baby?

This is the question that weighs most, and the short answer is that we found no evidence for it. The most direct study is a 1986 clinical trial with 99 healthy mother-infant pairs. The group told to hold their babies more throughout the day, on top of feeds and moments of crying, cried and fussed 43% less at 6 weeks: 1.23 hours a day versus 2.16 hours. More holding did not change sleep time.

There is an important limit to this. A second trial, with 66 babies already taken to the pediatrician for colic, found no difference in crying even with more than 2 extra hours of holding a day. So if you are already carrying your baby all day and they keep crying a lot, it isn't your fault, and carrying more may not solve it. It is worth reading about [how to soothe colic](/en/blog/how-to-soothe-baby-colic/) and talking to your pediatrician.

There is also a physiological reason holding works. In a Japanese study of 21 babies, walking with a baby in your arms reduced crying and heart rate, while sitting with them did not have the same effect. It is a small study, but it helps explain why your baby calms when you stand up and walk.

What the AAP notes, from 4 months, is something else: babies always rocked to sleep may have more trouble getting back to sleep at night. If that is weighing on you, see [how to transfer to the crib](/en/blog/baby-only-sleeps-when-held/).

## How to free your hands

No study compares these options. They are practical tips to try:

- **Sling or carrier.** Holds your baby with your hands free. Their face must stay visible, with the chin off the chest. If they fall asleep there, the AAP advises moving them to a firm, flat surface as soon as possible.
- **Floor time with you nearby.** With your baby awake, start with a few minutes on a mat on the floor, talking to them. Increase gradually. Rolling, sitting and crawling also make the floor more interesting over time.
- **Taking turns.** Another trusted person can also soothe, and each adult gets a break. It may take a while for your baby to accept this.
- **Check the basics first.** Hunger, tiredness and a dirty diaper also bring on the demand to be held. [Wake windows](/en/blog/wake-windows-by-age/) help you notice when crying is tiredness.

Swings and cushions are not places for your baby to sleep. An infant car seat is for the car: when you arrive, move them to a firm, flat surface as soon as possible.

## When they protest because you left

Around 9 months, many babies get more distressed when you leave. The AAP's advice works better for older babies who already notice the pattern, and every child reacts differently:

- **Keep the goodbye short and always the same,** and leave. Many children stop crying within a few minutes.
- **Pick the time.** A tired, hungry or sick baby suffers more, so when you can, leave after a nap and a meal.
- **Come back when you said you would,** so they learn to trust the routine.

The AAP suggests talking to the pediatrician if your child gets very upset by any stranger or separation, or seems sad or anxious.

## When to look into it

> **Talk to your pediatrician if your baby:**
>
> - only calms when upright and also arches their body, seems to be in pain or vomits often (it may be [reflux](/en/blog/baby-reflux-spit-up-when-its-gerd/), among other causes)
> - cries far more than usual, or keeps crying intensely after 3 months
> - cries nonstop for more than 2 hours and can't be calmed: the AAP advises calling the same day
> - suddenly changes behavior, or is feeding less and not gaining weight
> - doesn't make eye contact, doesn't react to people, or has lost something they already did. See [milestones month by month](/en/blog/milestones-month-by-month-0-12/)
>
> **Seek care immediately** if your baby is very limp, hard to wake, having trouble breathing, has bluish skin, or has a fever and is under 3 months old. Under 3 months, the AAP also advises seeking care now if they go more than 8 hours without feeding or feeding very little.

Don't give tea or medicine to calm your baby. Any treatment only with guidance from your pediatrician.

Take care of whoever is doing the carrying too. If exhaustion or sadness is weighing on you, tell your doctor. And if you reach your limit with the crying, put your baby on their back in the crib, leave the room for a few minutes and ask for help. Never shake a baby.

Logging for a few days when your baby cries, sleeps and feeds helps you see patterns and gives you concrete information for the appointment.
