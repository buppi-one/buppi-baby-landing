At 10 months, sleep usually keeps the shape of the previous months — one long night stretch and **2 naps** — but two themes dominate parents' conversations at this stage: the baby who **stands up in the crib** (and won't lie down) and **waking very early in the morning**. This guide covers how much a 10-month-old usually sleeps, wake windows, a flexible schedule, how to handle the 5 a.m. wake-up and the little crib gymnast, and what changes in safe sleep.

## How much a 10-month-old sleeps

The recommendation for this age is **12 to 15 hours a day**, counting night and naps — and observational studies show many babies closer to **13 hours**. Night holds most of it — usually **10 to 12 hours** — and the day splits into **2 naps**.

As always, the range of normal is wide. The best thermometer isn't hitting an exact number, but a baby who **eats well, gains weight, and is active and curious** when awake. (For the full picture across ages, see the [how much sleep by age table](/en/blog/how-long-should-baby-sleep-by-age/).)

## Wake windows at 10 months

The **[wake window](/en/blog/wake-windows-by-age/)** — how long your baby can comfortably stay awake between sleeps — usually sits between **3h15 and 4h** at 10 months, slightly longer than the month before. Worth remembering: wake windows are **practical reference ranges**, not a clinical parameter — the range is the starting point, your baby is the fine-tuning. The **last window of the day**, before night sleep, remains the longest.

Windows that run too short produce shallow naps and a choppy night; too long leaves the baby **overtired** — wired and, paradoxically, harder to settle (and more likely to wake too early the next day). **Sleep cues** (yawning, eye-rubbing, losing interest, fussiness) still beat the clock.

## Naps: still 2 — and the "false transition" still lurks

At 10 months, most babies still need **2 naps a day**, around **1h to 1h30 each**. The real transition to **1 nap** usually comes between **12 and 18 months** — and when it arrives, it gives consistent signs for weeks, not three bad days.

If your baby has entered a **nap strike** (resisting, rolling around the crib, 20-minute naps), the 8–10-month playbook from the [9-month guide](/en/blog/baby-sleep-9-months/) still applies: it's almost always a **developmental phase**, not spare sleep. Dropping a nap too early tends to **worsen the night and pull the morning wake-up even earlier** — the opposite of the goal.

What helps: nudge the timings slightly (a slightly longer window before the resisted nap), plenty of **motor practice** outside the crib, a short predictable ritual, and patience — strikes usually last one to two weeks.

## A flexible one-day schedule

There's no fixed agenda at 10 months. This example uses **2 naps and ~3h15–4h windows**. Treat it as an elastic reference:

| Time (example) | What happens |
|---|---|
| 6:45–7:00 | Wake up and milk |
| 8:00 | Fruit or breakfast (solids) |
| 10:15–11:30 | 1st nap |
| 11:45 | Milk, awake time |
| 12:45 | Lunch (solids) |
| 15:00–16:00 | 2nd nap |
| 16:15 | Milk, snack |
| 17:45 | Dinner (solids) |
| 19:00 | Milk, calm routine |
| 19:30–20:00 (varies) | Night sleep begins |
| Overnight | 0–1 waking |

At 10 months, **solids** typically organize into main meals and fruit snacks, following your country's pediatric guidance and each baby's rhythm — some families already include breakfast. **Milk (breast or formula) remains a central part of nutrition** until close to the first birthday. And one reminder that joins the night routine: with teeth in the mouth, **brush them after the last milk** before bed (your pediatrician or dentist can guide technique and toothpaste).

## The villain of this phase: waking too early

Waking at **just past 5 a.m.**, ready for the day, is one of the most common complaints around 10 months. Before accepting the new "official time," hunt the usual causes — usually stacked:

- **Too much tiredness, not too little.** A baby who goes into the night overtired (blown windows, a short or dropped afternoon nap) tends to have a more fragile end of night — and wakes earlier, not later.
- **A room that brightens.** Near dawn, sleep is already light; any light creeping in pulls the wake-up forward. Truly darkening the room (blackout) is one of the highest-return fixes.
- **Bedtime off its sweet spot.** Both too early (the math lands at 5 a.m.) and too late (overtiredness fragments the night) can end in the same place. Adjust in 15-minute steps, not hour jumps.
- **"Day mode" switching on early.** If at 5 a.m. the house lights up and the day begins, the baby's clock locks that time in. Responding to, comforting and feeding a hungry baby is never a mistake — the adjustment is only in the atmosphere: before the target time, respond like it's the middle of the night (low light, little talk), and switch on "day mode" only at the time you want.

No fix flips this in a day — pick one or two, hold them for several days, and watch. And remember: some babies are **physiological early birds**; if your baby wakes at 6 happy and rested, that's a schedule, not a problem.

## Standing in the crib (and refusing to lie down)

The 9-to-11-month classic: you lay your baby down, and they **pull up to stand at the rail** — sometimes proud, sometimes crying because they can't get back down yet. It's motor practice, not defiance.

- **During the day**, rehearse the way back a lot: pulling up and **sitting back down** with support, outside the crib. Once the move becomes automatic, the night show loses its appeal.
- **At night**, lay them back down calmly, few words, low light — once, the same way, without turning it into a game (or a tug-of-war). If they already know how to lie down on their own, you can give it a few minutes before stepping in.
- **In the crib**: mattress **on the lowest setting** from the moment your baby stands, and nothing inside that could work as a step.

The phase passes on its own once the skill stops being new — how long varies by baby, but it's rarely a matter of months.

## Nights at 10 months

At 10 months, many babies get through the night in one **long stretch** without feeding — while others still need night feeds, depending on each baby's growth and history (preemies and babies with slow weight gain in particular). And **wakings are still common**: in observational studies of this age range, the average runs from under 1 up to about 2–3 signaled wakings per night, with breastfed babies feeding more often. Wakings at this stage often ride along with **development** (standing practice, separation); an erupting tooth can disrupt things briefly (the discomfort concentrates in the days of eruption, not weeks), and unusual days count too.

**Separation anxiety** is still going strong — the peak usually falls between 9 and 18 months, and for many babies it even intensifies near the first birthday. The helpful package stays the same: a predictable routine, short calm goodbyes, peekaboo during the day. (A transitional object — a lovey — is a tool for after 12 months; before that, only during the ritual, out of the crib at sleep time.)

On **night feeds**: many 10-month-olds with good weight gain drop the overnight feed naturally. If you want to reduce it actively, align the plan with your pediatrician — especially for preemies or babies gaining weight below expectations.

## Safe sleep at 10 months

> Keep the **crib mattress on its lowest setting** and the crib **free of pillows, cushions, bumpers, loose blankets and large toys** (anything that could serve as a step for climbing). Keep **placing your baby down on their back** — if they roll both ways on their own and choose another position, you don't need to keep flipping them back. For warmth, a **sleep sack** instead of a blanket. No positioners or "nests." And with a baby cruising along furniture, check the area around the crib: furniture, cords and curtains out of reach.

## What helps sleep at 10 months

- **A light, predictable routine** before bed (bath, milk, dim light, a song), in the same order — predictability soothes separation
- **Protecting the 2 naps** and respecting the windows, so the baby doesn't reach the night overtired
- **Plenty of motor practice awake** during the day (free floor time, pulling up, cruising, sitting back down) — daytime practice declutters the night
- **A truly dark room** at the end of the night — the cheapest fix against the 5 a.m. wake-up
- **Putting your baby down drowsy but awake**, when it works — for many families it helps the baby re-settle on their own during wakings (it's not a requirement: it's a tool)

About **sleep training**: from 4–6 months some more structured methods can be considered, and the decision belongs to the family — worth discussing with your pediatrician. None of it is mandatory.

## When to talk to the pediatrician

Sleep at 10 months still varies, but talk to your pediatrician if:

- Your baby is **very sleepy**, hard to wake, or **not gaining weight** as expected
- Your baby **snores loudly**, pauses breathing, or seems to work hard to breathe while asleep — pauses and labored breathing deserve prompt evaluation, don't wait for the routine visit
- Sleep trouble comes with **fever, extreme irritability** or other signs something is off (ear infection is a classic cause of bad nights at this age)
- Your baby **doesn't sit without support, doesn't babble** or interact as expected (worth a development check)
- You are **exhausted beyond coping** — asking for help is also caring for your baby

## What to expect ahead

Ten months is the home stretch of the first year's "2 naps + long night" format: a body almost walking, separation still very present, and mornings that test your patience. Holding the windows, the dark room and the predictable routine, nights tend to firm up — and the coming months bring first steps and, further ahead (between 12 and 18 months), the real transition to 1 nap. The spirit remains that of the [9-month guide](/en/blog/baby-sleep-9-months/): follow your baby's pace, with consistency and safe sleep.
