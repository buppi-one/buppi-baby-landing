**Quick answer:** at 22 weeks you are at the end of the 5th month, in the 2nd trimester. Your baby is **about 27.8 cm** from head to heel and weighs **about 480 g**, roughly the size of a papaya. They are already swallowing amniotic fluid and practising breathing. If you have not had your mid-pregnancy anatomy scan yet, contact your midwife or doctor about it this week.

"Twenty-two weeks" means twenty-two completed weeks, from 22 weeks and 0 days to 22 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. The due date is set at 40 weeks, so there are about 18 to go. Converting weeks to months is approximate, which is why your care team always talks in weeks.

## How your baby is developing at 22 weeks

The length of about 27.8 cm comes from the NHS week-by-week guide and is measured from head to heel. The weight of about 480 g is the median on the World Health Organization fetal growth charts. For your own baby, go by the measurements in your scan report, as interpreted by your care team.

The lungs are developing and your baby makes practice breathing movements in the womb. They also swallow small amounts of amniotic fluid. Some of what is swallowed stays in the bowel and becomes part of meconium, the dark, sticky first poo that usually comes out after birth.

Taste buds are forming, and the NHS notes that what you eat could influence your baby's sense of taste. Eating a variety of fruit and vegetables is worthwhile, with no guarantee about what they will accept later.

Between 21 and 24 weeks, according to the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG), kicks get stronger and the sucking reflex develops. Hearing started to form a few weeks ago, and the NHS suggests this is a good time to talk or sing to your bump. There is no minimum amount of talking per day, and if you only remember at night, your baby is not missing out.

First movements are usually felt between 16 and 24 weeks, and at this stage they have no set time or pattern. If you reach 24 weeks without having felt anything, tell your midwife or doctor.

## What is changing in your body

Stretch marks often show up around now, usually on the tummy, thighs and breasts. They are pink, purple or brown lines, depending on your skin tone, and sometimes the first sign is mild itching in the area. Severe itching, especially on the palms of your hands and the soles of your feet, is a different matter and needs checking. The NHS estimates that around 8 in 10 pregnant women get stretch marks. They are not harmful, they tend to fade after the birth without going away completely, and there is limited evidence that creams or oils prevent them.

Other common symptoms at this stage:

- A bump that goes hard for a few moments and then softens again. These are practice contractions (Braxton Hicks), which are usually painless, short, infrequent and without a rhythm. Many women only notice them towards the end of pregnancy.
- Heartburn and indigestion. It often helps to eat smaller meals more often, not to eat in the three hours before bed, to cut down on fatty or very spicy food and to prop up your head and shoulders in bed.
- Constipation and piles (haemorrhoids), which cause itching, pain when you poo or a little bright red blood on the paper. Fibre, water and movement help prevent them. If you are not sure the blood is coming from your bottom, treat it as vaginal bleeding and get checked the same day.
- Twinges at the sides of the bump, as the ligaments that support the uterus stretch. Pain that is severe or does not ease needs checking.
- Leg cramps, backache, broken sleep and mood swings. Low mood or anxiety that does not lift, or that gets in the way of daily life, is worth telling your care team about.

Appetite often goes up, but the NHS points out that there is no need to eat for two and that, in general, extra calories only come into the picture in the third trimester. Your midwife or doctor follows your weight over time and gives advice that fits you.

## Antenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ by country, so here is what applies in England and what applies in the US. Twin and higher-risk pregnancies follow their own schedule.

In England, the NHS plans at least 10 appointments for a first baby and at least 7 if you have given birth before. After the 16-week appointment, the next routine ones are at 25 weeks (first baby) and 28 weeks. The 20-week screening scan is usually done between 18 and 21 weeks and can be done up to 23 weeks in some cases, so if you have not had it, contact your midwife now. In the US, your ob-gyn or midwife sets the date of the anatomy scan, and international ultrasound guidance (ISUOG) places it between 18 and 24 weeks.

The scan looks closely at your baby's body (the NHS lists the bones, heart, brain, spinal cord, face, kidneys and tummy) and checks the placenta. When the placenta is low at this stage, in most cases it moves away from the cervix as the uterus grows. That is why a repeat scan later on is common, and what you need to do until then depends on the finding and is decided by your doctor or midwife.

The whooping cough vaccine is where the two countries differ most. The NHS usually offers it around week 20. It can be given from week 16 and works best before week 32, so ask for it if you have not had it. In the US, the CDC and ACOG recommend it between 27 and 36 weeks, as early in that window as possible. Both repeat it in every pregnancy.

The next test on the calendar is the glucose test at 24 to 28 weeks, which looks for gestational diabetes. In the US it is routine for all pregnant women. In England it is only for women with a higher chance of the condition.

## What to do this week

1. If you have not had your anatomy scan, contact your midwife or doctor today. In England, 23 weeks is the latest it can be done.
2. Check whether you have had the whooping cough vaccine for this pregnancy (England), or ask at which visit it is planned (US).
3. If you smoke, ask for help to quit. Your baby's lungs are developing, and your midwife or doctor can refer you to stop-smoking support.
4. Keep moving, unless your care team has told you to hold back. The general recommendation is about 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, and 10-minute walks are a fine place to start.
5. Take a list of the medicines, supplements, creams and cosmetics you use to your next appointment, including those for acne, dark patches and wrinkles. Your care team will tell you what you can keep using, and no medicine should be started or stopped on your own.

> Get checked without waiting for your next appointment if you have vaginal bleeding, fluid leaking from the vagina, severe or persistent abdominal pain, cramps or contractions that come at regular intervals, a fever, pain or burning when you pee, a severe headache that does not go away, changes in your vision, severe pain just below the ribs or sudden swelling of your face, hands or legs. The last four can be signs of high blood pressure in pregnancy, which can appear from 20 weeks. If you already feel your baby move and notice the movements have dropped off sharply or stopped, that is also a reason to be assessed without delay. Call your maternity unit or midwife in the UK, or your ob-gyn in the US, and call 999 or 911 in an emergency.

If you work in the UK, the NHS notes that the latest you can tell your employer is 15 weeks before the baby is due, which is around week 25.
