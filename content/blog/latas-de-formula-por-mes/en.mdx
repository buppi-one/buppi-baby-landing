Formula is one of the most expensive items in the first-year budget — and one of the most practical questions there is: **how many cans go in the cart each month?** The short answer, at peak intake: **8 to 10 cans of 400 g per month** (or 4 to 5 of 800 g), roughly **one 400 g can every 3 days**. The full answer depends on your baby's age and real intake — and it's math you can do in 30 seconds.

One warning before the math, because it changes how you use this article: **the cans chart is for planning purchases, never for deciding how much your baby "should" drink**. Their hunger — together with the pediatrician — is in charge of the volume; the can just obeys.

## The math in 3 steps

1. **Find the daily intake** in ml (add up 24 h of bottles — the average of the last 3 to 5 days is the number that matters).
2. **Can yield**: at standard dilution (1 level scoop per 30 ml of water, scoops of 4.3 to 4.7 g depending on the brand), **400 g of powder makes 2.8 to 3.1 liters** of prepared formula — the powder adds volume: 30 ml of water + 1 scoop becomes ~33 ml of formula. The 800 g can makes double: 5.6 to 6.2 liters.
3. **Divide**: monthly intake (ml/day × 30) ÷ can yield = cans per month.

Real example: a 3-month-old drinking **900 ml a day** → 27 liters a month → 27,000 ÷ 2,800~3,100 = **9 to 10 cans of 400 g** (or 5 of 800 g).

One fine-tune the dry math doesn't show: **discarded leftovers are consumption too**. A started, unfinished bottle must be thrown out within 1 hour (a bacterial-safety rule) — in practice real usage runs about 5 to 15% above the math. When in doubt, round the cans up.

## Chart: cans per month, from birth to 12 months

Using the intake ranges from our [formula amounts by age guide](/en/blog/how-much-formula-by-age/) and the 2.8 to 3.1 L yield per 400 g can — always for an **exclusively formula-fed** baby:

| Age | Typical intake/day | 400 g cans/month | 800 g cans/month |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| First week (days 3–7, ramping up) | 200 to 500 ml | 2 to 5 | 1 to 3 |
| 2 weeks to 1 month | 500 to 700 ml | 5 to 7 | 3 to 4 |
| 1 to 2 months | 600 to 850 ml | 6 to 9 | 3 to 5 |
| 2 to 4 months | 700 to 950 ml | 7 to 10 | 4 to 5 |
| 4 to 6 months | 800 to 960 ml | 8 to 10 | 4 to 5 |
| 6 to 9 months | 600 to 900 ml + solids | 6 to 10, falling | 3 to 5 |
| 9 to 12 months | 500 to 750 ml + solids | 5 to 8, falling | 3 to 4 |

*(Daily volumes come from the [by-age guide](/en/blog/how-much-formula-by-age/); the ~960 ml/day (32 oz) ceiling the AAP flags is the safety reference — beyond it, the answer is a pediatrician conversation, not more formula. In the first 2 days of life volumes are tiny — the first-week row describes the days 3–7 ramp.)*

Five honest readings of this chart:

- **The peak is between 2 and 6 months.** That's when the budget feels it most — and when the 800 g can usually pays off.
- **After 6 months the curve descends**: solids take over part of the calories and formula volume falls month by month. Don't be surprised when cans start lasting longer.
- **Combination feeding has no chart**: usage falls in proportion to what comes from the breast, and only your bottle log says how much that is.
- **At 6 months the can usually changes**: follow-on formula (stage 2) comes with its own scoop and dilution — the math still works, just redo it with the new label's yield.
- **Preterm and low-birth-weight babies are outside this ruler**: they use formulas with different caloric density and scoops, set by the pediatrician.

## 400 g or 800 g: which pays off?

The bigger can almost always has a **lower price per gram** — but the right comparison on the shelf is **price per kilo** (most supermarket tags show it), not the sticker price. Two situations flip the logic:

- **Low intake** (a small top-up in combination feeding, or a baby near 1 year): an opened can has a use-by window (**3 to 4 weeks depending on the brand** — the label rules). If the 800 g won't be finished in that window, the saving becomes waste.
- **Trying a new brand, or a switch advised by the pediatrician**: start with the smaller can until adaptation is confirmed.

Store the opened can **tightly closed, in a cool, dry place** — never in the fridge (moisture clumps the powder) — and write the opening date on the lid.

## How to spend less with zero risk

What works:

- **Compare price per kilo** across brands of the same stage and across can sizes.
- **Know what the rules allow**: many countries restrict formula marketing under the WHO Code — in Brazil, the NBCAL law (11,265/2006) outright **bans promotions, discounts and loyalty programs** for stage 1 and 2 formulas, so a "deal" on them is a red flag, not a bargain. The legitimate saving lives in price per kilo, can size, and stores with lower everyday prices.
- **Rational stock**: 2 to 3 cans ahead of consumption is enough cushion — an oversized stock runs into expiry dates and possible formula switches advised by the pediatrician.
- **Check public programs**: in Brazil, the public system (SUS) provides **special formulas** (CMPA, prematurity and other clinically indicated conditions) via prescription and protocol — standard formula for a healthy baby generally isn't covered, though some cities run their own programs. Your local health unit is the place to ask.

And what to **never** do to save money:

> **Never dilute formula outside the label's ratio** to stretch the can, and **never replace formula with cow's milk before 12 months**. Watered-down formula undernourishes and can cause a dangerous sodium imbalance; over-concentrated formula overloads the kidneys. If the cost is unmanageable, bring it to the pediatrician as a problem to solve together — safe alternatives exist, and improvising on dilution is not one of them.

## The chart plans the purchase — the baby decides the feed

If the month ended with cans left over (or missing), the first reading isn't "my baby eats too little/too much" — it's that babies vary: growth spurts, longer naps, heat, the day's appetite. The indicators that say all is well are **weight gain on the curve**, **wet diapers** (6+ a day from the first week of life) and a baby **content between feeds**. The responsive-feeding research is clear: following your baby's hunger and fullness cues — not a volume target — is what protects against excessive weight gain.

To know your real intake (the number that feeds the calculator above), log the bottles for a few days — on paper or in a one-tap app. With 3 to 5 days of logging, the average shows up and the monthly purchase stops being a guess.
