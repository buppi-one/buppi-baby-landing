Straight answer: between 3 and 4 months, a baby drinks a total of **750 to 1,000 ml (25–34 oz) of formula in 24 hours**, across **5 to 7 feeds** — which works out to **120 to 180 ml (4–6 oz) per feed, depending on weight**. And here's the surprise: from 3 to 6 months, volume **almost stops climbing**. The baby grows, but so does efficiency — the volume curve hits a plateau.

This guide covers the 3–4-month pair — the weight-based tuning, the **distracted-feeding phase** (the signature of this age), the 3-month growth spurt, the effect of the 4-month sleep regression, and the question the whole family group chat asks: "can we start purées yet?" (spoiler: the general rule is no). For every age, the [formula amounts by age guide](/en/blog/how-much-formula-by-age/); for the earlier months, [1 month](/en/blog/how-much-formula-1-month-old/) and [2 months](/en/blog/how-much-formula-2-month-old/).

## The weight math — and the plateau nobody warns about

The practical reference is still **~150 ml per kilogram per day** (the AAP cites ~165 ml/kg as typical; guidelines range from ~120 to 180) — but at this age it starts to **overestimate**: the need per kilo falls as the baby grows, and the **~960 ml/day (32 oz) figure the AAP cites as a maximum average** arrives before the math does. Important: it's a guiding average, not an absolute limit — babies vary, and reference ranges (including our general guide's) reach ~1,050 ml. What it signals is: consistent intake above it deserves the pediatrician's reading, not another bottle.

| Baby's weight | Usual range (120–150 ml/kg/day) | In practice (5–7 feeds) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5.5 kg (12 lb) | 660–825 ml | 120–150 ml per feed |
| 6 kg (13 lb) | 720–900 ml | 130–165 ml per feed |
| 6.5 kg (14 lb) | 780–975 ml | 140–180 ml per feed |
| 7 kg (15+ lb) or more | 840–~1,050 ml — read it with the pediatrician | 150–180 ml, 5–6 feeds |

*(Starting estimates, never targets — the bottle obeys the baby's hunger, not the chart. The weight span shown is the usual variation at this age; percentiles are the pediatrician's reading.)*

Translating the plateau: most babies this age settle well below what the math suggests for the bigger ones. Big babies (high percentiles) **can** approach 1,000–1,050 ml with nothing wrong — but that reading belongs **with the pediatrician**, looking at weight-gain velocity, not to a parent stretching bottles alone. The AAP's maximum average (~960 ml) works as a check-in trigger, not a prohibition.

## The distraction phase: the world got too interesting

If there's one signature complaint of months 3–4, it's this: **the baby who used to finish every bottle now stops mid-feed, turns their head, smiles at the curtain and forgets the bottle exists**. The vast majority of the time it's not refusal or illness — it's development: vision and curiosity matured, and the world now competes with food.

What works:

- **A deliberately boring environment**: dim light, no TV, no foot traffic, same armchair.
- **Use the calm feeds**: first thing in the morning and the pre-sleep feed tend to flow better because there's less stimulation — but don't push volume into a half-asleep baby: fullness cues still rule, even then.
- **Paced feeding, always**: bottle held more horizontal, pauses, no insisting on the last drop — fullness cues count even when the feed was short.
- **Don't chase the baby with the bottle**: feed turned into a 40-minute battle? End it and offer again later. Forcing at this stage builds a bad association with the bottle.
- **Watch the day's average, not the feed**: what matters is the 24-hour total and growth — the format (more smaller feeds or fewer bigger ones) belongs to the baby. And if the drop comes with fewer diapers or listlessness, it's no longer distraction: see the pediatrician.

## The 3-month spurt and the 4-month regression: the two turbulences

**The "3-month" growth spurt** (the exact window varies — the name is approximate) shows up as a few days of visibly bigger hunger. The playbook is the usual: confirm it's hunger (not comfort sucking), add 15 to 30 ml at the feeds that empty, accept the higher frequency for a few days, don't switch formulas on your own.

**The 4-month sleep regression** touches feeds from the opposite direction: sleep fragments (it's a permanent neurological reorganization of sleep architecture, not a hunger phase), and some babies ask for night feeds again or compensate by day. Keep responsive feeding during the day (no rigid volume targets), flex without guilt for a few weeks, and avoid turning every waking into a feed — our [4-month regression guide](/en/blog/4-month-sleep-regression/) covers the sleep side in detail.

## "Can we start purées?" — the 4-month question

Short answer: **the general rule is to wait for 6 months**. The WHO and Brazil's Ministry of Health recommend milk (breast or formula) as the **exclusive food until 6 months**. Some international protocols mention a window from 4 months for specific situations, but that's an **individual pediatrician decision**, based on readiness signs (head control, sitting with support, active interest, loss of the tongue-thrust reflex) — never a calendar decision made at home.

And two "nevers" that hold at any age:

> **Never thicken the bottle on your own** with cereals or any food "to fill them up / help them sleep" — it doesn't improve sleep, adds unneeded calories and carries choking risk. (This is different from **AR formulas** — industrially thickened and prescribed by the pediatrician for reflux: those follow the prescriber's guidance.) And **never offer juice or tea** at this age. If the question about starting solids exists, it gets answered at the checkup — not at the supermarket.

## How to know the amount is right

First, responsive feeding still leads: offer at **hunger cues** (stirring, rooting, hands to mouth) and stop at **fullness cues** (releasing the nipple, turning away, slowing down, hands relaxing) — the cues get clearer at this age; use them.

The verification trio, worth more than any chart:

- **Diapers**: 6+ well-soaked a day.
- **Weight**: gain naturally slows this trimester (on average something like 15–20 g/day) — the curve is the pediatrician's to read.
- **Behavior**: active, curious (too curious — see above) and content between feeds.

Signs of excess are the same as ever: constant large spit-ups, discomfort, gain far above the curve — and the adjustment is a pediatrician conversation; it usually means respecting fullness cues more, using slow flow and reviewing volumes against the curve, investigating [reflux](/en/blog/baby-reflux-spit-up-when-its-gerd/) and CMPA if it persists.

> **Emergency (ER):** deep lethargy, dehydration signs (dry mouth, sunken fontanelle, barely any diapers), repeated projectile vomiting. **Visit without waiting for the routine checkup:** an abrupt, sustained intake drop with decreasing diapers, fewer than 6 diapers/day, weight gain falling off the curve.

And for **combination feeding**: the numbers above are for exclusive formula — with mixed feeding, breast first, top-up after, and diapers + the curve judge the whole.

## Preparation: the rules don't change

Same playbook as earlier months, no slack: **exact label dilution** (no stretching the can), **water boiled and mixed while still at ≥70 °C** with the prepared bottle cooled afterwards to feeding temperature — test on your wrist before offering (WHO guidance for non-sterile powder; some authorities relax it for older healthy babies — follow your label and pediatrician), prepared fresh, leftovers discarded within 1 hour **counted from the start of the feed**. And **no extra water or tea** — formula still covers hydration until 6 months.

## Log it — this phase's patterns fool the eye

Between distraction, spurt and regression, months 3–4 are when perception errs the most: "he's drinking way less" is often just redistribution (fewer feeds, bigger volumes). A few days of logging shows the real total — and turns the 4-month checkup (vaccines again) into a conversation with data instead of impressions.
