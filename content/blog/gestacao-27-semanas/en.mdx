**Quick answer:** at 27 weeks you are in the last week of the 2nd trimester and the end of the 6th month. Your baby is **about 36.6 cm** from head to heel and weighs **around 1 kg**. Where a glucose test is planned, it is usually done by 28 weeks, and next week your appointments and vaccines start to change.

"Twenty-seven weeks" means twenty-seven completed weeks, from 27 weeks and 0 days to 27 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. The due date is set at 40 weeks, so there are about 13 to go. Two thirds of your pregnancy are over.

## How your baby is developing at 27 weeks

The length comes from the NHS week-by-week guide and is measured from head to heel. The weight is the median of the WHO fetal growth charts. Both figures are averages. Not everyone has a scan this week, and when there is one, your care team reads the measurement on the report with you.

The skin was wrinkled all over a few weeks ago and is smoothing out as fat builds up underneath. The organs keep maturing. According to ACOG, between 25 and 28 weeks the eyelids open and the lungs start to make surfactant, the substance that keeps the air sacs open once a baby breathes air.

The NHS describes the lungs this week as capable of breathing. They are still a long way from ready, and they go on maturing until the end of pregnancy. The WHO classes a birth before 28 weeks as extremely preterm, and a baby born now usually needs intensive care and help with breathing. That is why the signs of early labour listed further down need a quick response.

Movements tend to be stronger and easier to notice by now. There is no right number of kicks a day. Each baby has a pattern, with busier and quieter times, and that pattern is what you keep an eye on.

## What changes in your body

Your bump has grown, and your stomach and bowel have less room. Add the effect of progesterone, which slows digestion, and you get the bloating, heartburn and constipation that are common at this stage. For constipation, drinking plenty of water and eating fruit, vegetables and wholegrain foods usually helps.

Other symptoms the NHS lists for 27 weeks:

- Tiredness and broken sleep. Many women start snoring, because the lining of the nose swells.
- Backache, and sharp pains at the sides of the bump caused by the ligaments that support the womb.
- Leg cramps, mostly at night.
- Feeling hot and sweating more. Light cotton clothes make the day more comfortable.
- Mild, gradual swelling of the feet and ankles, sometimes the hands, that gets worse towards the evening. Swelling that comes on suddenly, or in your face, is on the warning list.
- Stretch marks, bleeding gums and darker patches on your face.

Over the next few weeks it is common to get out of breath climbing stairs because, as the NHS explains, the baby starts pushing against your lungs. Breathlessness at rest, or that gets worse quickly or comes with chest pain, needs urgent care.

Your mind is busier too. Lying awake thinking about the birth, work and everything still to sort out is common at the end of the second trimester. If anxiety or low mood is taking up most of your days, say so at your appointment. Your midwife or doctor can point you to psychological support.

## Prenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ by country, so here is what applies in England and what applies in the US.

In England, the NHS plans an appointment at 25 weeks if this is your first baby and one at 28 weeks for everyone. At 28 weeks you are offered blood tests, and if you are rhesus negative your midwife explains the preventive treatment offered then. The glucose tolerance test for gestational diabetes is done between 24 and 28 weeks, and only for women with a higher chance of developing it.

In the US, ACOG lists glucose screening at 24 to 28 weeks as a routine test for all pregnant women. How often you are seen is set with your ob-gyn or midwife.

In both countries your blood pressure is measured at every visit. That is the main way pre-eclampsia is picked up early, since it often causes no symptoms at all.

Vaccines are where the two countries differ most this week. The whooping cough vaccine is usually offered around week 20 in England, where it can be given from week 16 and is best had before week 32. In the US, the CDC and ACOG recommend it between 27 and 36 weeks, as early in that window as possible, so the window opens this week. The RSV vaccine, which helps protect your baby against a common cause of bronchiolitis, is offered from 28 weeks in every pregnancy in England. In the US it is given between 32 and 36 weeks, during the RSV season. The flu vaccine is recommended at any stage of pregnancy.

## What to do this week

1. If a glucose test was planned for you, check that it has been done. If it has not, ask your care team to book it as soon as possible.
2. Sort out your vaccines. In the US, ask about the whooping cough vaccine, which is recommended from 27 weeks. In England, check you have had it and ask about the RSV vaccine, offered from 28 weeks.
3. Think about where you would like to give birth, and save the phone number and address of your maternity unit (UK) or labor and delivery unit (US). Do the same for where you will be staying if you travel.
4. Start sleeping on your side, either one. The NHS advises going to sleep on your side from 28 weeks, because research suggests that going to sleep on your back at that stage raises the risk of stillbirth. A pillow between your knees and another under your bump help. If you wake up on your back, just roll over again.
5. If you are employed, talk to your employer about leave. In the UK you have maternity rights, including paid time off for antenatal appointments and a risk assessment of your workplace. In the US, ask your employer what leave applies to you.

> Get medical help without waiting for your next appointment if you have a severe headache that will not go away, blurred vision or seeing spots, sudden swelling of your face or hands, or strong pain in the upper abdomen. The same goes for vaginal bleeding, fluid leaking from your vagina, contractions or cramps that come at regular intervals (a bump that tightens now and then, with no rhythm and for a few seconds, is a practice contraction), pressure low in your pelvis, a high temperature, or a baby who is moving much less than usual. Call your maternity unit or midwife (UK) or your ob-gyn (US). For a seizure, fainting, severe breathlessness or heavy bleeding, call 999 or 911.
