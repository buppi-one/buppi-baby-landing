**Quick answer:** weeks 1 to 3 are the start of the 1st month and the 1st trimester, but for the first two there is no baby yet. Pregnancy is counted from the first day of your last period, and conception does not usually happen until around the end of week 2. In week 3 the fertilised egg travels to the womb. There is **no size or weight to measure yet**, and a pregnancy test only becomes reliable once your period is late.

The count looks odd, and the reason is practical: almost nobody knows the exact day they conceived, while the date of the last menstrual period (LMP) is usually remembered. So midwives and doctors add 40 weeks, or 280 days, to that date to reach the estimated due date. About two of those 40 weeks pass before there is an embryo. If your cycle is irregular or you cannot remember the date, the first scan is used to set how many weeks pregnant you are.

## How your baby is developing at 1 to 3 weeks

In week 1 you are on your period. The womb sheds the lining from the previous cycle and, in the ovaries, usually one new egg starts to mature.

In week 2 the lining of the womb thickens again and the egg gets ready to be released. Ovulation generally happens about 14 days before your next period. In a 28-day cycle that falls at the end of week 2, and in longer or shorter cycles the date shifts with it.

A study that followed 221 women trying to conceive, with daily urine samples, found that conception only happened when they had sex during a six-day window ending on the day of ovulation. The chance was about 10% per cycle with sex five days before ovulation and about 33% on the day itself. The same study found no link between the day of intercourse and the sex of the baby.

In week 3, if an egg was fertilised, that usually happened in the fallopian tube. The fertilised egg divides as it moves down towards the womb and arrives as a cluster of cells still too small to measure. Implantation, when that cluster attaches to the lining of the womb, took place 6 to 12 days after ovulation in the pregnancies in the study that continued, and in 84% of them between day 8 and day 10. In practice that is the end of week 3 or the start of week 4.

Not every fertilised egg goes on to become a pregnancy. In that group, about a quarter of the pregnancies picked up by the tests ended before 6 weeks, early enough for the loss to pass for a late period. It is a common outcome at this stage. If it happens to you and leaves you with questions, raise them with your GP, midwife or doctor.

## What is changing in your body

In weeks 1 and 2, what you feel comes from your menstrual cycle: the bleeding, the cramps and, close to ovulation, a clearer, stretchier discharge.

In week 3 most women notice nothing. The first signs of pregnancy usually start at around 4 to 6 weeks, and the most common is a missed period. The pregnancy hormone (hCG) only starts to be produced about six days after fertilisation, and that hormone is what urine and blood tests detect.

Some women have a very light bleed around the time their period was due, known as implantation bleeding. Many pregnant women never have it, and its absence says nothing about the pregnancy. Because you cannot tell the cause of a bleed at home, tell your midwife or doctor if it shows up after a positive test. Every body reacts differently, and feeling nothing at this stage is what you would expect.

## Antenatal care at this stage

In these weeks a pregnancy cannot be confirmed yet, so the care that exists is the care that comes before it. If you are trying for a baby, the NHS in England recommends checking that you have had the measles, mumps and rubella vaccine, that your cervical screening is up to date, and that you see a GP if you or your partner have a long-term condition or take any medicine. It also recommends taking folic acid from before conception and through the first weeks, because it helps prevent neural tube defects. Your GP, midwife or pharmacist can tell you which supplement and how much. In the US the same conversation happens with your ob-gyn or primary care provider. If the pregnancy came without that preparation, raise it at your first appointment, which is worth booking soon.

To confirm a pregnancy, most home tests are reliable from the first day of a missed period, using urine collected at any time of day. A positive result is almost certainly correct. A negative taken too early can be wrong, so repeat it after a few days if your period does not arrive. If the second is also negative and your period is still late, see your GP or healthcare provider.

Once you have a positive test, the next step is to get your care started. In England you can refer yourself to NHS maternity services as soon as you find out. The first midwife appointment, called the booking appointment, usually takes place between 8 and 12 weeks, and the dating scan is offered at around 10 to 14 weeks. A first pregnancy has at least 10 appointments, and at least 7 if you have given birth before. In the US, call your ob-gyn or midwife's office to schedule your first prenatal visit, and ask when they will see you.

## What to do in these weeks

1. Write down the first day of each period. It is the date you will be asked for at the first appointment to work out how many weeks pregnant you are and your due date.
2. If you are trying to conceive, talk to a healthcare professional about folic acid and about any medicine you take. Do not stop a prescribed treatment on your own.
3. Give up alcohol and smoking now, because the first weeks go by before a test turns positive. If stopping is hard, ask your GP, midwife or doctor for support. Go easy on coffee and other sources of caffeine.
4. Have sex every two or three days throughout your cycle if you are hoping to get pregnant. That covers the fertile days without having to pin down ovulation, which varies even in women with regular cycles.
5. Wait until your period is late to test. If it is positive, refer yourself for NHS pregnancy care (England) or call your ob-gyn or midwife (US).

> If there is a chance you are pregnant, with or without a positive test, get seen the same day if you have vaginal bleeding that is different from your period, pain low down in your tummy, especially on one side, or a fever. In the UK contact your GP or call NHS 111, and in the US call your ob-gyn. These signs can have several causes, and one of them is an ectopic pregnancy (outside the womb), which tends to cause symptoms between weeks 4 and 12. Pain in the tip of your shoulder, sudden and severe abdominal pain, feeling very dizzy or fainting are an emergency: call 999 (UK) or 911 (US), or go straight to an emergency department.

If you have been trying for a year without getting pregnant, book an appointment to look into it. From age 36, or if you already know of a fertility problem, the NHS advises asking for help sooner.
