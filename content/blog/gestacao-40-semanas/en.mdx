**Quick answer:** at 40 weeks you are at the end of the 9th month and the third trimester, in your due-date week. The baby is **about 51.2 cm** from head to heel and weighs **about 3.6 kg**, roughly the size of a pumpkin. Your baby is full term, and reaching this week with no sign of labour is common: only about 1 in 20 babies is born on the due date itself.

"Forty weeks" means 40 completed weeks, from 40 weeks and 0 days to 40 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. The due date is set at exactly 40 weeks. It marks the middle of a range, and the pregnancy is still full term up to 40 weeks and 6 days.

## Your baby at 40 weeks

The length comes from the NHS week-by-week guide and is measured from head to heel. The weight is the median of the World Health Organization fetal growth charts, and in that study 2 in 10 babies fell outside the 3.1 kg to 4.1 kg range. Late in pregnancy, ultrasound weight estimates have a margin of error, so a number above or below average does not on its own decide how the birth will go. Your care team interprets the result.

According to ACOG, the brain and lungs finish maturing in the last weeks, and that maturing is the reason full term only starts at 39 weeks.

Space is tight, but the NHS says your baby should keep moving in their usual pattern. Movements do not slow down or stop near the birth. If the pattern changes or drops, it can be an important sign and needs checking at your maternity unit straight away. Any clear drop is enough reason to call; there is no set number of hours to wait.

## Changes in your body

Practice contractions, or Braxton Hicks, are common now. They make your bump tight, come irregularly and are usually painless. Labour contractions feel different: they hurt while the bump tightens, ease when the muscle relaxes, and get stronger and closer together.

The NHS advises calling your hospital or midwife when contractions last at least 60 seconds and come every 5 minutes. The mucus plug can come away days before labour and does not mean on its own that labour has started. According to the NHS, the first stage of labour, until the cervix is 10 cm open, lasts 6 to 12 hours, or less if you have had a baby before.

Your waters can break at any time, and it is not always a gush: sometimes it is just a trickle. If you think your waters have broken, even without contractions, call your midwife or maternity unit, and if you can't tell whether it is urine or amniotic fluid, get checked. Note the colour of the fluid and tell the team.

Backache, tiredness, trouble sleeping, heartburn, mild gradual swelling of the hands and feet, leg cramps and constipation are also common at this stage. Every pregnancy has its own rhythm, and not everyone gets all of these. For pain, only take a medicine your midwife or doctor has advised.

The wait can be hard, and daily "any news yet?" messages and comparisons with other people's pregnancies wear you down. If anxiety is affecting your sleep or your day, tell your care team, who can point you to support.

## Prenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ by country, so here are England and the United States separately.

In England, the NHS schedule includes an appointment at 40 weeks if this is your first baby and one at 41 weeks for everyone still pregnant. Your blood pressure is checked, your bump is measured and you give a urine sample, partly to check for signs of pre-eclampsia. The RSV vaccine is offered from 28 weeks and can still be given right up until labour, so if you have missed it, ask your midwife or GP. The same goes for the whooping cough vaccine, which works best before 32 weeks but can still be given later.

In the United States, ACOG says tests to check on the baby may be recommended at 41 weeks, and induction may be recommended at that point too. The decision depends on how you and the baby are doing. If you did not get the maternal RSV vaccine between 32 weeks and 36 weeks and 6 days, the CDC guidance is that the baby is protected after birth with an infant immunization instead.

In both countries, 42 weeks and beyond is called post-term. Ask at this week's appointment what your unit's plan is if labour hasn't started.

## What to do this week

1. Keep your hospital bag ready with your notes, documents and clothes. If you're going by car, have the car seat fitted and practise buckling it.
2. Plan the route to the maternity unit and who will take you.
3. Line up help for the first weeks: who can handle meals, laundry and the house while you recover and feed the baby. Freezing a few meals now helps.
4. Ask at your appointment what happens if labour hasn't started by 41 weeks.
5. Practise breathing for labour: the NHS suggests deep breaths in through the nose and out through the mouth, with your jaw relaxed. A warm bath and changing positions are also on the NHS list of tips for coping with contractions.

> Contact your maternity unit or midwife (UK) or ob-gyn (US) straight away if your waters break, you have vaginal bleeding, strong regular contractions, a fever or strong constant tummy pain, or if your baby is moving less than usual. A severe headache that won't go away, blurred vision or flashing lights, strong pain under the ribs and sudden swelling of the face, hands or legs can be signs of pre-eclampsia and need checking straight away. If you have a seizure, faint, feel very short of breath or have chest pain, call 999 (UK) or 911 (US).
