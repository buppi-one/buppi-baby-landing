**Quick answer:** at 12 weeks you are in the 3rd month and the last days of the 1st trimester. Your baby is **about 5.4 cm** from head to bottom, roughly the size of a lime. This is the time of the first-trimester scan and, for many people, the week they share the news.

"Twelve weeks" means twelve completed weeks, from 12 weeks and 0 days to 12 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. The due date is set at 40 weeks, so there are about 28 to go. Converting weeks to months is approximate, which is why your care team always talks in weeks.

## How your baby is developing at 12 weeks

The length is given from head to bottom because the legs are curled up and hard to measure. The figure of about 5.4 cm comes from the NHS week-by-week guide and is an average. Your own baby's measurement is the one on your scan report.

The internal organs and muscles have grown a lot and keep maturing until the end of pregnancy. The skeleton, which started as soft tissue, is hardening into bone. The heartbeat can be picked up on an ultrasound scan. Between 9 and 12 weeks the eyelids and fingernails also form, and the kidneys start to make urine.

The sex organs are there, but most scans cannot tell the sex reliably yet. That usually waits for the second-trimester scans.

Your baby is moving, although you cannot feel it. There is plenty of room and the baby is small. First movements are usually felt between 16 and 24 weeks, and in a first pregnancy often after 20.

## What changes in your body

According to the NHS, the placenta has probably started taking over the job of feeding your baby, in a gradual handover, and your hormones tend to settle a little. Many women notice the sickness easing. For most it is gone by week 14, and for some it lasts longer. Nausea that continues, without the warning signs listed further down, does not usually point to a problem with the pregnancy.

Your waist thickens and your breasts keep growing. Bump size varies a lot from one woman to another. Hormones, your weight before pregnancy, the strength of your abdominal muscles and how many pregnancies you have had all play a part, so comparing your bump with someone else's tells you little.

Other common symptoms at this stage:

- Bloating, heartburn and indigestion, because your gut works more slowly. Smaller, more frequent meals often help.
- Headaches, dizziness and mood swings.
- A metallic taste in your mouth, a sharper sense of smell and new cravings or dislikes.
- White, milky discharge, which is expected. If it smells strong, itches or turns yellowish or greenish, book an appointment.
- Mild cramps, a bit like period pains. If they are strong or come with bleeding, get checked.
- Darker patches on your face, greasier skin and thicker hair.

As the sickness fades, you will probably feel hungrier. The NHS points out that there is no need to eat for two and that most weight gain happens after week 20. Some women reach 12 weeks without having put on any weight. Your care team follows your weight and gives advice that fits you.

## Prenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ by country, so here is what applies in England and what applies in the US.

In England, the first midwife appointment (the booking appointment) takes place between 8 and 12 weeks. If you have not had it yet, contact your GP surgery or local maternity service this week. The NHS plans at least 10 appointments if this is your first baby and at least 7 if you have given birth before, plus 2 ultrasound scans. After the booking appointment and the scan, the next routine appointment is at 16 weeks.

The dating scan, often called the 12-week scan, is offered at around 10 to 14 weeks. It works out how far along you are and your due date, shows whether you are expecting more than one baby and checks early development. If you have agreed to screening for Down's, Edwards' and Patau's syndromes, it happens at this scan when it takes place between 10 and 14 weeks. That screening is the combined test: a blood test plus a measurement of the fluid at the back of your baby's neck, called the nuchal translucency. A screening result gives a chance and is not a diagnosis, so a higher-chance result leads to a conversation about further tests with your midwife or doctor.

In the US, the timing of first-trimester visits, scans and screening is set with your ob-gyn or midwife, so ask at your next visit what is planned and when.

On vaccines, the flu vaccine is recommended in pregnancy at any stage in England. The whooping cough vaccine comes later: the NHS usually offers it around week 20 and can give it from week 16, while in the US the CDC and ACOG recommend it between 27 and 36 weeks.

## What to do this week

1. If your first-trimester scan is not booked, book it now. In England the combined screening test can only be done at a scan that takes place by 14 weeks. If you cannot get a slot in time, tell your midwife.
2. Ask at your next appointment which vaccines are due and when.
3. Decide when to tell people. Many women wait until the end of the first trimester and the first scan, because the risk of miscarriage is higher in the early weeks and falls as they pass. There is no right date. In the UK, once you tell your employer you have maternity rights and can attend antenatal appointments in paid work time.
4. If your energy is back, get moving again. The general recommendation is about 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, and you can start with 10-minute walks. Check with your care team what suits your situation.
5. Stay away from alcohol and smoking, go easy on caffeine and keep to the supplements your midwife or doctor has advised. Do not start or stop any medicine or supplement on your own.

> Get medical advice without waiting for your next appointment if you have vaginal bleeding, fluid leaking from your vagina, strong or persistent abdominal pain, a high temperature, pain or burning when you pee, or vomiting that stops you keeping food or fluids down for 24 hours, with very dark urine or dizziness when you stand up. Light bleeding can happen in the first trimester without anything being seriously wrong, but it needs to be checked the same day. Call your maternity unit, midwife or early pregnancy unit (UK, or NHS 111 if you cannot reach them) or your ob-gyn (US). Heavy bleeding, severe pain, pain in your shoulder or fainting are an emergency: call 999 or 911.
