At 5 months, your baby's sleep tends to calm down a bit — wake windows get longer, naps start settling into three more predictable blocks, and for many families nights come back together after the turbulence of the [4-month regression](/en/blog/4-month-sleep-regression/). It's not a Swiss watch yet, but the rhythm shows up more clearly. This guide covers how much a 5-month-old usually sleeps, how many naps they take, wake windows, a flexible schedule, how nights look, and what changes in safe sleep now that your baby rolls.

## How much a 5-month-old sleeps

In total, a 5-month-old sleeps about **12 to 15 hours a day**, counting night and naps, with the average around **14 hours**. The difference from 4 months is that sleep is now **more concentrated at night**: the night stretch tends to be longer, and daytime sleep organizes into fewer but more structured naps.

As always, this is a **wide range of normal**. The best sign that things are fine isn't hitting a number — it's a baby who feeds well, gains weight, and is alert and smiley during awake times.

## Wake windows at 5 months

The **wake window** — how long your baby can comfortably stay awake between sleeps — is usually **2 to 2.5 hours** at 5 months, a bit longer than the month before. The **last window of the day** (before night sleep) tends to be the longest, closer to 2.5 to 3 hours.

Going much beyond that still tends to leave the baby **overtired**: more wound up and, paradoxically, harder to settle. Keep **reading sleepy cues** — yawning, a glazed stare, rubbing their eyes or face, going quieter or fussier — instead of just timing.

## Naps at 5 months

At 5 months, most babies take **3 naps a day**, though some still take 4. They start settling into more predictable times, and some get longer — but **short 30- to 45-minute naps are still common** and normal. The big transition to **2 naps** usually comes later, between 6 and 9 months, so it's not the time to force dropping any.

A practical tip: when a nap is short, shorten the next window a little, so your baby doesn't build up overtiredness before the next nap.

## A flexible one-day schedule

There's no fixed schedule at 5 months, but an example with **3 naps and windows of ~2 to 2.5 hours** helps picture the rhythm. Use it as an elastic reference, not a rule:

| Time (example) | What happens |
|---|---|
| 7:00 | Wakes and feeds |
| 9:00–10:15 | 1st nap |
| 10:30 | Feed, awake time |
| 12:30–13:45 | 2nd nap |
| 14:00 | Feed, awake time |
| 16:00–16:40 | 3rd nap (shorter) |
| 17:00 | Feed, awake time |
| 18:45 | Feed, calm routine |
| 19:15–20:00 (varies) | Start of night sleep |
| Overnight | 0–2 wakings to feed |

Notice: **3 naps**, windows that grow across the day, and a bedtime that's more consistent than at 4 months. There may still be wakings to feed — usually fewer than in earlier months.

## Sleep at night

At 5 months, many babies do a **longer first night stretch** — 6 to 8 hours or more in some cases — and nights tend to get more predictable than at the peak of the regression. Even so, **waking 0 to 2 times to feed stays normal and expected**. The frequency varies a lot, and **breastfed babies tend to feed more often** than formula-fed ones. It's not the time to cut night feeds on your own. On the other hand, if wakings are **very frequent** (every hour, for several weeks), it's worth talking to your pediatrician to rule out real hunger, discomfort, or a heavy reliance on an adult to fall back asleep.

You support this progress with the same cues as always:

- **By day**: natural light, normal household noise, interaction and stimulation
- **By night**: low light, quiet voice, changing the diaper only if needed, and feeding in "quiet and boring" mode, no play

## Still echoes of the 4-month regression?

For many babies, by 5 months the worst of the [4-month sleep regression](/en/blog/4-month-sleep-regression/) has passed and sleep starts to consolidate. For others, it still gives some trouble — and that's OK: there's no exact end date. If sleep is still messy, keep betting on **consistency** (a light routine, wake windows, safe sleep) rather than changing everything after each bad night.

Worth remembering that **not every hard night is "the regression"**: around this age, the first **teeth** can start bothering, and discomfort, a cold, or a growth spurt also disrupt sleep. If the worsening comes with fever, inconsolable crying, or signs of pain, think of a physical cause and, when in doubt, see your pediatrician.

## Safe sleep at 5 months

At 5 months, many babies already **roll** — and that changes part of the safe-sleep guidance.

> If your baby can roll both ways on their own (**back to tummy and back again**), you can **leave them in whatever position they choose** during sleep. And even if they only roll one way, **if they roll on their own during sleep you don't need to wake them to turn them back** — what matters is always **placing them on their back to sleep**. Keep the crib safe: **a firm, flat mattress**, **no** pillows, cushions, bumpers or loose blankets, and **in the same room as the parents** (not the same bed) for at least the first 6 months. The **swaddle should have been dropped well before this** — at the first signs of rolling, usually between 2 and 4 months — so at 5 months your baby sleeps with **arms free**. If you need to keep them warm, use a **sleep sack with arms out** instead of a blanket.

## What helps sleep at 5 months

As the rhythm settles, a few things help consolidate better sleep:

- **Keep a light, predictable routine** before sleep (warm bath, feed, low light, a song), in the same order — the ritual signals "time to sleep"
- **Respect wake windows** and protect the last window of the day, so your baby reaches night without being overtired
- **Put your baby down drowsy but still awake** when you can — it helps them practice falling asleep on their own, useful too for the wakings between cycles
- Keep the **day–night difference** clearly marked

On **sleep training**: between 4 and 6 months some more structured methods start to be considered, and whether to use them (or not) is the family's call — worth discussing with your pediatrician. None of it is mandatory: many babies consolidate sleep on consistency and time alone.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Sleep at 5 months still varies, but it's worth talking to your pediatrician if:

- Your baby is **very sleepy**, hard to wake, and **doesn't wake to feed** enough
- There's **insufficient weight gain** or few wet diapers
- Your baby **snores loudly**, pauses in breathing, or seems to work hard to breathe while asleep
- The sleep trouble comes with **extreme irritability**, fever or other signs something's wrong
- You're **exhausted to the point of not coping** — asking for help is also caring for your baby

## What to expect next

Five months is a stage of sleep settling in: longer windows, three more organized naps, and nights that, in most cases, start to stretch after the regression. On the other side, around [6 months](/en/blog/baby-sleep-6-months/), many babies gain even longer nights and a clearer nap routine — and it's when the first solids come in. If your baby's sleep is still messy, re-read the [4-month guide](/en/blog/baby-sleep-4-months/) and remember: the trick stays the same — go at your baby's pace, with wake windows, a light routine, safe sleep and patience.
