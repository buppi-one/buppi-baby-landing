**Quick answer:** at 17 weeks you are in the last week of the 4th month, in the 2nd trimester. Your baby is **about 12 cm** from head to bottom and weighs around **180 g**, roughly the size of a pear. Some women start to feel the first movements around now, and many still feel nothing.

"Seventeen weeks" means seventeen completed weeks, from 17 weeks and 0 days to 17 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period and checked against your early pregnancy scan. The due date is set at 40 weeks, so there are about 23 to go. Because converting weeks to months is approximate, your care team always talks in weeks.

## How your baby is developing at 17 weeks

Length is still measured from head to bottom, because the legs are curled up. The figure of about 12 cm comes from the NHS week-by-week guide. The weight is the median on the World Health Organization fetal growth charts. Other tables give different numbers because they measure in a different way. For your own baby, the scan report is what counts.

According to the NHS, this week your baby can move their eyes, which stay shut, open and close their mouth and react to loud noises. The fingernails are starting to grow. Fingerprints form at this stage too, and even identical twins end up with different ones.

Your baby is moving, but plenty of women cannot feel it this week. The NHS says first movements are felt between 16 and 24 weeks, and that in a first pregnancy it is common not to notice them until after 20. At first they feel like bubbles or a light flutter low in your belly, and many people take them for wind. There is no pattern of movements to keep track of yet. That only applies later, once they are regular.

## What changes in your body

Your womb is rising up and out of your pelvis, and your waist is disappearing. The pregnancy starts to show to other people, although bump size varies a lot from one woman to another. The placenta grows along with your baby and, according to the NHS, weighs around 500 g by the end of pregnancy.

Back pain often shows up at this stage. Your ligaments soften to prepare your body for labour, and that strains the joints of your lower back and pelvis. The NHS suggests bending your knees and keeping your back straight when you pick something up from the floor, avoiding heavy lifting, moving your feet when you turn so you do not twist your spine, wearing flat shoes and sitting with your back well supported. When the pain gets in the way of your day, your midwife or doctor can suggest exercises or refer you to a physiotherapist.

Other common symptoms this week:

- Sharp pains on the side of your belly, caused by the growing womb stretching the ligaments that hold it.
- Stretch marks on your belly, breasts, hips or thighs, which affect up to 8 in 10 women. They usually fade and become much less noticeable after the birth.
- Leg cramps, slightly swollen hands and feet, and feeling hot. Swelling that comes on suddenly in your face, hands or legs needs to be checked.
- Dizziness, mild headaches, and nosebleeds or bleeding gums.
- Heartburn, bloating and constipation.
- White, milky discharge, which is expected. If it smells strong, itches or turns yellowish or greenish, book an appointment.

Your mood changes too. The NHS estimates that around 1 in 5 women feel stressed or anxious during pregnancy, and hormones can make small problems feel overwhelming. Talk to your midwife or doctor if you notice sadness that does not lift, strong anxiety, constant fear or a pull to withdraw from people. They can refer you for mental health support, and how that works depends on where you live. Thoughts of harming yourself need help the same day: call your midwife or GP (UK) or your ob-gyn (US), or go to A&E or the emergency room. Call 999 or 911 if you are in immediate danger.

## Prenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ by country, so here is what applies in England and what applies in the US.

In England, the NHS plans a routine appointment at 16 weeks, where your midwife or doctor checks your blood pressure and urine and goes over your screening results. If you have not had it, contact your midwife this week. The next one is at 25 weeks if this is your first baby and at 28 weeks if you have given birth before. In the US, your ob-gyn or midwife sets the visit schedule, so ask at your next visit what is planned and when.

No routine test falls in week 17 itself, but the next ones are close. In England, the 20-week screening scan looks in detail at your baby's anatomy and usually takes place between 18 and 21 weeks. In the US, ask your ob-gyn when the second-trimester anatomy scan is scheduled. The test for gestational diabetes comes at 24 to 28 weeks: in England it is offered only to women with risk factors, while ACOG describes glucose screening at 24 to 28 weeks as a routine test in the US.

On vaccines, the flu vaccine is recommended at any stage of pregnancy in England. The whooping cough vaccine can be given from 16 weeks on the NHS and is usually offered around week 20, ideally before 32 weeks. In the US, the CDC and ACOG recommend it between 27 and 36 weeks, in every pregnancy.

## What to do this week

1. Check that your second-trimester scan is booked. In England the 20-week scan usually takes place between 18 and 21 weeks, so the letter or appointment should be with you by now. In the US, confirm the date with your ob-gyn's office.
2. Ask about the whooping cough vaccine. In England you are already eligible, since it can be given from 16 weeks. In the US it comes later, at 27 to 36 weeks.
3. Look after your back day to day: bend your knees to reach down, wear flat shoes and support your lower back when you sit.
4. Start pelvic floor exercises, which help prevent leaks when you cough or laugh. To find the muscle, imagine you are stopping your pee midflow, without practising while you actually pee. Keep moving as well: the general recommendation is about 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, and your care team can confirm what suits your situation.
5. If your mood has been low, say so at your next appointment, or before it.

> Get medical advice without waiting for your next appointment if you have vaginal bleeding, fluid leaking from your vagina, strong or persistent abdominal pain, a high temperature, pain or burning when you pee, a severe headache or one that will not go away, changes in your vision, or back pain that is severe, does not ease or comes with your belly tightening at regular intervals. If you are not sure whether what came out is discharge, urine or fluid, get checked. Call your maternity unit or midwife (UK) or your ob-gyn (US). Heavy bleeding, fainting, shortness of breath, severe chest pain or loss of feeling in your legs or genital area are an emergency: call 999 or 911.
