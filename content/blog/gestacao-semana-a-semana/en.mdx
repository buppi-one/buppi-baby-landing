**Quick answer:** a pregnancy lasts about 40 weeks, counted from the first day of your last period, and is divided into three trimesters. This page brings together the guide to each week, a weeks-to-months table, the baby's approximate size from week 4 to week 40, and the care schedule in England and the United States.

The numbers here are averages, and healthy pregnancies vary around them. For you, what matters is what your care team measures and records in your notes.

## How to count the weeks of pregnancy

Gestational age is counted from the first day of your last menstrual period. Counted this way, the first two weeks come before conception: in week 1 you are having your period, conception usually happens around week 2, and the embryo implants in the womb between weeks 3 and 4. If your cycles are regular, around 28 days, and you test just after a missed period, you are already about 4 weeks pregnant.

"20 weeks pregnant" means 20 completed weeks, from 20 weeks and 0 days to 20 weeks and 6 days. The same logic applies to every week.

The due date is set at 40 weeks after the first day of the last period. It is an estimate, and only about 1 in 20 babies is born on that exact day. The ultrasound done in the first trimester measures the embryo and helps date the pregnancy. If the measurement does not match your period date, which is more common when the date is uncertain or your cycles are irregular, your care team may adjust the gestational age and the due date.

The timing of birth also has names defined by gestational age:

| Classification | Gestational age |
|---|---|
| Preterm | before 37 weeks |
| Early term | 37 weeks to 38 weeks and 6 days |
| Full term | 39 weeks to 40 weeks and 6 days |
| Late term | 41 weeks to 41 weeks and 6 days |
| Post-term | 42 weeks and beyond |

## Weeks of pregnancy in months

Converting weeks into months is approximate, because calendar months are a little longer than four weeks. This is the table used in every article of the series:

You may find slightly different tables elsewhere. To avoid confusion, pregnancy care always works in weeks, and scan reports and the timing of tests and vaccines are given in weeks too.

## First trimester: weeks 1 to 13

The first trimester runs to 13 weeks and 6 days. This is when the organs form: between 9 and 12 weeks the eyelids and fingernails appear and the kidneys start making urine. By the end of the trimester the baby measures about 7.4 cm from head to bottom.

As for your own body, nausea usually starts before 9 weeks and, for most women, is gone by 14. Nausea that appears for the first time after 9 weeks needs to be checked by your care team, and vomiting that stops you keeping fluids down needs medical attention at any week.

In England, the first midwife appointment (the booking appointment) happens between 8 and 12 weeks, and the dating scan is offered at around 10 to 14 weeks. In the United States, call your ob-gyn or midwife as soon as you have a positive test to arrange the first visit.

## Second trimester: weeks 14 to 27

The second trimester runs from 14 weeks to 27 weeks and 6 days. The baby goes from about 90 g to about 1.0 kg. Hearing starts to develop between 13 and 16 weeks. Between 25 and 28 weeks the eyelids open and the lungs begin to make surfactant, the substance that helps them stay open after birth.

First movements are usually felt between 16 and 24 weeks, and in a first pregnancy it is common to notice them only after 20. If you reach 24 weeks without feeling anything, tell your midwife or doctor.

In England, the 20-week screening scan is usually done between 18 and 21 weeks, and the whooping cough vaccine is usually given around 20 weeks. In the United States, glucose screening for gestational diabetes is done for everyone between 24 and 28 weeks; in England the test is offered in that same window only to women with a higher chance of gestational diabetes.

## Third trimester: weeks 28 to 40

The third trimester starts at 28 weeks and lasts until the birth. The baby goes from about 1.2 kg to about 3.6 kg at week 40. Between 33 and 36 weeks the baby may turn head-down, and in the final weeks the lungs and brain finish maturing.

Appointments get closer together, and more vaccines are offered late in pregnancy. The windows differ between the two countries, as the table in the next section shows.

The NHS advises calling your maternity unit when contractions come every 5 minutes or more often. Call straight away, whatever the stage, if your waters break, you are bleeding, the baby is moving less than usual, or you are under 37 weeks and think labour has started. If the birth has not happened by 41 weeks, your care team will talk to you about monitoring and about the option of induction.

## Antenatal and prenatal care: England and the United States

Schedules differ from country to country. The table puts side by side what the NHS offers in England and what ACOG (the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists) and the CDC recommend in the United States. A dash means we have no verified national schedule to quote, so ask your own provider.

| | England (NHS) | United States (ACOG / CDC) |
|---|---|---|
| Appointments | At least 10 for a first baby, at least 7 if you have given birth before. Booking appointment at 8 to 12 weeks | — |
| Ultrasound scans | Two: dating scan at around 10 to 14 weeks and the 20-week scan, usually at 18 to 21 weeks | — |
| Whooping cough vaccine | From 16 weeks, usually around 20, ideally before 32 | Between 27 and 36 weeks, in every pregnancy |
| RSV vaccine | From 28 weeks, in every pregnancy | Between 32 weeks and 36 weeks and 6 days, in season; not repeated in later pregnancies |
| Flu vaccine | Recommended at any stage of pregnancy | — |
| Glucose test for gestational diabetes | 24 to 28 weeks, only for women with a higher chance of it | 24 to 28 weeks, for everyone |
| Group B strep test | Not routinely offered | Between 36 weeks and 37 weeks and 6 days |
| At 41 weeks | An appointment is scheduled at 41 weeks | Testing may be recommended, and induction may be recommended |

Other tests may be added depending on your situation, such as those for women with a rhesus negative blood type. The result of each one is interpreted at your appointment.

> At any point in pregnancy, get help without waiting for your next appointment if you have vaginal bleeding, fluid leaking from the vagina, severe or constant abdominal pain, a severe headache that will not go away, changes in your vision, pain just below the ribs, sudden swelling of the face, hands or feet, or difficulty breathing. Regular contractions or period-type pains before 37 weeks also need to be checked the same day. Once you feel the baby move every day, call immediately if the movements slow down, stop or change from their usual pattern. Contact your maternity unit or midwife (UK) or your ob-gyn (US), and call 999 or 911 in an emergency.

After the birth the count starts again from zero. To get ready, read about the [first 30 days with a newborn](/en/blog/first-30-days-with-a-newborn/), [breastfeeding in the first days](/en/blog/breastfeeding-first-days/) and [newborn sleep in the first weeks](/en/blog/newborn-sleep-weeks-1-4/).
