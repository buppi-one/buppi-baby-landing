**Quick answer:** at 28 weeks you are starting the 7th month and the 3rd and final trimester. Your baby is **about 37.6 cm** long from head to heel and weighs **about 1.2 kg**, roughly the size of an aubergine (eggplant). In England this is the week the vaccine that protects your baby against bronchiolitis becomes available, and in the US the whooping cough vaccine is now due.

"Twenty-eight weeks" means 28 completed weeks, from 28 weeks and 0 days to 28 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. The due date is set at 40 weeks, so there are about 12 to go.

## How your baby is developing at 28 weeks

The length comes from the NHS week-by-week guide and is measured from head to heel. The weight is the median of the World Health Organization fetal growth charts, which were built from low-risk pregnancies. Both numbers are reference values. In that study, 2 in 10 babies fell outside the 1.0 kg to 1.4 kg range, so a different estimate does not point to a problem by itself. Your care team reads the scan by looking at how the measurements change from one to the next.

According to the NHS, your baby's heart rate changes all the time and is now around 140 beats per minute. It was faster earlier, close to 170 beats around weeks 9 and 10, and should be about 130 at birth. That is still well above an adult's heart rate, because a heart this small pumps very little blood with each beat.

Between 25 and 28 weeks the eyelids, which had been closed, open. In the same period the lungs start to make surfactant, the substance that lets the air sacs fill with air and stops them sticking together when they empty. The lungs keep maturing until the end of pregnancy.

Under the World Health Organization classification, a baby born between 28 and just under 32 weeks is very preterm and needs care in a neonatal unit.

Movements are strong by now. With less room they may feel different, but they tend to become more frequent until around 32 weeks and then stay about the same.

## What is changing in your body

The NHS warns that over the next few weeks you will probably feel more tired and uncomfortable. Heartburn and indigestion become more frequent, because the baby takes up more room and hormones slow your digestion. Your back is carrying the extra weight, and your joints and ligaments are looser than usual.

Your ankles and feet may swell a little, mostly in hot weather and at the end of the day. The NHS puts this down, in most cases, to water retention. Mention it at your appointment anyway, because swelling also appears in pre-eclampsia, a condition in which blood pressure rises and which can develop while you still feel well.

Other common symptoms at this stage:

- Nosebleeds, caused by hormonal changes. The NHS advises sitting or standing up, leaning forward and pinching your nose just above the nostrils for 10 to 15 minutes. If the bleeding does not stop, get medical help.
- Leg cramps, backache and pains at the side of your bump, caused by your expanding womb. Pain that is severe or does not ease needs checking.
- Poor sleep, constipation, piles and swollen, bleeding gums.
- Sore breasts that may leak a few drops of colostrum, the first milk.
- Stretch marks and darker patches of skin on the face.

How strong these are varies from one woman to another and from one pregnancy to the next.

Your bump may also tighten now and then. Practice contractions are irregular, short and far apart. Before 37 weeks, contractions that come regularly or often, period-type cramps, pressure in the pelvis or a constant dull ache low in your back are signs of preterm labour, and you should call your maternity unit or ob-gyn straight away to be assessed.

According to the NHS, after 28 weeks it is safest to go to sleep on your side, at night and for daytime naps, because this helps reduce the risk of stillbirth. If you wake up on your back, turn onto your side and go back to sleep.

## Antenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ by country, so here is what applies in England and what applies in the US.

In England, 28 weeks is one of the scheduled NHS antenatal appointments. The next ones are at 31 weeks if this is your first baby, then 34 and 36. If your blood group is rhesus negative, the 28-week appointment is when a blood test is done and a preventive injection is offered if your baby is at risk of rhesus disease. The glucose tolerance test for gestational diabetes is done between 24 and 28 weeks, but only for women at higher chance of the condition. The RSV vaccine is offered from 28 weeks in every pregnancy, usually around this appointment, and can still be given later, up to labour. RSV is a common cause of bronchiolitis, and the antibodies you make cross the placenta and protect your baby from birth. The whooping cough vaccine is usually offered around week 20 and is best had before 32 weeks, so check that you have had it.

In the US, the CDC and ACOG recommend the whooping cough (Tdap) vaccine in every pregnancy between 27 and 36 weeks, as early in that window as possible, so now is a good time to ask for it. The maternal RSV vaccine comes later, as a single dose between 32 and 36 weeks that is not repeated in later pregnancies. It is seasonal, so ask your provider whether it applies to your dates. Glucose screening is done for all pregnant women at 24 to 28 weeks. If you have not had it, tell your ob-gyn's office this week.

The flu vaccine is recommended during flu season, at any stage of pregnancy, in both countries.

## What to do this week

1. Get the vaccine due in your country: RSV in England, whooping cough in the US. Take your vaccination record and ask your midwife or provider anything you are unsure about.
2. If you are in the US, or in England with risk factors for gestational diabetes, check that your glucose test has been done.
3. Get to know your baby's pattern of movements. There is no set number of kicks per day. Your baby's usual rhythm is the reference, and a drop in it is a reason to call your maternity unit or ob-gyn the same day.
4. Ask about antenatal classes. The NHS suggests asking your midwife or doctor, who may be able to recommend one, and bringing your partner along if they would like to come.
5. Start going to sleep on your side, and begin pelvic floor exercises if you have not yet. The NHS describes the movement as tightening the muscles you would use to stop a pee midflow. They help prevent leaks when you cough or laugh.

> Get checked without waiting for your next appointment if you have vaginal bleeding, fluid leaking from your vagina, regular contractions or strong pressure low in your belly, a fever, or pain or burning when you pee. Call straight away if your baby is moving less than usual or the pattern of movements changes. A severe headache that does not go away, blurred vision or flashing lights, severe pain just below the ribs and sudden swelling of the face, hands or legs are signs of high blood pressure in pregnancy and need immediate assessment. Call your midwife or maternity unit in the UK, or your ob-gyn in the US. For a seizure, fainting, severe breathlessness, chest pain or heavy bleeding, call 999 in the UK or 911 in the US.
