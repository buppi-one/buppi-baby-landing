**Quick answer:** 35 weeks is the last week of the 8th month, well into the 3rd trimester. Your baby is **about 46.2 cm** long from head to heel and weighs **about 2.5 kg**, roughly the size of a honeydew melon. A baby born now would still be premature, and this is a good week to ask about group B strep testing and write your birth plan.

"Thirty-five weeks" means 35 completed weeks, from 35 weeks and 0 days to 35 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. The due date is set at 40 weeks, so there are about 5 to go.

## How your baby is developing at 35 weeks

The length comes from the week-by-week guide of the NHS, the UK's public health service, and is measured from head to heel. The weight is the median of the World Health Organization fetal growth charts. The range of 2.2 kg to 2.9 kg shows how much babies differ from one another by now. A weight from an ultrasound is an estimate with a margin of error, and your care team reads it against your earlier scans.

According to the NHS, your baby is getting chubbier, and that fat will help them keep their body temperature steady after birth. Between 33 and 36 weeks many babies turn head down, the most common position for birth. If yours is still bottom down there is time to turn, and the position is checked at your appointments.

Space in the womb is tight, but movements should not drop off. The NHS says you should keep feeling your baby as you have in previous weeks. The reference is the pattern you already know, and a change in it, with weaker or fewer movements, is a reason to call your maternity unit.

A baby born this week is premature. The World Health Organization counts every baby born before 37 completed weeks as preterm and places those born from 32 to less than 37 weeks in the moderate to late preterm group. The maternity team decides what care the baby needs after examining them.

## What is changing in your body

Yellowish stains in your bra are usually colostrum, the first milk, which is rich in antibodies. Some women start making it weeks or months before the birth and others only afterwards, and both are expected. The NHS notes that your milk usually comes in around 3 to 5 days after the birth.

Sore ribs are typical of this week. The NHS links them to a baby who is head down and kicking upwards. Severe pain, or pain just under the ribs, is a different matter: it also happens in pre-eclampsia and needs to be checked straight away.

Other common symptoms at 35 weeks, according to the NHS:

- Braxton Hicks contractions, which tighten the bump without pain and without a rhythm.
- Poor sleep, backache and leg cramps.
- Heartburn, indigestion, bloating and constipation.
- Slightly swollen hands and feet, feeling hot and dizziness. Swelling that comes on suddenly is one of the warning signs.
- Piles (haemorrhoids), bleeding gums and stretch marks.

Leaking a little urine when you cough, laugh or sneeze also shows up late in pregnancy, and pelvic floor exercises help prevent it. Fluid that runs out without your control, in a gush or a trickle, may be your waters breaking. If you cannot tell urine from amniotic fluid, call your maternity unit and get checked.

The NHS also lists urine and vaginal infections among this stage's complaints. Burning when you pee or discharge that is different from usual needs to be assessed. How strong the symptoms are varies from one woman to another and from one pregnancy to the next.

## Antenatal care at this stage

In England, the NHS schedule has appointments at 34, 36 and 38 weeks, so your next one is close. In the United States the timing of prenatal visits is set by your ob-gyn or midwife.

The group B strep swab raises a lot of questions at this stage. These bacteria are common, cause no symptoms in most people and, in a small share of cases, can pass to the baby during labour and make them ill. In England it is not routinely tested for in NHS care, though it may be found during tests done for another reason. In the United States, ACOG recommends a vaginal and rectal swab for everyone between 36 weeks and 37 weeks 6 days, so it usually happens at one of the next visits. If the test is positive, the team gives you care during labour that greatly reduces the risk of your baby becoming ill.

Check your vaccines too. In England the whooping cough vaccine can be given from 16 weeks, ideally before 32, and is still worth having now. The RSV vaccine is offered from 28 weeks and can be given right up until labour. In the United States the CDC window for the whooping cough vaccine is 27 to 36 weeks, and the RSV vaccine is given from 32 weeks to 36 weeks 6 days during the RSV season, so both windows close soon. The flu vaccine can be given at any stage of pregnancy.

## What to do this week

1. Talk to your employer about maternity leave. The NHS notes that many women start their leave around now and others work until the birth. In the UK you can ask for a workplace risk assessment and attend antenatal appointments during paid work time. In the United States the rules depend on your employer and your state, so ask HR.
2. Write your birth plan and take it to your next appointment. Ask which pain relief options your unit offers, with and without medicine, such as warm water, moving around freely and relaxation techniques. The NHS points out that you can combine methods and change your mind on the day.
3. Confirm where you will give birth, how you will get there and who your birth partner will be.
4. Keep your maternity notes, test results and documents in one folder next to your hospital bag.
5. Learn when to call. The NHS advises calling your midwife or maternity unit when contractions come every 5 minutes or more often. Before 37 weeks, do not wait that long: contractions that settle into a rhythm are a reason to call straight away. Losing the mucus plug on its own does not mean active labour.

> Get help without waiting for your next appointment if you have vaginal bleeding, fluid leaking from your vagina, regular contractions, period-type pains or strong pressure low in your bump, a fever, or pain or burning when you pee. Call immediately if your baby moves less than usual or their pattern changes. A severe headache that will not go away, blurred vision or flashing lights, bad pain at the top of your bump and sudden swelling of the face, hands or legs are signs of high blood pressure in pregnancy and need immediate assessment. Contact your maternity unit or midwife (UK) or your ob-gyn (US). In an emergency such as a seizure, fainting, severe breathlessness, chest pain or heavy bleeding, call 999 or 911.
