**Quick answer:** at 21 weeks you are in the 5th month and the 2nd trimester, into the second half of pregnancy. Your baby is **about 26.7 cm** long from head to toe and weighs **about 400 g**, roughly the length of a carrot. This week your baby becomes heavier than the placenta and can hear sounds from outside the womb.

"Twenty-one weeks" means twenty-one completed weeks, from 21 weeks and 0 days to 21 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. The due date is set at 40 weeks, so there are about 19 to go. Depending on how the months are divided, the same week is called the fifth or the sixth month, which is why your care team always talks in weeks.

## How your baby is developing at 21 weeks

The length comes from the NHS week-by-week guide and, from week 20 on, is measured from head to toe. The weight is the median of the World Health Organization fetal growth charts, estimated by ultrasound. Both are averages, and the measurements that count for your baby are the ones in your scan report.

According to the NHS, this is about when your baby becomes heavier than the placenta. The placenta keeps growing, because it goes on delivering oxygen and nutrients and carrying away what your baby gets rid of.

The body is covered in lanugo, a fine down that probably helps keep your baby at the right temperature and usually disappears before birth. Hair and eyebrows are growing too. Your baby can now hear noises and voices from outside the womb, including yours when you talk or sing to your bump. Your baby is also practising movements needed after birth, such as sucking and breathing.

ACOG, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, places the stage when kicks get stronger between 21 and 24 weeks. The Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (RCOG) explains that a baby's sleep periods mostly last between 20 and 40 minutes, day and night, and that the afternoon and evening tend to be the times of peak activity. You are less aware of movements when you are busy, which helps explain why they seem to start as soon as you lie down.

There is no set number of movements and no need to count kicks. Some women feel little at this stage, especially in a first pregnancy or with the placenta at the front of the womb. First movements are usually felt between 16 and 24 weeks, so not having felt anything at 21 is still within the expected range. If you reach 24 weeks without feeling anything, tell your midwife or doctor.

## What is changing in your body

As your bump grows, your centre of gravity shifts and your joints become looser. It is common to feel less steady when walking or going down stairs. If you fall, your baby is well protected inside the womb, but the NHS advises letting your midwife know as soon as possible, even without symptoms.

Sleep often gets harder: it is difficult to find a position, and your baby may be wide awake just when you want to sleep. The NHS says the safest position to fall asleep in is on your side, left or right. The research behind that advice concerns the period after 28 weeks, so for now it is mostly about comfort and building the habit. A pillow between your knees and another supporting your bump often help. If you wake up on your back, just turn onto your side.

Other common symptoms at this stage:

- Sharp pains at the sides of your bump, caused by the stretching of the ligaments that support the womb. Pain that is severe or does not ease needs to be assessed.
- Backache, leg cramps and slightly swollen feet and hands at the end of the day. Swelling that comes on suddenly is a warning sign.
- Heartburn, constipation and piles (hemorrhoids).
- Gums that bleed easily, the occasional nosebleed and feeling hot.
- Urine infections, which are more frequent in pregnancy. Pain or burning when you pee is not an expected symptom and needs an appointment.

Pregnancy can be a happy time and a stressful one, often both in the same week. If you feel hopeless, lose interest in things you used to enjoy or are kept awake by worry, tell your midwife or doctor, because mental health is part of antenatal care.

## Antenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ by country, so here is what applies in England and what applies in the US.

In England, the NHS plans at least 10 appointments for a first baby and at least 7 if you have given birth before. After the 16-week appointment, the next routine ones are at 25 weeks (first baby) and 28 weeks. The 20-week screening scan is usually done between 18 and 21 weeks and can be completed up to 23 weeks in some cases, so if you have not had it, contact your midwife now. If you have had it and the report mentioned something unexpected or asked for a repeat, talk it through with your midwife or doctor before drawing conclusions, because many findings only become clear at a second look.

In the US, the timing of the anatomy scan is set by your ob-gyn or midwife. International ultrasound guidance (ISUOG) places the routine mid-pregnancy scan between 18 and 24 weeks.

The whooping cough vaccine is where the two countries differ most. The NHS usually offers it around week 20. It can be given from week 16 and works best before week 32, so if you have not had it yet, this is the time to ask. In the US, the CDC and ACOG recommend it between 27 and 36 weeks, as early in that window as possible. Both repeat it in every pregnancy.

The next test on the calendar is the glucose test at 24 to 28 weeks. In the US it is routine for all pregnant women. In England it is only for women with a higher chance of gestational diabetes.

## What to do this week

1. In England, if you have not had the 20-week scan, contact your midwife now. It can be completed up to 23 weeks in some cases.
2. Check whether you have had the whooping cough vaccine for this pregnancy (England), or ask at which visit it is planned (US).
3. Set up your bed for side sleeping, with a pillow between your knees and another under your bump.
4. Start pelvic floor exercises, which help prevent leaks when you cough, laugh or sneeze.
5. Lower your risk of falls with firm, comfortable shoes and extra care when walking.

> Get checked without waiting for your next appointment if you have vaginal bleeding, fluid leaking from the vagina, severe or persistent abdominal pain, cramps or contractions at regular intervals, a fever, pain or burning when you pee, a severe headache that does not go away, changes in your vision, severe pain just below the ribs or sudden swelling of your face, hands or legs. The last four can be signs of high blood pressure in pregnancy, which can appear from 20 weeks. You also need to be assessed if you already feel your baby move every day and notice the movements have slowed or stopped, or if you have had a fall. Call your maternity unit or midwife (UK) or your ob-gyn (US), and call 999 or 911 in an emergency.
