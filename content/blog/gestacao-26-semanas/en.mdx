**Quick answer:** at 26 weeks you are in the 6th month and two weeks away from the 3rd trimester. Your baby is **about 35.6 cm** long from head to heel and weighs **about 900 g**, roughly the size of a head of lettuce. The eyes start to open around now, and this is the period of the test that looks for gestational diabetes.

"Twenty-six weeks" means twenty-six completed weeks, from 26 weeks and 0 days to 26 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. The due date is set at 40 weeks, so there are about 14 to go. Converting to months is approximate, which is why your care team always talks in weeks.

## How your baby is doing at 26 weeks

The length comes from the NHS week-by-week guide, and the weight is the median on the World Health Organization fetal growth charts, estimated by ultrasound. Both are averages, and a scan done now can show different numbers without anything being wrong. Your midwife or doctor is the one who reads the report.

According to the NHS, the eyes open for the first time around now, and the next step is learning to blink. The final eye colour takes a while: it depends on the parents' genes and, by the same guide, has usually settled by around age 3.

The lungs are developing too. ACOG, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, describes them starting to make surfactant between 25 and 28 weeks, the substance that later helps the lungs fill with air. Hearing, by the same source, begins to form much earlier, between 13 and 16 weeks.

Movements are stronger, and you start to recognise a pattern of busy times and quiet times. There is no set number of kicks per day. What counts is noticing when your baby's pattern changes, and getting checked without delay if the movements slow down.

A baby born this week is extremely preterm, the World Health Organization's term for birth before 28 weeks. Survival is possible, but the baby needs care in a neonatal unit, and every extra week in the womb lowers the risks. The signs of preterm labour are listed further down and need a quick response.

## What changes in your body

The NHS describes this stage as one of tiredness and a certain clumsiness. Your bump shifts your centre of gravity and affects your balance. Standing up quickly from a bed or chair can make you feel faint, so get up slowly and accept a hand when it is offered.

The most common symptoms now:

- Swelling in your feet, ankles and hands, worse at the end of the day and in hot weather. Your body holds more water, and the womb slows the blood coming back from your legs. It helps to avoid standing for long periods, rest with your feet up, wear comfortable shoes and go for walks. Swelling that suddenly gets worse in your face, hands or feet is a warning sign.
- Leg cramps, mostly at night. Pulling your toes up towards your shin and rubbing the muscle usually eases them.
- Backache and pains at the sides of your bump, which usually come from the ligaments that support the womb stretching. Pain that is severe, does not go away or comes with a fever needs to be checked.
- A bump that tightens for a few seconds and then relaxes. These are practice contractions, usually painless, with no rhythm and no build-up. They become more frequent towards the end of pregnancy.
- Leaking a little pee when you cough, sneeze or laugh. Pelvic floor exercises help. Pain or burning when you pee can mean a urine infection and needs to be checked. If you cannot tell whether it is pee or your waters, get examined.
- Heartburn, constipation, poor sleep and feeling hot.
- Sore breasts, which in some women already leak a little colostrum, the first milk.

Not every pregnant woman gets all of these, and how strong they are varies from one pregnancy to the next.

## Prenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ by country. In England, the NHS plans an appointment at 25 weeks if this is your first baby and one at 28 weeks for everyone, with at least 10 appointments for a first baby and 7 if you have given birth before. In the US, ask your ob-gyn or midwife how often you will be seen from here on. Twin and higher-risk pregnancies follow their own schedule. Blood pressure gets particular attention in the second half of pregnancy, because pre-eclampsia can appear after 20 weeks.

The test at this stage is the one for gestational diabetes, between 24 and 28 weeks. In the US, ACOG lists glucose screening for everyone in that window. In England, the NHS offers a glucose tolerance test only to women with a higher chance of the condition. Gestational diabetes usually causes no symptoms, which is why it tends to be found only by testing. Your care team explains the result. If it is raised, they set out the follow-up, and most women with gestational diabetes go on to have an otherwise normal pregnancy and a healthy baby.

In England, the 28-week appointment also includes a blood test and, when your baby is at risk of rhesus disease, the offer of a preventive injection.

On vaccines, the dates differ too. For whooping cough, the NHS usually offers the vaccine at around 20 weeks, it can be given from 16 weeks, and it is best had before 32 weeks. In the US, the CDC and ACOG recommend it in every pregnancy between 27 and 36 weeks, as early in that window as possible. The RSV vaccine protects the baby from bronchiolitis in the first months. The NHS offers it from 28 weeks in every pregnancy, and the CDC recommends it between 32 and 36 weeks during the RSV season. The flu vaccine can be given at any stage of pregnancy.

## What to do this week

1. Ask whether a glucose test is planned for you and when. The window runs to 28 weeks.
2. Check your vaccines. In England, ask about whooping cough if you have not had it and about RSV at your 28-week appointment. In the US, ask your provider to schedule the whooping cough vaccine from 27 weeks.
3. Notice the times of day your baby tends to move most, so you can tell if that rhythm changes.
4. Start or keep up your pelvic floor exercises. Your midwife, doctor or a pelvic health physiotherapist can teach you the technique.
5. Ask about antenatal classes and when they start. In the UK, the NHS notes that you may be able to claim Maternity Allowance from this week if you do not qualify for Statutory Maternity Pay.

> Get medical help straight away, without waiting for your next appointment, if you have vaginal bleeding, fluid leaking from your vagina, contractions that are painful or come at regular intervals, period-type pains, pressure in your pelvis or a constant low backache that is unusual for you. Before 37 weeks, these can be signs of preterm labour. The same goes for a severe headache that does not go away, blurred vision or flashing lights, sudden swelling of your face, hands or feet, severe pain just below your ribs, a fever and pain when you pee. If your baby is moving less than usual or the pattern of movements has changed, call immediately, even in the middle of the night. Call your maternity unit or midwife (UK) or your ob-gyn (US). For heavy bleeding, a seizure, fainting or difficulty breathing, call 999 or 911.
