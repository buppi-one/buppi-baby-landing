**Quick answer:** at 34 weeks you are in the 8th month, in the 3rd trimester. Your baby is **about 45 cm** long from head to heel and weighs **about 2.3 kg**, roughly the size of a cantaloupe melon. Many babies turn head down around now, and it is a good week to get things ready for the trip to hospital.

"Thirty-four weeks" means 34 completed weeks, from 34 weeks and 0 days to 34 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. The due date is set at 40 weeks, so there are about 6 to go.

## How your baby is doing at 34 weeks

The length comes from the NHS guide, and the weight is the median of the World Health Organization fetal growth charts. On those charts, 2 in 10 babies fall outside the 2.0 kg to 2.7 kg range, so a different number on a scan report does not signal a problem by itself. Your midwife or doctor follows growth at each visit and decides whether and when another scan is needed.

The NHS describes the baby curled up in the womb, with the legs bent up towards the chest. There is not much room, but the movements carry on, and your bump may change shape when the baby shifts. Movements become more frequent until about 32 weeks and then stay the same, without dropping off late in pregnancy. If your baby starts moving less, that needs checking.

Many babies turn head down between 33 and 36 weeks. If yours is still bottom first (breech) or lying sideways, there is time: according to the NHS, most babies are head down by the time labour begins. Position starts to matter in the conversation about birth at around 36 weeks. In the UK, a woman whose baby is still breech at that point is usually offered an external cephalic version, in which a professional tries to turn the baby by pressing on the abdomen. If that does not work, the team discusses vaginal birth or caesarean with her.

In boys, the testicles are moving down from the abdomen into the scrotum.

A baby born now is premature (moderate to late preterm in the World Health Organization classification), which is why signs of labour at this stage need immediate assessment.

## What changes in your body

Some symptoms may ease quite suddenly. According to the NHS, this happens when the baby moves head down into the pelvis, a process called engagement that takes place in stages. The drop frees up space higher in your abdomen: breathlessness tends to ease, and heartburn may improve because there is less pressure on your stomach. On the other hand, you need to pee more often and walking gets harder. Engagement does not announce the birth, which is probably still several weeks away.

Your breasts may be much bigger and leak small amounts of yellowish colostrum, the first milk, which is rich in antibodies.

Your bump goes hard now and then. The NHS describes Braxton Hicks contractions as painless, and they are irregular, short and spaced out. Before 37 weeks, anything outside that pattern needs checking at your maternity unit: regular or frequent contractions, period-type pains, pressure in your pelvis, a constant ache low in your back, a gush or trickle of fluid. If you cannot tell whether the fluid is your waters or urine, get checked.

Other complaints the NHS lists for this week:

- Poor sleep, backache and leg cramps.
- Constipation, piles, feeling hot and dizziness.
- Swollen hands and feet. Swelling that comes on suddenly, especially with a severe headache or vision changes, needs immediate assessment.
- Urine infections. Pain or burning when you pee is a reason to contact your midwife or doctor.

How strong these are varies from one woman to another, and severe pain should not wait for the next appointment.

For sleep, the NHS recommends lying on your side, without naming one, because the position helps blood flow to the placenta and tends to spare your back.

## Antenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ by country, so here is what applies in England and what applies in the US.

In England, 34 weeks is one of the scheduled NHS antenatal appointments. The midwife checks your blood pressure, tests your urine, measures your bump, asks about your baby's movements and your mental health, and talks through preparing for the birth, your preferences and how to tell when labour has started. The next appointment is at 36 weeks, when the midwife feels your bump to check the baby's position. The RSV vaccine, which helps protect your baby from bronchiolitis, is offered from 28 weeks in every pregnancy and can still be given later, up to labour. The whooping cough vaccine is best had before 32 weeks but can also be given later, so ask if you have missed it. Group B strep is not routinely tested for in NHS pregnancy care.

In the US, the CDC and ACOG recommend the whooping cough (Tdap) vaccine in every pregnancy between 27 and 36 weeks. The maternal RSV vaccine is a single dose given between 32 weeks and 36 weeks and 6 days. It is seasonal and is not repeated in later pregnancies, so ask your provider whether it applies to your dates. ACOG recommends screening all pregnant women for group B strep between 36 weeks and 37 weeks and 6 days, so expect the swab at one of your next visits.

The flu vaccine is recommended during flu season, at any stage of pregnancy, in both countries.

## What to do this week

1. Pack your hospital bag, with your maternity notes or insurance documents on top, and work out how you will get there.
2. Write down your preferences for the birth and decide who will be with you. In England the 34-week appointment covers this. In the US, bring it up at your next visit.
3. Check your vaccines: RSV and whooping cough in England, Tdap and, in season, RSV in the US.
4. If you work in the UK and want to change your maternity leave start date, the NHS notes that your employer needs at least 28 days' notice.
5. Ask about antenatal classes if you have not booked any, and keep up your pelvic floor exercises. The NHS describes the movement as tightening the muscles you would use to stop a pee midflow.
6. Notice your baby's movements every day. There is no set number of kicks to reach, so your baby's usual pattern is the reference. If it changes or you feel unsure, call without waiting.

> Get checked without waiting for your next appointment if you have vaginal bleeding, fluid leaking from your vagina, regular contractions or strong pressure low in your belly, a fever, or pain or burning when you pee. Call straight away, without waiting until the next day, if your baby is moving less than usual or the pattern of movements changes. A severe headache that does not go away, blurred vision or flashing lights, severe pain just below the ribs and sudden swelling of the face, hands or legs are signs of high blood pressure in pregnancy and need immediate assessment. Call your midwife or maternity unit in the UK, or your ob-gyn in the US. For a seizure, fainting, severe breathlessness, chest pain or heavy bleeding, call 999 in the UK or 911 in the US.
