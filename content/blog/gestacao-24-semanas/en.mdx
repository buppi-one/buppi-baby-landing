**Quick answer:** at 24 weeks you are in the 6th month and the 2nd trimester. Your baby is **about 30 cm** from head to heel and weighs **around 660 g**, roughly the size of a corn on the cob. The window for the glucose test opens now and runs until 28 weeks.

"Twenty-four weeks" means 24 completed weeks, from 24 weeks and 0 days to 24 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. The due date is set at 40 weeks, so there are about 16 to go. Converting weeks to months is approximate, which is why your care team works in weeks.

## How your baby is developing at 24 weeks

The length, measured from head to heel, comes from the NHS week-by-week guide. The weight is the median on the WHO fetal growth charts. Healthy babies sit above or below these averages, and the reference for yours is the estimate from your scan.

According to the NHS, the body is already in proportion, just much smaller than that of a baby who has spent longer in the womb. ACOG's guide to fetal development places stronger kicks and the sucking reflex between 21 and 24 weeks. The lungs are still far from ready: in the same guide, they start making surfactant, the substance that keeps the air sacs open during breathing, between 25 and 28 weeks.

The NHS describes this as the week your baby is considered viable: there is a chance of survival outside the womb with the support of a neonatal unit. The WHO classes a birth before 28 weeks as extremely preterm. ACOG says survival rises between 23 and 25 weeks and that serious disability is still frequent in that range.

First movements are usually felt between 16 and 24 weeks. If you have reached 24 weeks and never felt your baby move, the NHS advises telling your midwife. Do it this week instead of waiting for your next appointment.

## What changes in your body

How fast the bump grows varies a lot from one woman to another, and the NHS notes that some can barely see theirs at this stage. Many women feel hungrier. The same guide says there is no need to eat more until the third trimester. Advice for your own case, including a twin pregnancy or being underweight, comes from your care team.

Pregnancy hormones loosen your ligaments and muscles, and the baby presses on the structures around it. That brings pain around the ribs and back, and twinges at the sides of the bump as the womb grows. Other common symptoms this week:

- Heartburn and indigestion, constipation and piles.
- Leg cramps, mostly at night.
- Mild swelling in your feet and hands. Sudden swelling, especially in your face and hands, is a warning sign.
- Feeling hot, dizziness and trouble sleeping.
- Bleeding gums and nosebleeds.
- Dark patches on your face, greasier skin and stretch marks.

Urine infections and vaginal infections become more frequent in pregnancy and need treatment. Pain or burning when you pee should be checked the same day.

Some women start to notice their bump going hard now and then. These are practice contractions, more common towards the end of pregnancy. They are usually painless, short and far apart, and they do not settle into a rhythm or get stronger. Contractions at regular intervals before 37 weeks need to be assessed.

## Prenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ by country, so here is what applies in England and what applies in the US.

In England, routine appointments fall at 16 and 28 weeks, with an extra one at 25 weeks if this is your first baby. In the US, visit timing is set with your ob-gyn or midwife.

The main test at this stage checks for gestational diabetes, which does not usually cause symptoms and so only shows up on a blood test. In England, the NHS offers an oral glucose tolerance test between 24 and 28 weeks only to women with a higher chance of the condition, which your midwife assesses at the booking appointment. In the US, ACOG lists glucose screening at 24 to 28 weeks as a routine test, done earlier if you have risk factors. For the NHS test you fast for 8 to 10 hours (water is usually allowed, but check with the hospital), have a blood sample taken, drink a glucose drink and give another sample after resting for 2 hours. If you already have a diabetes diagnosis, your team sets your follow-up and may not need this test.

According to the NHS, most women with gestational diabetes have healthy babies, and the risks fall when it is found early and well managed. Left unmanaged, the baby can grow larger than expected, which makes the birth harder, and can have low blood sugar after birth. A raised screening result may need a second test to confirm it. Once gestational diabetes is confirmed, care starts with changes to diet, physical activity and blood sugar monitoring, and some women also need medicine, which the doctor decides.

In England the 20-week screening scan is usually done between 18 and 21 weeks and can be completed up to 23 weeks in some cases. If you have not had it, tell your midwife this week.

On vaccines, the NHS usually offers the whooping cough vaccine around week 20, can give it from week 16 and advises having it before 32 weeks. In the US, the CDC and ACOG recommend it between 27 and 36 weeks. The flu vaccine is recommended at any stage. The RSV vaccine comes later: from 28 weeks in England, and between 32 and 36 weeks in the US, where it is seasonal.

## What to do this week

1. Ask at your next appointment whether a glucose test is planned for you, where it is done and how to prepare. The window runs from 24 to 28 weeks.
2. Check whether you have had the whooping cough vaccine. In England, if you have not, speak to your midwife or GP. In the US, ask when it is planned.
3. Ask where you are expected to give birth and whether that hospital has a neonatal unit. In the UK, if you have not told your employer yet, the NHS notes that the latest you can leave it is 15 weeks before the due date, which is about now.
4. Add pelvic floor exercises to your routine. According to the NHS, they help prevent leaks when you cough or laugh. The general target for physical activity is still about 150 minutes of moderate exercise a week, as long as your care team has not advised against it.
5. Keep away from alcohol and smoking and keep to the supplements your midwife or doctor has advised. Do not start or stop any medicine or supplement on your own.

> Call your maternity unit or midwife (UK) or your ob-gyn (US) straight away, without waiting for your next appointment, if you have contractions or tightenings at regular intervals, period-type pains, backache that is unusual for you, fluid leaking from your vagina or bleeding. Before 37 weeks these are signs of premature labour. Call straight away too if you have a severe headache that will not go away, blurred vision or flashing lights, strong pain in the upper part of your tummy, or sudden swelling of your face, hands or legs. These can point to pre-eclampsia, a complication involving high blood pressure that appears after 20 weeks. The same applies if your baby, whose movements you have been feeling, moves much less or stops moving. A high temperature or pain when you pee should be checked the same day. A seizure, fainting, difficulty breathing or heavy bleeding is an emergency: call 999 or 911.
