**Quick answer:** at 11 weeks you are in the 3rd month and near the end of the 1st trimester. Your baby is **about 41 mm** from head to bottom, roughly the size of a fig. The placenta is gradually taking over the job of feeding your baby, and this is the time of the first-trimester scan, the one that can include the nuchal translucency measurement.

"Eleven weeks" means eleven completed weeks, from 11 weeks and 0 days to 11 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. The due date is set at 40 weeks, so there are about 29 to go. Converting weeks to months is approximate, which is why your care team talks in weeks.

## Your baby at 11 weeks

The figure of about 41 mm comes from the NHS week-by-week guide and runs from the top of the head to the bottom, because the legs are folded up. The head is still large compared with the rest of the body, but the body is now growing fast.

The fingers and toes are separating, the ears are there in miniature and the fingernails are starting to form. Between 9 and 12 weeks the eyelids also form and the kidneys begin to make urine, according to the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG).

Your baby is already moving and kicking in the womb. They are still too small for you to notice, and first movements are usually only felt several weeks from now.

In the early weeks your baby was fed mainly by the yolk sac, a small pouch attached to the embryo. The placenta, which has been forming since the start, gradually takes over around this stage. It delivers nutrients and oxygen through the umbilical cord and carries waste away. The NHS notes that the hormones involved in this switch can leave you more tired and more emotional.

## What is changing in your body

The amount of blood in circulation rises a lot in pregnancy, and by now it can be up to 50% more than before. That extra blood supplies the womb and, according to the NHS, can also make you feel hot, sweaty and dizzy. Light clothes, water within reach and breaks to rest help. Dizziness with fainting or with repeated vomiting needs to be checked.

For some women the lower belly starts to round out and clothes get tight at the waist, while for others nothing shows yet. The muscles and ligaments around the womb are stretching, which can cause twinges or a discomfort like mild period cramps. Pain that is severe or does not go away needs to be assessed.

Other common symptoms at this stage:

- Nausea. It usually starts before 9 weeks and for most women is gone by about 14 weeks. For some it lasts longer.
- A sharper sense of smell, a metallic taste in the mouth and new likes or dislikes for food.
- Sore breasts, tiredness and mood swings.
- Heartburn, wind and bloating.
- Headaches, which are common in early pregnancy and tend to improve as the weeks go on. A severe headache, one that does not ease or one that comes with changes in your vision needs medical attention.
- Greasier, spotty skin, darker patches on the face and thicker, shinier hair.
- A white, milky discharge, which is expected in pregnancy. If it turns yellowish or greenish, smells strong or itches, mention it at your appointment.
- Light spotting can happen in early pregnancy, but any bleeding, even light and painless, should be checked.

Not everyone gets all of these, and how strong they are varies from one pregnancy to the next.

For nausea, eating small amounts often, choosing cold food when the smell of hot meals bothers you and sipping fluids usually help. A small share of pregnant women, up to 3%, have the severe form, hyperemesis gravidarum, which can cause dehydration and needs treatment. Any medicine for sickness or headaches should only be taken on the advice of the professional looking after your pregnancy.

## Antenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ between countries, so here is what applies in England and what is known for the US.

In England, the NHS booking appointment with a midwife usually takes place between 8 and 12 weeks. If you have not arranged it yet, this is the week to do it. You can expect around 10 appointments if this is your first baby, around 7 if you have given birth before, and 2 routine ultrasound scans. Around now you are offered blood, urine and blood pressure checks, along with screening for infectious diseases. The flu vaccine is recommended at any stage of pregnancy, and the whooping cough vaccine comes later, in the second trimester.

The first NHS scan is the dating scan, offered at around 10 to 14 weeks. It shows how many weeks pregnant you are, whether you are expecting more than one baby, that the baby is growing in the right place and how the baby is developing. If you have agreed to screening for Down's syndrome and the scan falls between 10 and 14 weeks, it also includes the combined test: a blood test plus a measurement of the fluid at the back of the baby's neck, the nuchal translucency. The result is an estimate of chance. A higher-chance result leads to a conversation about further tests and does not amount to a diagnosis. After 14 weeks the combined test is no longer offered, and a different blood test is used between 14 and 20 weeks.

In the US, your ob-gyn or midwife sets the visit schedule, and it varies between practices. International ultrasound guidance (ISUOG) places the first-trimester scan between 11 and 14 weeks, so ask your provider now whether and when it will be booked, and which screening options your practice offers.

A scan at this stage does not usually show the baby's sex reliably. In England you may be asked at the 20-week scan whether you would like to know.

## What to do this week

1. Arrange your first appointment if you have not had it. In England the booking appointment usually happens between 8 and 12 weeks.
2. Ask about your first-trimester scan. In England the combined test is only done between 10 and 14 weeks, so the scan has to be booked inside that window.
3. Take any test results and your vaccination record to the appointment, and check with your midwife or doctor which supplements they recommend for you.
4. Watch what goes on the plate: meat cooked all the way through, because of the risk of toxoplasmosis, pasteurised milk and dairy, and fruit and vegetables washed well. Leave out alcohol and smoking, and cut down on caffeine: ask your midwife or doctor what the daily limit is.
5. Keep moving. The general advice is to build up to about 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, starting with 10-minute walks. Some situations call for restrictions, so check with your care team what suits you.

> Get medical help without waiting for your next appointment if you have vaginal bleeding, severe or persistent abdominal pain, a high temperature, pain or burning when you pee, fainting or a severe headache that does not ease. The same applies if you are vomiting and cannot keep food or fluids down for 24 hours, if your urine is very dark or you have not peed in more than 8 hours, if you feel very weak or dizzy when standing up or if you are losing weight. Contact your maternity unit or midwife in the UK, or your ob-gyn in the US. In an emergency call 999 in the UK or 911 in the US.
