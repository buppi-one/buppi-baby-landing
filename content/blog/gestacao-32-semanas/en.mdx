**Quick answer:** at 32 weeks you are starting the 8th month, in the 3rd trimester. Your baby is **about 42.4 cm** long from head to heel and weighs **about 1.9 kg**, roughly the size of a small pineapple. Over the next few weeks your baby mostly puts on fat, and in the US this is the week the window for the maternal RSV vaccine opens.

"Thirty-two weeks" means 32 completed weeks, from 32 weeks and 0 days to 32 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. The due date is set at 40 weeks, so there are about 8 to go. Converting to months is approximate, which is why your care team talks in weeks.

## How your baby is developing at 32 weeks

The length comes from the NHS week-by-week guide, and the weight is the median of the World Health Organization fetal growth charts. Both are reference values. A weight estimated on a scan has a margin of error, and your care team follows your baby's growth from one check to the next.

According to the NHS, your baby is fully formed and the job of the next few weeks is to put on weight. The fat building up under the skin will help your baby keep a steady body temperature after birth.

Many babies are already head down by now. If yours is still bottom down or lying sideways, there is time to turn. The NHS says that it is around 36 weeks that your midwife or doctor will talk to you about what can be done if your baby has not turned.

Women at risk of preterm birth often ask what would happen if the baby came now. The World Health Organization classes birth from 32 weeks to before 37 completed weeks as moderate to late preterm. The baby usually needs to stay in a neonatal unit, and, according to the WHO, in high-income countries almost all survive, although how each baby does depends on the circumstances of the birth. Every extra week in the womb still counts for the lungs and for weight gain.

## What is changing in your body

Your womb now takes up much of your abdomen and pushes against your stomach and bowel. That explains the heartburn, feeling full after a small meal and constipation, which tend to get worse late in pregnancy. Eating smaller portions more often, drinking water through the day and adding fibre to your meals usually helps. Take medicine for heartburn or constipation only on the advice of your midwife or doctor.

The NHS estimates a gain of around 450 g a week over the next four weeks, part of it your baby's. That is an average, and the expected gain varies with your weight before pregnancy. Your care team is the reference for what is right for you.

Other common symptoms at 32 weeks:

- A bump that tightens for a few moments and then relaxes. These are practice contractions, or Braxton Hicks: they usually do not hurt, are irregular and do not get stronger or closer together over time.
- A waddling walk, backache and pains at the side of your bump, because of the weight at the front and your expanding womb.
- Feeling hot and tired, and broken sleep.
- Swollen feet and hands at the end of the day. Swelling that comes on suddenly, or in your face, is on the warning list further down.
- Leg cramps, piles and gums that bleed when you brush.
- Breasts that leak a little colostrum, the first milk. Not leaking at all is just as normal and says nothing about how breastfeeding will go.

Your baby's movements feel different now. With less room, somersaults give way to pushes and slower movements, but you should not feel your baby less often: according to the RCOG, the UK college of obstetricians, the number of movements increases until around 32 weeks and then stays about the same until birth. There is no set number of movements per day, and the reference is your own baby's pattern. Your baby's sleep periods, when they stay still, are rarely longer than 90 minutes.

## Antenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ by country, so here is what applies in England and what applies in the US.

In England, the NHS schedules appointments at 31 weeks if this is your first baby, then at 34 and 36 weeks for everyone. The NHS offers two routine scans, both earlier in pregnancy, so a third-trimester scan is arranged only when there is a reason for one. On vaccines, the NHS advises having the whooping cough vaccine before week 33. You can still have it later, but your baby is less likely to get the protection from you, so speak to your midwife or GP this week if you have not had it. The RSV vaccine, which lowers your baby's risk of bronchiolitis, is usually offered around week 28 and can be given later, right up until labour.

In the US, the CDC recommends one dose of the maternal RSV vaccine between 32 weeks and 36 weeks and 6 days, so the window opens this week. It is seasonal and is not repeated in later pregnancies, so ask your provider whether it applies to your dates. The CDC and ACOG recommend the whooping cough (Tdap) vaccine in every pregnancy between 27 and 36 weeks, as early in that window as possible. If you have not had it, ask now. Screening for group B strep comes later, between 36 and 38 weeks.

In both countries the flu vaccine is given during flu season, at any stage of pregnancy.

## What to do this week

1. Check your vaccines: whooping cough if you are in England and have not had it, RSV and Tdap if you are in the US.
2. Settle where you would like to give birth and visit if you can. The NHS suggests asking as many questions as you like, and reminds you that you can change your mind.
3. Notice your baby's pattern of movements through the day, such as the times when they tend to be most active.
4. Start looking into who will care for your baby after birth. It is easier to choose now than with a newborn at home. See [how to choose a pediatrician](/en/blog/how-to-choose-a-pediatrician/).
5. If unrequested advice about your bump and the birth is wearing you down, keep a short answer ready, such as "thank you, I'm following what my midwife told me". Anxiety or low mood that does not lift is something to raise at your appointment.

> Get checked without waiting for your next appointment as soon as you notice your baby moving less than usual or a change in the usual pattern. Go as well if you have vaginal bleeding, fluid leaking from your vagina (if you cannot tell whether it is your waters, pee or discharge, get assessed), regular or painful contractions, period-type pains or a constant ache low in your back, pressure in your pelvis, a severe headache that does not go away, blurred vision or flashing lights, severe pain just below the ribs, sudden swelling of the face, hands or legs, a fever, or pain when you pee. With any of these signs, call your midwife or maternity unit straight away in the UK, or your ob-gyn in the US. For heavy bleeding, a seizure, fainting or breathlessness, call 999 in the UK or 911 in the US.
