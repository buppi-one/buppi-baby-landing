**Quick answer:** at 14 weeks you enter the 2nd trimester and the 4th month of pregnancy. Your baby is **about 8.5 cm** from head to bottom and weighs **about 90 g**, roughly the size of a lemon. For most women the sickness has gone or is fading, and energy starts to come back.

"Fourteen weeks" means fourteen completed weeks, from 14 weeks and 0 days to 14 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. The due date is set at 40 weeks, so there are about 26 to go. Converting weeks to months is approximate, which is why your care team always talks in weeks.

## How your baby is developing at 14 weeks

At this stage length is measured from head to bottom, because the legs are curled up. The NHS week-by-week guide puts it at about 8.5 cm. The head is rounder and more in proportion with the rest of the body.

Weight appears in this series from now on because the World Health Organization fetal growth charts start at 14 weeks. The figure of about 90 g is the median on those charts, estimated by ultrasound. Your scan report has your own baby's measurements, and your care team interprets them.

Your baby already swallows small amounts of amniotic fluid, which pass into the stomach. The kidneys are working and send fluid back out as urine.

There is plenty of kicking and turning, and it shows on a scan. You will probably not feel any of it yet. Mothers usually start to notice movements between 16 and 24 weeks, and in a first pregnancy often only after 20. At an appointment, your midwife or doctor may already be able to hear the heartbeat with a handheld monitor placed on your tummy.

Everything your baby receives comes through the placenta, which is attached to the wall of the womb and linked to the baby by the umbilical cord. It delivers oxygen and nutrients, makes hormones and removes waste such as carbon dioxide. Your blood and your baby's come very close there but run in separate circuits.

## What is changing in your body

Pregnancy sickness usually starts before 9 weeks and, for most women, goes away by around 14. For some it lasts longer, which is worth mentioning at your next appointment. As it eases, appetite tends to return. The NHS points out that, in general, your baby does not need extra calories at this stage, so what matters now is variety on your plate. Your midwife or doctor follows your weight over time and gives advice that fits you.

The second trimester is also when a bump starts to show. Common symptoms at this stage include:

- Twinges at the sides of the belly, from the ligaments that support the growing womb being stretched. Pain that is severe or does not ease needs checking.
- Swollen gums that bleed easily and the occasional nosebleed.
- Heartburn, constipation and feeling bloated.
- Headaches and dizziness, especially when you stand up quickly. A severe headache, or one that does not get better, should be checked.
- Breasts that are still tender. Some women notice yellowish stains in their bra, which are usually colostrum, the first milk.
- Darker patches on the face, greasier skin and thicker, shinier hair.
- Milky white vaginal discharge without a strong smell. If it turns yellow or green, smells strong or itches, book an appointment.

Not every woman gets all of these, and intensity changes from one pregnancy to the next.

A common question is how to sleep. For now you can sleep in whatever position is comfortable, including on your front for as long as your bump allows. The advice to go to sleep on your side applies from 28 weeks.

Pregnancy can also put a strain on mood and on relationships. If you feel you are not coping, talk to your midwife or doctor.

## Antenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ by country, so here is what applies in England and what applies in the US.

In England, the first midwife (booking) appointment happens at 8 to 12 weeks, and the NHS plans at least 10 appointments for a first baby and at least 7 if you have given birth before. The dating scan, often called the 12-week scan, is offered at around 10 to 14 weeks, and the screening test for Down's, Edwards' and Patau's syndromes can be done with it up to 14 weeks and 1 day, so that window closes at the very start of this week. If you have not had your booking appointment or this scan, contact your midwife or maternity unit now, and they will tell you which scan and screening can still be offered. The next routine appointment is at 16 weeks, and the 20-week screening scan usually takes place between 18 and 21 weeks.

In the US, your ob-gyn or midwife sets the visit schedule and the timing of scans. International ultrasound guidance (ISUOG) describes a first-trimester scan at 11 to 14 weeks and a routine mid-pregnancy scan between 18 and 24 weeks.

Vaccines also differ. The NHS can give the whooping cough vaccine from week 16 and usually offers it around week 20. In the US, the CDC and ACOG recommend it between 27 and 36 weeks, as early in that window as possible. Both repeat it in every pregnancy. The flu vaccine is recommended at any stage of pregnancy in both countries.

Further ahead is the glucose test at 24 to 28 weeks. In the US it is routine for all pregnant women. In England it is only for women with a higher chance of gestational diabetes.

## What to do this week

1. Check that nothing from early pregnancy is outstanding: booking appointment, blood tests and the dating scan. If something is, call your midwife or provider now.
2. In England, ask about the whooping cough vaccine at your 16-week appointment. In the US, ask at which visit it is planned.
3. Start pelvic floor exercises, which help prevent leaks when you cough, laugh or sneeze. Your midwife or a pelvic health physiotherapist can teach you the technique.
4. Use the returning energy to keep moving. The general recommendation is about 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, starting with 10-minute walks if you have not been active. Some situations call for restrictions, so check with your care team what suits you.
5. If you smoke or drink, ask your midwife or doctor for help to stop, and go easy on coffee and other drinks with caffeine.

> Get checked without waiting for your next appointment if you have vaginal bleeding, even a little, fluid leaking from the vagina, severe abdominal pain or cramps that do not ease, a fever, pain or burning when you pee, vomiting that stops you keeping fluids down, fainting, shortness of breath or chest pain. At this stage you are not yet expected to feel your baby move, so not noticing movements is not a warning sign. Call your midwife or maternity unit in the UK, or your ob-gyn in the US. In an emergency, call 999 (UK) or 911 (US).

Many women choose this moment to tell their employer. In the UK, once you have told them you have maternity rights, can attend antenatal appointments in paid work time and can ask for a risk assessment of your workplace.
