At 3 months, your baby's sleep is in the middle of a welcome transition: day and night are now clearer, nights start to stretch, and the baby stays awake and interactive for longer between naps. There's still no fixed schedule — but you can already feel a rhythm emerging. This guide covers how much a 3-month-old typically sleeps, how many naps they take, the slightly longer wake windows, a flexible schedule, and an important heads-up: the 4-month regression may be just around the corner.

## How much a 3-month-old sleeps

In total, a 3-month-old sleeps about **14 to 17 hours a day**, counting night sleep and naps — with the average edging toward **15 hours**. The difference from 2 months is subtle: sleep starts to **concentrate a bit more at night**, and daytime naps become slightly more defined.

The engine of this change is the **circadian rhythm**, the internal "clock" that separates day from night. It has matured over the past weeks, and at 3 months the baby produces more melatonin at night — which helps stretch the night blocks.

## Wake windows: how long awake

At 3 months, the **wake window** (how long the baby comfortably stays awake between sleeps) is usually **75 to 105 minutes** (about 1h15 to 1h45) — a bit more than at 2 months. Going much beyond it still tends to leave the baby **"overtired"**: crankier and, paradoxically, harder to get to sleep.

Keep **watching for sleepy cues** — yawning, a distant or "glazed" stare, going quieter or fussier, turning the face away from stimulation — rather than just timing it. Every baby has their own window.

## A flexible one-day schedule

There's no fixed agenda at 3 months, but an example helps you picture the rhythm of longer windows and still-frequent naps. Use it as an elastic reference, not a rule:

| Time (example) | What happens |
|---|---|
| 7:00 | Wakes and feeds |
| 8:15–9:30 | 1st nap |
| 11:00 | Feed, awake time |
| 11:15–12:30 | 2nd nap |
| 14:00 | Feed, awake time |
| 14:15–15:30 | 3rd nap |
| 16:30 | Feed, awake time |
| 17:00–17:30 | 4th nap (short) |
| 19:00 | Feed, awake time |
| 19:30–21:00 (varies) | Wind-down and start of night sleep |
| Overnight | 1–3 wake-ups to feed |

Notice: it's **3 to 5 naps**, of variable length, plus **night wakings to feed** (usually fewer than at 2 months). Short naps are still common — and after a short nap (20–30 minutes), the next wake window tends to be shorter, so the baby doesn't build up overtiredness. Bedtime still **isn't fixed** — it tends to be in the evening, variable, as the body clock settles in.

## Night sleep

At 3 months, many babies start doing a **longer night block** — 5 to 6 hours or more, usually early in the night. But **sleeping all night without waking still isn't the rule**: most keep waking 1 to 3 times to feed, and that's expected and healthy at this stage. Not every waking is hunger — growth spurts, discomfort, or simply seeking comfort also wake the baby — but at 3 months most still genuinely need to feed at night.

You can help the baby's clock settle in with simple cues:

- **During the day**: natural light, normal household noise, interaction and stimulation
- **At night**: low light, quiet voice, change the diaper only if needed (poop or a very full one, so you don't wake the baby for nothing), and feed in "silent and boring" mode, no play

## A light routine starts to fit

Unlike at 2 months, by 3 months you can introduce a **light, predictable routine** — without rigid timing. What usually helps:

- A **calm wind-down before sleep** (warm bath, feed, dim light, a song), in the same order — it doesn't have to be identical every day — that signals "time to sleep"
- **Respecting the wake windows** and sleepy cues to avoid "overtired"
- **Putting the baby down drowsy but still awake** when you can — it helps them get used to falling asleep in their own space. No pressure: it's a gentle introduction, not sleep training (still not recommended at this age)
- Keeping the **day–night difference** clearly marked

> **Safe sleep, always:** the baby should sleep **on their back**, on a **firm, flat mattress** (crib or bassinet), with **no** pillows, cushions, bumpers, or loose blankets, and **in the parents' room** (not the same bed) for at least the first 6 months. If you still swaddle, **stop at the first sign the baby is trying to roll** — which can appear as early as 2 months, so don't wait — because a swaddled baby who rolls onto their front is at risk of suffocation.

## The 4-month regression may be coming

If, near the end of the 3rd month or the start of the 4th, your baby's sleep **suddenly gets worse** — more wakings, short naps, trouble falling back asleep — it's probably the **4-month sleep regression**. Despite the name, it's a **step forward**: the baby's sleep matures and starts having cycles more like an adult's, with brief wakings between them. It's uncomfortable, but temporary (a few weeks) and part of development.

At this stage, what helps is **staying consistent** (light routine, safe sleep, wake windows) and being patient. It's not the time to create new habits you don't want to keep — it's the time to ride it out.

## When to talk to the pediatrician

Sleep at 3 months is still variable, but it's worth talking to the pediatrician if:

- The baby is **very sleepy**, hard to wake, and **doesn't wake to feed** enough
- There's **poor weight gain** or few wet diapers
- The baby **snores loudly**, has pauses in breathing, or seems to work hard to breathe while asleep
- The sleep difficulty comes with **extreme irritability**, fever, or other signs that something's off
- You are **exhausted to your limit** — asking for help is also caring for your baby

## What to expect next

Three months is a stage of gains: slightly longer nights, a more interactive baby, and a rhythm starting to show. Enjoy it — and if the 4-month regression arrives, remember it's temporary. The trick stays the same: go at your baby's pace, with wake windows, frequent naps, a light routine, and patience. Week by week, sleep gets a little more predictable.
