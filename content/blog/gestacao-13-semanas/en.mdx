**Quick answer:** at 13 weeks you are in the last week of the 1st trimester and of the 3rd month. Your baby is **about 7.4 cm** from head to bottom, roughly the size of a peach. The window for the first-trimester scan with screening is also about to close: in England the combined test can be done up to 14 weeks and 1 day.

"Thirteen weeks" means thirteen completed weeks, from 13 weeks and 0 days to 13 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. The due date is set at 40 weeks, so there are about 27 to go. Some guides already place week 13 in the 2nd trimester, the NHS week-by-week guide among them. This series follows ACOG, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists: the 1st trimester runs to 13 weeks and 6 days. Only the name of the trimester changes: the week count and the timing of appointments and tests stay the same.

## How your baby is developing at 13 weeks

The length is measured from head to bottom because the legs are curled up. The figure of about 7.4 cm is an average, and your own baby's measurement is the one on your scan report.

According to ACOG, by the end of week 12 the eyelids and fingernails have formed and the kidneys have started to make urine. Between 13 and 16 weeks, hearing begins to develop.

The NHS describes the ovaries or testes as fully formed inside, while the outside of the genitals is still being finished. That is why the question about the baby's sex usually waits for the second-trimester scan.

Your baby is already moving. The movements start out jerky and random and gradually look more deliberate. You cannot feel any of this yet: first movements are usually noticed between 16 and 24 weeks. The NHS adds that some babies already suck their thumb in the womb, which helps develop the sucking reflex they will need for feeding.

## What changes in your body

Your womb is growing upwards and outwards from the pelvis, and a small bump may start to show. For many women it only appears weeks later. According to the NHS, as the womb moves away from the bladder, the constant need to pee tends to ease.

Sickness often lets up around now. ACOG says that for most women it is gone by around week 14. For some it lasts longer: if yours is still there, mention it at your next appointment.

Other common symptoms at 13 weeks:

- Pains on the sides of your belly, caused by your expanding womb and known as round ligament pain. Pain that is severe or does not go away needs to be checked.
- Swollen, sore gums that bleed when you brush.
- Heartburn, which the NHS says becomes more common from 12 weeks. It often helps to eat smaller meals more often, not to eat in the three hours before bed, to cut down on fatty or spicy food and to sleep with your head and shoulders raised or on your left side.
- Constipation. Foods high in fibre (fruit, vegetables, beans, wholemeal bread and cereals), plenty of water and regular activity help prevent it.
- Headaches, dizziness when you stand up quickly, the occasional nosebleed and feeling thirstier than usual.
- Sore breasts, feeling hot and darker patches on your face.
- White, milky discharge, which is expected in pregnancy. If it turns yellowish or greenish, smells strong, itches or burns, book an appointment.

For heartburn, constipation or headaches, do not start any medicine on your own, including products sold as natural. Your midwife, doctor or pharmacist can tell you what is suitable, and anything already prescribed for you still applies.

## Prenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ by country, so here is what applies in England and what applies in the US.

In England, the first midwife appointment (the booking appointment) takes place between 8 and 12 weeks. If you have not had it yet, contact your GP surgery or local maternity service now. The NHS plans at least 10 appointments for a first baby and at least 7 if you have given birth before, plus 2 ultrasound scans. The next routine appointment after booking and the scan is at 16 weeks.

The test with a deadline is the dating scan, often called the 12-week scan, which the NHS offers at around 10 to 14 weeks. It works out how far along you are and your due date, shows whether you are expecting more than one baby and checks early development. This is also where the nuchal translucency, the fluid at the back of the baby's neck, is measured.

That measurement is part of the combined test, the screening for Down's, Edwards' and Patau's syndromes, and having it is your choice. It pairs the measurement with a blood test and can be done between 10 weeks and 14 weeks and 1 day. It estimates whether the chance of these conditions is higher or lower and does not give a diagnosis. If the chance comes back higher, your midwife or doctor explains which tests can confirm or rule it out, and whether to have them is also up to you.

If your scan falls after that window, the combined test is no longer offered. The NHS then offers a different blood test between 14 and 20 weeks, which screens for Down's syndrome and is not quite as accurate. The 20-week scan looks at the baby's anatomy again, without replacing the nuchal translucency measurement.

In the US, the timing of first-trimester visits, scans and screening is set with your ob-gyn or midwife, so ask this week what is planned and by when.

This is also a good moment to check whether the results of your first blood and urine tests are back, and to ask about anything you do not understand in them.

On vaccines, the NHS recommends the flu vaccine at any stage of pregnancy. The whooping cough vaccine comes later: the NHS usually offers it around week 20 and can give it from week 16, while in the US the CDC and ACOG recommend it between 27 and 36 weeks.

## What to do this week

1. If you have not had your first-trimester scan, call today. In England the combined test can only be done up to 14 weeks and 1 day. If there is no slot in time, tell your midwife. Missing the window does not end screening: a blood test is offered between 14 and 20 weeks.
2. Gather your test results and vaccination record to take to your next appointment.
3. Book a dental check-up. In the UK, NHS dental care is free during pregnancy and for a year after the birth. Use a soft toothbrush and, if you are sick, rinse your mouth with water to wash the acid off your teeth.
4. Step up the precautions against toxoplasmosis: if you have a cat, ask someone else to empty the litter tray, and avoid raw or undercooked meat.
5. If you plan to travel, talk first to your midwife or doctor, who can say whether the trip suits your pregnancy and the destination (areas with Zika call for caution). Journeys of more than four hours carry a small risk of blood clots: drink water and get up to walk around regularly.

> Get medical advice without waiting for your next appointment if you have vaginal bleeding, even a small amount, fluid leaking from your vagina, severe abdominal pain or cramps that do not go away, a high temperature, pain or burning when you pee, vomiting that stops you keeping fluids down, a severe headache that does not ease, fainting, shortness of breath or chest pain. Call your maternity unit or midwife (UK, or NHS 111 if you cannot reach them) or your ob-gyn (US). In an emergency, call 999 or 911.

Many women choose this moment to tell their employer: the first scan is behind them, and pregnancy loss is more common in the first three months.
