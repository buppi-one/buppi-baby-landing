You spend half an hour rocking, your baby finally goes out, and the moment their back touches the mattress their eyes open. It is one of the most common complaints of the first months, and the NHS, the UK's health service, treats the pattern as common. According to the NHS, a baby who wakes in the cot just wants to be near you.

This is only a problem if it is weighing on the family. If holding works and you are all rested, there is nothing to fix. If you can't eat, sleep or put your baby down, you can adjust two things: the transfer to the crib and, around 4 months, the habit of falling asleep in your arms.

## Why your baby wakes when you put them down

Your baby has gone from somewhere warm, with a familiar smell, your heartbeat and movement, to a surface that stays still. They notice the change, even asleep.

A study published in *Current Biology* in 2022 followed 21 babies with heart rate sensors and showed closely what happens at this moment. Three findings matter if you have a baby in your arms:

- **Walking calms better than sitting.** With crying babies, walking while carrying them reduced crying; sitting with them in your arms did not. About half fell asleep during 5 minutes of walking. In babies who were not crying, walking did not help them sleep.
- **The alert comes when your body starts to pull away.** What most stirred sleeping babies was the start of the separation, more than touching the mattress. In the transfers that worked, they calmed once the mother had finished pulling away.
- **Time asleep before the transfer affected the result.** Babies put in the crib too soon after falling asleep woke more often.

That led to the authors' suggestion: 5 minutes of walking with your baby in your arms and, once they are asleep, another 5 to 8 minutes sitting before putting them in the crib. The study is small, it does not guarantee the same result with every baby, and the authors themselves say the method needs confirmation. Even so, it was the most direct data we found on this exact scene, and trying it costs little.

## How to transfer your baby to the crib

The minutes come from the study above. The other details are practical tips that no study has compared, so keep only what works for your baby. If your baby is under 3 months and has a fever, don't try to soothe them first: seek care immediately.

1. **If your baby is crying, walk.** Use steady steps, without abrupt stops, for about 5 minutes. First check it isn't hunger or the diaper.
2. **After they fall asleep, wait 5 to 8 minutes.** Sit down if you like, but only if you are wide awake.
3. **Get everything ready beforehand.** A dark room and a crib that is already set up mean you won't have to adjust anything with your baby in your arms.
4. **Lower them slowly, with your body close to the mattress.** Many parents set down the bottom first and then the head, or the other way around. Use the order that disturbs your baby least.
5. **Release your hands gradually.** Rest one hand on their tummy for a few seconds and remove it without hurrying, always before you step away. Nothing stays on top of your baby after you leave.
6. **Don't pick them up at the first movement.** Since the babies in the study calmed after the mother finished pulling away, it is worth waiting a few seconds to see whether they go back to sleep on their own. If your baby fully wakes and cries, pick them up and try again later.

An overtired baby also tends to be harder to settle, so it helps to follow the [wake windows for their age](/en/blog/wake-windows-by-age/). And not every attempt will work: even in the study, transfers sometimes interrupted sleep and sometimes deepened it.

## What to avoid to keep them from waking

The internet is full of tricks to make the crib feel like your arms. Several go against the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) safe sleep recommendations, so leave them out:

- **Cushions, rolled towels, baby nests or pillows** against your baby. Nothing soft in the crib, because of the risk of suffocation and entrapment.
- **Clothing that smells like a parent inside the crib.** It is also a loose object.
- **An inclined mattress.** The surface must be firm and flat. The AAP considers any incline above 10 degrees unsafe.
- **Laying them on their side or tummy because that keeps them asleep.** For sleep, it is always on the back.
- **A hot water bottle or heavy clothing to warm the crib.** Overheating is on the list of risks too.
- **An infant carrier, swing or car seat as a regular nap spot.** If your baby fell asleep there, move them to a firm surface as soon as possible. A review of 348 deaths in sitting devices found car seats in 62.9% of cases, and more than half of the car seat deaths happened at home, nowhere near a car trip.

Don't give tea to make a baby sleep. Any medicine or supplement only with guidance from your pediatrician.

## The most important precaution: don't doze off with them on the sofa

When you are short on sleep, it is easy to nod off sitting there waiting out the minutes. That is when holding becomes dangerous. The AAP reports that the risk of sleep-related death can be up to 67 times higher when a baby sleeps with someone on a sofa, soft armchair or cushion.

If you notice you might fall asleep, put your baby in the crib, even if they wake. Ask someone else to take over if anyone else is home. For sleeping in the same bed, the guidelines and precautions are in our article on [bed sharing](/en/blog/bed-sharing-with-baby-risks-guidelines/).

If your baby falls asleep in your arms or in a sling, the adult needs to stay awake and attentive. These are not regular places to sleep, and the AAP advises moving them to a firm, flat surface, on their back, as soon as possible. In a sling, your baby's face must stay visible, with the chin off the chest.

## What to do at each age

| Age | What to expect | What to try |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 3 months | Sleeping in arms is very common, and sleep has no regular cycles yet | Holding as much as you like with an awake adult, calm transfers and attempts in the crib with no pressure |
| 4 to 5 months | Sleep cycles are more regular | Putting them in the crib drowsy, first for one nap or at night, and keeping a [short routine](/en/blog/how-to-build-a-sleep-routine/) |
| 6 to 12 months | May link being held with falling asleep and ask for the same when waking at night | Gradually reducing holding or, if the family decides, a structured method discussed with the pediatrician |

In the first months, there is no reason to hurry. A 2013 systematic review found no proven benefit from behavioral sleep interventions before 6 months, for either crying or postpartum depression, and warned of possible unintended effects when applied early to all babies, such as stopping breastfeeding too soon. A pacifier can help calm a baby, and the AAP links it to a lower risk of sudden infant death. If you breastfeed, the advice is to wait until breastfeeding is well established.

If your baby was sleeping well and suddenly got worse around 4 months, read about the [4-month sleep regression](/en/blog/4-month-sleep-regression/) before concluding that holding is to blame.

## How to ease off holding gradually

The AAP suggests, for babies 4 months and older, putting them in the crib when drowsy, and notes that those who always fall asleep in arms may have more trouble getting back to sleep overnight. The first step is usually to change when the holding ends:

1. **Start with one situation.** Many families choose the first nap or the start of the night. The other naps stay as they are for now.
2. **Keep the ritual and shorten the holding.** If your baby usually falls asleep after 15 minutes of rocking, stop at 12 and then at 10, always before they are fully out.
3. **Put them in the crib drowsy and stay close.** A hand on the tummy and a low voice can replace the rocking, and the hand comes off before you step away. If your baby cries hard, pick them up, calm them and try again.
4. **Remove one support at a time.** First the rocking, then the hand, then your presence next to the crib.
5. **If your baby falls asleep nursing,** move the feed earlier in the ritual: feed, change the diaper, listen to a song and then go to the crib. This doesn't require cutting feeds, and night hunger still needs a response.

We found no clinical trial testing this sequence for babies who sleep in arms. It is a gentle way to apply what the AAP recommends, and it can take weeks.

## What about methods where the baby cries a little?

Some families prefer a structured method, and there are more studies on these, done in older babies with sleep problems in general, where being held was not always the complaint:

- **Graduated waiting:** leaving your baby in the crib and responding at intervals that slowly get longer.
- **Bedtime fading:** putting them down later, close to the time they usually fall asleep, and then moving bedtime earlier.

In a clinical trial with 43 babies aged 6 to 16 months, both reduced the time it took to fall asleep. Graduated waiting also reduced awakenings. A year later, there was no difference in attachment or behavior compared with the control group. Another study followed to age 6 children whose families received this kind of guidance at 8 to 10 months, and likewise found no emotional harm and no lasting benefit. A review of 52 studies concluded that these techniques tend to work in the short and medium term.

Letting a baby cry remains each family's choice, and these figures apply to groups of babies, with no guarantee of how yours will react. Before starting, check with your pediatrician that your baby is gaining weight and that no health issue is behind it.

## When to seek help

> **Talk to your pediatrician if your baby:**
>
> - only calms when upright and cries a lot when laid down, or arches their body, seems to be in pain or vomits often
> - snores, pauses in breathing or breathes with effort while asleep
> - cries for a long time and nothing calms them, or suddenly changes behavior
> - isn't gaining weight or feeds poorly
>
> **Seek care immediately** if your baby is very limp, hard to wake, having trouble breathing, has bluish skin, or has a fever and is under 3 months old.

Talk about yourself too. If exhaustion or sadness is weighing on you, tell your doctor or the pediatrician. And if you reach your limit with the crying, put your baby on their back in the crib, leave the room for a few minutes and ask for help. Never shake a baby.

Logging naps for a few days helps you see which one is easiest to start with, and gives you concrete information for the appointment.
