**Quick answer:** at 16 weeks you are in the 4th month and the 2nd trimester. Your baby is **about 11.6 cm** from head to bottom and weighs **about 140 g**, roughly the size of an avocado. They can already make a fist and pull faces. Some women start to notice the first movements around now, but for most that comes in the next few weeks.

"Sixteen weeks" means sixteen completed weeks, from 16 weeks and 0 days to 16 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period (your care team may adjust that date after an early scan). The due date is set at 40 weeks, so there are about 24 to go. Converting weeks to months is approximate, which is why your care team talks in weeks.

## How your baby is doing at 16 weeks

The length of about 11.6 cm comes from the NHS week-by-week guide and runs from the top of the head to the bottom, because the legs are tucked up against the body. The weight of about 140 g is the median of the World Health Organization fetal growth charts, estimated by ultrasound. Your own baby's measurements are the ones in your scan report, read by the team looking after you.

The muscles of the face can already contract, so your baby may frown or seem to smile. These movements are random, because there is no control over those muscles yet. The nervous system keeps maturing, which lets the arms and legs move, and the hands can now close into fists. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) places the start of hearing development between 13 and 16 weeks, and it will keep maturing over the coming months.

Feeling those movements can still take a while. The NHS places the first movements a mother notices between 16 and 24 weeks, and the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists says most women first feel them at 18 to 20 weeks. In a first pregnancy it is common for this to happen only after 20 weeks. Women who have been pregnant before sometimes recognise the feeling earlier. At first it is like bubbles or a light flutter low in the belly, easy to mistake for wind. If you reach 24 weeks without noticing any movement, mention it at your appointment.

## What is changing in your body

This week the top of the womb is usually about halfway between the pubic bone and the navel. Many women start to show, but bump size varies a lot and says nothing about the size of the baby. Plenty of women have more energy in the second trimester, though every pregnancy is different.

For most women, nausea has eased by around 14 weeks, but for some it lasts longer. If it is still there, mention it at your appointment. Vomiting that stops you keeping down food or fluids needs same-day care, because of the risk of dehydration.

Common symptoms at 16 weeks include:

- Constipation, which can come with wind and a bloated feeling. High-fibre foods (fruit, vegetables, beans, lentils, wholemeal bread and cereals), plenty of water and daily movement usually help. If you think your pregnancy supplements have made it worse, talk to your care team before changing anything.
- Quick twinges low on the side of the belly when you stand up or turn over in bed. They usually come from the stretching of the ligaments that support the womb. Pain that is severe, does not pass or comes with bleeding, a fever or burning when you pee needs to be assessed.
- More vaginal discharge, clear or milky white, which is expected in pregnancy. A strong smell, a yellowish or greenish colour, itching or soreness are reasons to book an appointment. Watery fluid that trickles out or discharge with blood in it needs same-day care.
- Swollen gums that bleed when you brush, a blocked nose and the occasional nosebleed.
- Sore breasts, leg cramps, feeling hot and dizziness when you stand up quickly.

The NHS points out that there is no need to eat for two. Your care team keeps track of your weight and will tell you what is right for you.

## Antenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ by country, so here is what applies in England and what applies in the US.

In England, the NHS plans at least 10 appointments for a first baby and at least 7 if you have given birth before, and one of them falls at 16 weeks. At this visit your midwife or doctor goes over the results of the blood tests from your booking appointment, checks your blood pressure and tests your urine for protein. According to the NHS week 16 guide, you might also get to hear your baby's heartbeat for the first time. The next routine appointments are at 25 weeks (first baby) and 28 weeks.

The mid-pregnancy scan is getting close. The NHS offers the 20-week screening scan to everyone, usually between 18 and 21 weeks, and in some cases it can be done up to 23 weeks. In the US, the timing of the anatomy scan is set by your ob-gyn or midwife; international ultrasound guidance (ISUOG) places the routine mid-pregnancy scan between 18 and 24 weeks. If you want to know your baby's sex, you can usually ask during this scan.

The whooping cough vaccine is where the two countries differ most. In England it can be given from week 16, is usually offered around week 20 and works best before week 32, so your 16-week appointment is a good moment to ask about it. In the US, the CDC and ACOG recommend it between 27 and 36 weeks, as early in that window as possible. Both repeat it in every pregnancy. The flu vaccine is recommended at any stage of pregnancy in both countries.

## What to do this week

1. Check that your mid-pregnancy scan is booked. In England it is usually done between 18 and 21 weeks; in the US, ask your provider when yours is planned. If you cannot get a slot in time, tell your care team.
2. Ask about vaccines at your next visit: whooping cough if you are in England, flu in season in both countries, and when whooping cough is planned if you are in the US.
3. Start pelvic floor exercises. The NHS flags this stage as a good time, because they help prevent leaks when you laugh, cough or sneeze. Your midwife or a pelvic health physiotherapist can teach you the technique.
4. Keep moving. The general recommendation is about 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, starting with 10-minute walks if you have not been active. Some situations, such as bleeding, call for restrictions, so check with your care team what suits you.
5. Think about telling your employer, if you have not yet. In the UK, the NHS notes that once you do, you have maternity rights, can go to antenatal appointments during paid work time and can ask for a workplace risk assessment. Rules differ in the US, so check your employer's policy.

> Get checked without waiting for your next appointment if you have vaginal bleeding, fluid leaking from the vagina, severe or persistent abdominal pain, cramps that come at regular intervals, a fever, pain or burning when you pee, vomiting that stops you keeping fluids down, a severe headache that does not go away, shortness of breath, chest pain or feeling very unwell. Call your midwife or maternity unit in the UK, or your ob-gyn in the US, and call 999 or 911 in an emergency.
