**Quick answer:** at 37 weeks you are in the 9th month, in the third trimester. Your baby is **about 48.6 cm** long from head to heel and weighs **about 3 kg**, roughly the size of a bunch of Swiss chard. From this week a birth is no longer counted as premature, so the focus shifts to recognising the signs of labour.

"Thirty-seven weeks" means 37 completed weeks, from 37 weeks and 0 days to 37 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. Your due date is set at 40 weeks, so you have about 3 to go.

## Your baby at 37 weeks

The length comes from the NHS week-by-week guide and is measured from head to heel. The weight is the median of the World Health Organization fetal growth charts. On those charts 1 in 10 babies is below 2.5 kg and 1 in 10 above 3.4 kg, and a scan estimate outside that range does not mean on its own that something is wrong. Your antenatal team will interpret it.

At 37 completed weeks you reach what ACOG, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, calls early term, which lasts until 38 weeks and 6 days. Full term starts at 39 weeks. The NHS uses a broader definition and already calls this stage full term. If labour starts on its own now, the baby is not premature. The split between early and full term exists because outcomes for babies are not identical across these weeks, so planning a birth before 39 weeks without a medical reason is not usually the first choice.

According to the NHS, around 95% of babies are now head down, usually facing their mother's back. When the head moves down into the pelvis the baby is said to be "engaged", and your bump may look lower. If your baby is still bottom down (breech), there is still time to turn, and some only move into place when labour starts. Your midwife or doctor checks the position at appointments, and if the baby stays breech they will talk you through the options and a plan for the birth.

The NHS says your baby is trying out facial expressions such as frowning and smiling, at random and not linked to feeling sad or happy. By now you probably know when your baby tends to be active and when calmer. That pattern stays your reference until the birth: movements feel different with less room, but they should not slow down. If you notice fewer movements or a change in the pattern, contact your maternity unit or ob-gyn straight away and do not wait to see if it improves.

## Changes in your body

As the baby moves down into your pelvis, heartburn and indigestion may ease a little, says the NHS. On the other hand, you feel more pressure low down. It is common to notice more vaginal discharge and more Braxton Hicks contractions, the practice contractions, which can be uncomfortable but should not be painful.

Pains on the side of the bump, tiredness, cramp, mild headaches and dark patches on the face (chloasma) can carry on too. How strongly you feel any of this varies a lot from one woman to another.

Many women get a sudden urge to clean the whole house. The NHS calls this nesting and suggests not overdoing it: resting as much as you can is more useful now.

Signs that labour may be on its way, include Braxton Hicks contractions becoming more frequent, the show (a sticky blob of mucus, sometimes yellowish or a little bloody) and an urge to go to the toilet as the baby's head presses on your bladder and bowel. None of this is active labour, and days can pass between the show and the birth.

Labour usually starts with an early (latent) phase, which many women spend at home. In England the NHS advises calling your midwife or maternity unit when contractions come every 5 minutes or more often. Ask someone to note when each contraction starts and ends so you can see the pattern. If you live far from the hospital or your team has given you different advice because of your history, follow what you agreed with them.

If your waters break, note the colour of the fluid, tell your team and get checked, even without contractions. If you are not sure whether it is fluid or urine, get checked anyway.

## Antenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ by country, so here are England and the United States separately.

In England, the NHS schedule has appointments at 36 and 38 weeks, plus one at 40 weeks if this is your first baby. Group B strep is not routinely tested for in NHS pregnancy care, and the NHS week 37 guide suggests talking to your midwife about it. If you have not had the RSV vaccine, which lowers your baby's risk of bronchiolitis, it can still be given right up until labour: ask your midwife or GP.

In the United States, screening for group B strep is done for everyone between 36 weeks and 0 days and 37 weeks and 6 days, so if you have not had the swab yet, this is the week to ask. The maternal RSV vaccine is given between 32 weeks and 36 weeks and 6 days, during the RSV season; after that, the CDC says the baby is protected with an infant immunisation instead, so ask your provider about it before the birth.

## What to do this week

1. Have your hospital bag packed and by the door, or already in the car, with your maternity notes and any documents you need.
2. Save your maternity unit's or birth centre's phone number and address, and agree on who will drive you and who will be your birth partner. The NHS warns it is not safe to drive yourself while in labour.
3. Check where you stand at work. You may already be on maternity leave or about to stop, so confirm your dates and pay with your employer.
4. Read about postnatal depression with the people you live with. The NHS notes it affects more than 1 in 10 women and can come on gradually. Our guide to [postpartum mental health](/en/blog/postpartum-mental-health-baby-blues-depression/) covers the signs.
5. Keep up your pelvic floor exercises, which help prevent leaks when you cough, laugh or sneeze.

> Contact your maternity unit without waiting for your next appointment if you have regular contractions every 5 minutes or more often, fluid leaking from your vagina, vaginal bleeding, a fever, or pain or burning when you pee. Call straight away if your baby is moving less than usual or the pattern of movements changes. A severe headache that does not go away, blurred vision or flashing lights, severe pain just below the ribs and sudden swelling of the face, hands or legs need immediate assessment, because they can be signs of high blood pressure in pregnancy. Call your midwife or maternity unit in the UK, or your ob-gyn in the US. For a seizure, fainting, severe breathlessness, chest pain or heavy bleeding, call 999 in the UK or 911 in the US.
