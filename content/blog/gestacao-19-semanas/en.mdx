**Quick answer:** at 19 weeks you are in the 5th month and the 2nd trimester, one week short of halfway. Your baby is **about 15.3 cm** from head to bottom and weighs **about 270 g**, roughly the size of a mango. The mid-pregnancy anatomy scan is due around now, and many women start to notice the first movements.

"Nineteen weeks" means nineteen completed weeks, from 19 weeks and 0 days to 19 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period (your first scan may have adjusted the dating). The due date is set at 40 weeks, so there are about 21 to go. Converting weeks to months is approximate, which is why your care team always talks in weeks.

## How your baby is developing at 19 weeks

The length is still given from head to bottom, because the legs are curled up and hard to measure. The figure of about 15.3 cm comes from the NHS week-by-week guide, which switches to head-to-heel length in week 20. That is why next week's number is so much bigger.

The weight of about 270 g is the median on the World Health Organization fetal growth charts, estimated by ultrasound. Your scan report has your own baby's measurements, and your care team interprets them.

According to the NHS, your baby is putting on weight and the adult teeth are starting to form inside the gums, lined up behind the first set. Between 17 and 20 weeks the body is covered in a fine down called lanugo, and the facial features can be recognised on ultrasound.

Your baby moves a lot, and you may already feel it. First movements are usually felt between 16 and 24 weeks. In a first pregnancy it is common for that to happen only after 20 weeks, and women who have had a baby before tend to recognise the feeling sooner. Early on it is like bubbles low in the belly and is easy to mistake for wind. Feeling nothing at 19 weeks is common. If you reach 24 weeks without noticing any movement, tell your midwife or doctor.

## What is changing in your body

Relaxin, one of the pregnancy hormones, loosens your ligaments. The NHS explains that your back, knees and ankles lose some of their usual support, so high-impact exercise such as running can become uncomfortable. When there is no medical reason to hold back, staying active is good for you and your baby. If the exercise you were doing has started to hurt, ask your care team how to adapt it.

The growing uterus also stretches the ligaments that hold it. That causes twinges at the sides of the belly, known as round ligament pain. Pain that is severe, does not ease, comes at regular intervals or arrives with bleeding, leaking fluid, a fever or burning when you pee needs checking.

A more visible bump brings guesses about the baby's sex. Its size and shape vary a lot from one woman to another. The sex is usually seen on the scan, when the baby's position allows it.

Other common symptoms at this stage:

- Tiredness and poor sleep. Lying on your side, with one pillow between your knees and another supporting your bump, often helps.
- Heartburn, bloating and constipation. Smaller, more frequent meals ease heartburn for many women.
- Darker patches on the face, greasier skin and stretch marks.
- Swollen gums that bleed when you brush, the occasional nosebleed, feeling hot and dizziness.
- Leg cramps and mild swelling of the hands and feet.
- White, milky discharge, which is expected. Burning when you pee, itching or discharge with a strong smell are reasons to book an appointment. Watery fluid that leaks is something else and needs same-day care.

Not every woman gets all of these. On food, the NHS points out that there is no need to eat for two and that extra calories only come into the picture in the third trimester. Your midwife or doctor can give advice on weight gain that fits you.

## Antenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ by country, so here is what applies in England and what applies in the US.

In England, the NHS plans at least 10 appointments for a first baby and at least 7 if you have given birth before. After the 16-week appointment, the next routine ones are at 25 weeks (first baby) and 28 weeks, so the main event around now is the scan. The 20-week screening scan is offered to everyone, usually between 18 and 21 weeks, and can be done up to 23 weeks in some cases. Week 19 is inside that window, so yours may be this week. The sonographer looks closely at your baby's bones, heart, brain, spinal cord, face, kidneys and tummy, checks growth and looks at the placenta. You may be asked whether you want to know the sex. No scan can find everything, and a result that needs a second look is a reason to talk it through with your team before drawing conclusions.

In the US, the timing of the anatomy scan is set by your ob-gyn or midwife. International ultrasound guidance (ISUOG) places the routine mid-pregnancy scan between 18 and 24 weeks.

The whooping cough vaccine is where the two countries differ most. The NHS usually offers it around week 20; it can be given from week 16 and works best before week 32. In the US, the CDC and ACOG recommend it between 27 and 36 weeks, as early in that window as possible. Both repeat it in every pregnancy. The flu vaccine is recommended at any stage of pregnancy in both countries.

Further ahead is the glucose test at 24 to 28 weeks. In the US it is a routine test for all pregnant women. In England it is only for women with a higher chance of gestational diabetes.

## What to do this week

1. If your scan is not booked yet, sort it out now. In England, tell your midwife or GP if you are between 18 and 21 weeks and have no date.
2. Think about where you want to give birth and ask your midwife or doctor what the options are near you. There is still plenty of time to decide.
3. Adjust your exercise to what your body can take now. For women with no medical restrictions, the general recommendation is about 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, and you can start with 10-minute walks. Check with your care team what suits you.
4. Start pelvic floor exercises, which help prevent leaks when you cough, laugh or sneeze. Your midwife or a pelvic health physiotherapist can teach you the technique.
5. Ask about the whooping cough vaccine: around now in England, and at which visit it is planned in the US.

> Get checked without waiting for your next appointment if you have vaginal bleeding, fluid leaking from the vagina, severe or persistent abdominal pain, cramps or tightenings of the belly that come at regular intervals, a fever, pain or burning when you pee, a severe headache that does not go away, changes in your vision, sudden swelling of your face, hands or legs, shortness of breath or fainting. Call your maternity unit or midwife in the UK, or your ob-gyn in the US. In an emergency, call 999 or 911.
