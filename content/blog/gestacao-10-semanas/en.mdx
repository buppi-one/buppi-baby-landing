**Quick answer:** at 10 weeks you are in the 3rd month and close to the end of the 1st trimester. Your baby is **about 30 mm** (3 cm) long from head to bottom, roughly the size of a strawberry, and already moves on a scan. Nausea is still common at this stage and tends to ease over the coming weeks. The window for the first-trimester scan is opening.

"Ten weeks" means ten completed weeks, from 10 weeks and 0 days to 10 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. The due date is set at 40 weeks, so there are about 30 left. Converting to months is approximate, which is why your care team always talks in weeks. If the date of your last period is uncertain or your cycle is irregular, the early scan is used to correct the count.

## Your baby at 10 weeks

The 30 mm measurement is taken from head to bottom, because the legs are folded and are left out. A week ago it was about 22 mm, so your baby has grown by more than a third in seven days. Weight is not estimated this early: the scan report gives the length, and that measurement helps confirm how far along you are.

According to the NHS week-by-week guide, the head is still large for the body, but the face is coming into proportion. The eyes are half closed. The ears are starting to form, the nose has two small nostrils and the mouth has an upper lip. Inside the jawbone sit the buds of the future milk teeth.

The heart beats very fast, close to 180 times a minute, about three times your own rate. Your baby is also moving, in short jerky movements that can be seen on a scan. You cannot feel any of it yet. Mothers usually notice first movements between 16 and 24 weeks.

## What is changing in your body

Your uterus is about the size of a large orange. Jeans may feel tight at the waist before any bump shows, and much of that is bloating. Progesterone relaxes the muscle of the uterus so it can expand, and the same effect reaches the digestive tract. That brings wind, burping, a bloated feeling and sometimes heartburn.

Common symptoms at this stage include:

- Nausea, with or without vomiting, at any time of day. It usually starts before 9 weeks and, for most women, is gone by around 14 weeks. For some it lasts until 16 to 20 weeks.
- Extreme tiredness and sleepiness at odd hours.
- Sore, more sensitive breasts.
- Dizziness, mostly when you stand up quickly. Getting up slowly from bed or a chair helps.
- A sharper sense of smell, a metallic taste in the mouth and new food likes and dislikes.
- Mood swings and headaches.
- White, milky discharge without a strong smell, and mild cramps like period pains. Strong cramps, or cramps with bleeding, need to be checked.

Not everyone gets all of these, and the intensity changes from one pregnancy to the next. For nausea and bloating, eating small amounts more often tends to help (the NHS suggests six small meals a day), along with not eating late at night, eating slowly and sipping fluids. Cold food is easier when the smell of hot meals makes the nausea worse. A short walk after meals also helps digestion.

A severe form of pregnancy sickness, with persistent vomiting and dehydration, affects up to 3% of pregnant women and needs treatment. If nausea is stopping you from eating, drinking or working, say so at your appointment: there are medicines your doctor or midwife can recommend, and that decision is theirs.

On weight, there is no need to eat for two. Your care team follows your weight over the pregnancy, and individual advice comes from them.

## Antenatal care at this stage

In England, the NHS booking appointment with a midwife usually takes place between 8 and 12 weeks and lasts about an hour. You are asked about your health and medical history, and you are offered screening tests for infectious diseases and for conditions such as Down's syndrome. If you have not arranged it yet, contact your GP surgery or refer yourself to your local maternity service now. If this is your first baby you can expect at least 10 appointments, or at least 7 if you have given birth before, and 2 routine scans.

The first of those scans is the dating scan, offered at around 10 to 14 weeks and often called the 12-week scan. It checks how many weeks pregnant you are, whether there is more than one baby and that the pregnancy is growing in the right place. If you have agreed to screening for Down's, Edwards' and Patau's syndromes, the combined test happens at the same visit: a blood test plus a measurement of the fluid at the back of the baby's neck, the nuchal translucency. Screening gives a chance, not a diagnosis.

In the US, your ob-gyn or midwife sets the schedule of visits and tests with you. International ultrasound guidance describes a first-trimester scan between 11 and 14 weeks, and that is the window in which a nuchal translucency measurement is taken when you choose that screening. Ask at your next visit what is offered and when.

On vaccines, the NHS recommends the flu vaccine at any stage of pregnancy. The whooping cough vaccine comes later: from 16 weeks and usually around 20 in England, and between 27 and 36 weeks in the US.

## What to do this week

1. If antenatal care has not started, book the first appointment. In England the aim is to have it between 8 and 12 weeks.
2. Ask when your first-trimester scan will be, and whether you want the screening that goes with it. The window is short, so it is worth having the date in hand.
3. Take your vaccination record to the appointment so the team can check what is due.
4. Keep taking the supplements your midwife or doctor recommended, which at this stage usually include folic acid. If you take a regular medicine for a long-term condition, do not stop or switch it on your own: tell the person looking after your pregnancy.
5. Stop alcohol and smoking and cut down on caffeine, which is in coffee, tea and cola drinks. If you have been cleared to exercise, 10-minute walks are a good start towards 150 minutes of moderate activity a week.

> Get medical advice without waiting for your next appointment if you have vaginal bleeding, severe or persistent abdominal pain, a fever, pain or burning when you pee, or vomiting that stops you keeping food or fluids down for 24 hours. Vomiting together with very dark urine, more than 8 hours without peeing, or feeling dizzy and weak when you stand up is also a reason to be checked, even before 24 hours have passed. Light bleeding is not always serious, but it does need to be assessed. Contact your maternity unit, early pregnancy unit or midwife, or your GP or NHS 111 if you have not booked yet (UK), or your ob-gyn (US). Heavy bleeding, severe pain in your tummy, pain in your shoulder or fainting need emergency care: call 999 in the UK or 911 in the US.
