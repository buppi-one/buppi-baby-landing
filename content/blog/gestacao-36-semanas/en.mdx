**Quick answer:** at 36 weeks you are starting the 9th month, in the third trimester. Your baby is **about 47.4 cm** long from head to heel and weighs **about 2.7 kg**, roughly the size of a romaine lettuce. Their lungs are probably ready to breathe outside the womb, but a birth this week still counts as preterm. Many babies have already moved head down, and some drop into the pelvis.

"Thirty-six weeks" means 36 completed weeks, from 36 weeks and 0 days to 36 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. Your due date is set at 40 weeks, so you have about 4 to go.

## Your baby at 36 weeks

The length comes from the week-by-week guide of the NHS, the UK's public health service, measured from head to heel. The weight is the median of the World Health Organization's fetal growth charts. On those charts, 2 in 10 babies fall outside the 2.4 kg to 3.2 kg range, and a different estimate on a scan is not a problem in itself. Your prenatal team interprets it.

According to the NHS, your baby's lungs are probably mature enough by now to breathe without help outside the womb. Your baby can also suck and digest breast milk. Development carries on: ACOG, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, describes weeks 37 to 40 as the time when the lungs and brain finish developing.

That is why the exact week still matters. The World Health Organization calls any baby born before 37 completed weeks preterm, and a baby born at 36 weeks is late preterm. ACOG calls a birth between 37 weeks and 38 weeks 6 days early term, and one between 39 weeks and 40 weeks 6 days full term. Many babies born now do well, and the maternity team works out what care each one needs.

Many babies are already head down. ACOG places this turn between 33 and 36 weeks. The NHS explains that the head can move down into the pelvis, which is called being engaged, and that this does not mean labour is close: it could still be weeks away.

If your baby is still breech, your team may check whether external cephalic version is an option for you. A doctor or midwife gently presses on your bump with their hands to encourage the baby to turn. The NHS says it works about half the time. How you give birth depends on the baby's position and other factors, and you will talk this through at your appointments.

Movements can feel different now, but there should not be fewer of them before birth. What counts is your own baby's usual pattern.

## What is changing in your body

If your baby has engaged, your bump may sit lower and you may find it easier to breathe and eat. The trade-off is usually more pressure on your bladder and pelvis.

Leaking a little urine when you laugh or cough is common at this stage. The NHS explains that the pelvic floor muscles around the bladder are relaxing ahead of the birth, and suggests doing pelvic floor exercises and stocking up on maternity pads, which you will need after the birth anyway.

Other symptoms the NHS lists for week 36:

- Braxton Hicks contractions, when the bump tightens, usually without pain, and then relaxes. They are irregular and do not get closer together.
- Trouble sleeping, backache and pains on the side of the bump caused by your growing womb.
- Heartburn, indigestion, bloating, constipation and piles.
- Leg cramps, feeling hot and dizziness.
- Swollen hands and feet. Swelling that comes on suddenly, especially in the face, needs to be checked the same day.
- Headaches. If one is severe and does not go away, get medical advice.

Do not take anything on your own for pain, heartburn or sleep. In pregnancy, take medicines only on the advice of your midwife or doctor.

Every pregnancy is different: some women reach this week with few complaints, others feel all of them at once.

## Prenatal care at this stage

Care differs between countries. Here is how it works in England (NHS) and in the United States (ACOG and CDC).

**England.** The NHS schedule has an antenatal appointment at 36 weeks, then at 38 weeks, at 40 weeks for a first baby, and at 41 weeks if the baby has not arrived. The NHS week 36 guide says this check covers your blood pressure, your urine and the size of your bump, and that these appointments can pick up changes you might not notice, such as very high blood pressure. If you have not had the RSV vaccine, which protects your baby against bronchiolitis, the NHS says it can still be given up until labour. Group B strep is not routinely tested for in NHS pregnancy care.

**United States.** ACOG recommends testing everyone for group B strep, a common bacterium, between 36 weeks 0 days and 37 weeks 6 days, usually with a swab of the vagina and rectum. If the result is positive, your team will explain what happens in labour. The maternal RSV vaccine is given between 32 and 36 weeks 6 days during the RSV season, so this is the last week of that window: ask your provider whether it applies to you. The whooping cough (Tdap) vaccine is recommended between 27 and 36 weeks of every pregnancy.

## What to do this week

1. Keep your hospital bag packed and near the door, with your maternity notes, ID, clothes and nappies for the baby, comfortable clothes and maternity pads for you. The NHS suggests writing down your hospital reference number and important phone numbers.
2. Save the phone number and address of your maternity unit or hospital, and agree who will drive you there.
3. If you are still working, check your leave dates with your employer.
4. If you have other children, arrange who will look after them while you are in hospital, and keep their routine as close to normal as you can.
5. Ask your midwife or ob-gyn how to tell practice contractions from labour, and when they want you to call or come in.

> Get checked without waiting for your next appointment if you have regular or painful contractions before 37 weeks, fluid leaking from your vagina (if you are not sure whether it is fluid, urine or discharge, get checked), vaginal bleeding, a fever, or pain or burning when you pee. Call straight away if your baby is moving less than usual or the pattern of movements changes. A severe headache, blurred vision or flashing lights, severe pain just below the ribs and sudden swelling of the face, hands or legs can be signs of high blood pressure in pregnancy. Call your midwife or maternity unit in the UK, or your ob-gyn in the US. For a seizure, fainting, severe breathlessness, chest pain or heavy bleeding, call 999 in the UK or 911 in the US.
