**Quick answer:** at 29 weeks you are in the 7th month and the second week of the 3rd trimester. Your baby is **about 38.6 cm** from head to heel and weighs **about 1.4 kg**. Constipation and leg cramps tend to get worse around now, and the vaccines of late pregnancy come up at your appointments.

"Twenty-nine weeks" means 29 completed weeks (from 29 weeks and 0 days to 29 weeks and 6 days), counted from the first day of your last period. The due date is set at 40 weeks, so there are about 11 to go. Converting weeks to months is approximate, which is why your care team always talks in weeks.

## How your baby is developing at 29 weeks

The length comes from the NHS week-by-week guide and is measured from head to heel. The weight is the middle value on the World Health Organization fetal growth charts. Both are reference points, and a baby a little above or below them can be growing well. Your midwife or doctor is the one who assesses that.

According to the NHS, your baby is now fully formed. The job of the coming weeks is to mature the organs and put on fat, which gradually smooths out the skin. The greasy white coating that protects the skin (vernix) and the fine, downy hair covering the body start to disappear from now on.

Movements follow the baby's own pattern of sleeping and waking, which often does not match yours. Many babies get livelier just as their mother lies down. There is still room to change position, so being bottom-down or sideways this week says nothing about how your baby will be lying at the birth. Many babies only turn head-down closer to the end.

A baby born at 28 weeks or later and before 32 completed weeks is classed by the WHO as very preterm and usually needs care in a neonatal unit. That is why the signs of early labour listed further down need to be checked quickly.

## What changes in your body

Your growing womb presses on your diaphragm and your bladder, so you may get a little breathless when you exert yourself and be up several times a night. Your centre of gravity has shifted, and it is normal to feel clumsy and less steady on your feet. If you fall, tell your midwife or doctor the same day, even if it seems like nothing. If you hit your bump, or if the fall is followed by pain, bleeding, leaking fluid or your baby moving less, go straight to your maternity unit or hospital.

Constipation deserves some attention at this stage. Pregnancy hormones slow the bowel down, and hard stools make piles more likely. Piles (haemorrhoids) are swollen veins in and around the anus. They can cause itching, soreness, a small lump and bright red blood on the paper after a bowel movement. What usually helps, according to the NHS:

- Eating more fibre: wholemeal bread and cereals, fruit, vegetables, beans and lentils.
- Drinking plenty of water through the day.
- Walking or doing some other gentle activity regularly.
- Not sitting or standing for long periods, and not straining on the toilet, because straining makes piles worse.
- Cleaning the area with moist toilet paper, patting instead of rubbing. A cold compress eases the pain.

If your iron supplement is making constipation worse, bring it up at your appointment instead of stopping it on your own. The same goes for any medicine or product for your bowels or for piles: check with your midwife, doctor or pharmacist first. Mention any bleeding too.

Other common symptoms at 29 weeks:

- Cramp in your calves and feet, nearly always at night. Pulling your toes up towards your ankle and rubbing the muscle eases it on the spot. Moving your feet and ankles during the day may help prevent it.
- Heartburn, backache and twinges at the sides of your bump as your womb grows. Pain that is severe or does not go away needs to be checked.
- Hands and feet that swell gradually over the day. Swelling that comes on suddenly is a warning sign.
- A bump that goes hard now and then, irregularly and for a few seconds. These are practice contractions, different from the regular ones described in the warning box below.
- Sore breasts, which in some women already leak a little colostrum, the first milk.
- Broken sleep. The NHS advises going to sleep on your side from 28 weeks, with one pillow under your bump and another between your legs.

## Prenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ between countries, so here is what applies in England and in the US.

In England, the NHS antenatal appointments in this part of pregnancy are at 28 weeks, at 31 weeks if this is your first baby, and at 34 weeks. The NHS offers two routine ultrasound scans, both earlier in pregnancy, so a third-trimester scan is only arranged if your midwife or doctor sees a reason for one. The glucose tolerance test for gestational diabetes is done at 24 to 28 weeks and only for women at higher chance of the condition.

For vaccines in England, the RSV (respiratory syncytial virus) vaccine is offered from 28 weeks in every pregnancy, usually around the 28-week appointment, and can still be given later, up to labour. It lowers your baby's risk of bronchiolitis in the first months. The whooping cough vaccine is usually given around 20 weeks and works best before 32 weeks, although you can still have it later. If you have not had it, speak to your midwife or GP this week. The flu vaccine can be given at any stage.

In the US, the CDC recommends the whooping cough (Tdap) vaccine in every pregnancy between 27 and 36 weeks, as early in that window as possible, which means around now. The RSV vaccine comes later, at 32 to 36 weeks, and only during RSV season, so ask your provider whether it will apply to you. ACOG lists the glucose screening test for all pregnant women at 24 to 28 weeks. If you have not had it yet, tell your ob-gyn.

## What to do this week

1. Check where you are with the whooping cough vaccine. In England it works best before 32 weeks, and in the US the window opened at 27 weeks. In England, also ask about the RSV vaccine if it was not offered at your 28-week appointment.
2. Talk to your employer. The NHS notes that in the UK you can ask for a risk assessment of your workplace and go to antenatal appointments in paid work time. In the US, ask your HR department what leave you are entitled to.
3. Start drafting a birth plan, if you would like one. Two things to think about first: what pain relief you would like to have available, if any, and who you want as your birth partner.
4. Spend a few days noticing when your baby tends to move most. You do not need to count movements: once you know the pattern, you will notice when it changes. If it does, do not wait until the next day to be checked.
5. Keep moving. The general recommendation is about 150 minutes of moderate activity a week. Check with your midwife or doctor what suits you.

> Contact your maternity unit or midwife (UK) or your ob-gyn (US) without waiting for your next appointment if you have vaginal bleeding, fluid leaking from your vagina (if you are not sure whether it is urine or your waters, get checked), regular or frequent contractions, period-like cramps, pressure in your pelvis or a constant dull ache low in your back. Do the same if your baby moves less than usual or the pattern of movements changes. A severe headache that will not go away, blurred vision or flashing lights, severe pain just below the ribs and sudden swelling of your face, hands or legs can be signs of high blood pressure and also need a same-day check, as do a high temperature and pain when you pee. For heavy bleeding, a seizure, fainting, difficulty breathing or chest pain, call 999 (UK) or 911 (US).
