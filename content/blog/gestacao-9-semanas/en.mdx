**Quick answer:** at 9 weeks you are starting the 3rd month of pregnancy and are still in the 1st trimester. Your baby is **about 22 mm** from head to bottom, roughly the size of a grape. The pregnancy hormone (hCG) reaches its highest level around this week, the stage when nausea and tiredness tend to be at their worst.

"Nine weeks" means nine completed weeks, from 9 weeks and 0 days to 9 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. There are about 31 weeks to go until the due date, which is set at 40 weeks. Converting weeks to months is approximate, which is why your care team always talks in weeks.

## How your baby is developing at 9 weeks

The length of about 22 mm runs from the top of the head to the bottom and leaves out the legs, which are still forming. It is an average, and what counts for your baby is what the scan measures.

According to the NHS week-by-week guide, the face is becoming more recognisable. The eyes are now protected by eyelids, the mouth has formed and the tongue has its first taste buds. Hands and feet are developing but have no separate fingers or toes yet, only grooves where they will appear.

The heart, brain, lungs, kidneys and gut are developing, and bones are starting to form. The external genitals are beginning to take shape too, although they cannot be told apart on a scan yet. The NHS notes that the sex is usually seen at the mid-pregnancy scan.

The eyes are getting bigger and already have some pigment. The colour a baby is born with depends on the parents' genetics and often changes after birth.

## What is changing in your body

The NHS guide describes hCG rising fast over the past few weeks and reaching its highest level at around 9 weeks. Levels vary a lot from one woman to another, and a single number does not say how the pregnancy is going. Oestrogen and progesterone are high at this stage as well. Very little shows on the outside yet, though your breasts may be bigger and your waist a little thicker.

Common symptoms at this stage include:

- Nausea, with or without vomiting, at any time of day. It usually starts before 9 weeks and, for most women, has gone by 14. The NHS suggests eating little and often, which can ease it.
- Extreme tiredness and sleepiness.
- Sore, tender breasts.
- A sharper sense of smell, a metallic taste in the mouth and sudden changes in what you want to eat or drink.
- Bloating and wind. The NHS explains that progesterone relaxes the muscles of the womb and also those of the digestive tract, which contributes to heartburn.
- Mood swings, headaches and mild cramps, a bit like period pains. Pain that is severe, does not ease or stays on one side needs checking.
- Milky white discharge, which is expected in pregnancy. If it smells strong, itches, stings or turns yellow or green, book an appointment.

Not every woman gets all of these, and intensity changes from one pregnancy to the next. Having few symptoms, or noticing that they come and go, does not by itself say how the pregnancy is progressing.

The NHS lists light spotting among the possible signs at this stage, and also advises that any bleeding in pregnancy should be checked. The amount or colour of the blood does not tell you the cause, so get seen even if it is only a little.

## Antenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ by country, so here is what applies in England and what applies in the US.

In England, the first midwife appointment, called the booking appointment, takes place between 8 and 12 weeks. If you have not arranged it, contact your GP surgery or refer yourself to your local maternity service now. At the appointment the midwife asks about your health and history, and you are offered blood tests and screening for infectious diseases and for conditions such as Down's syndrome. The NHS plans at least 10 appointments for a first baby and at least 7 if you have given birth before, with 2 routine scans. The first, the dating scan, is offered at around 10 to 14 weeks. It confirms how many weeks pregnant you are, and combined screening can be done at the same visit.

In the US, your ob-gyn or midwife's office sets the timing of the first prenatal visit and of the tests that follow, so call to book if you have not done so. International ultrasound guidance (ISUOG) describes a first-trimester scan at 11 to 14 weeks.

On vaccines, the flu vaccine is recommended during flu season at any stage of pregnancy in both countries. The whooping cough vaccine comes later: in England it is usually offered around week 20 and can be given from week 16, and in the US the CDC and ACOG recommend it between 27 and 36 weeks.

## What to do this week

1. Book your first appointment if you have not yet, and take any vaccination records and test results you already have.
2. Write down the date of your last period and any questions that come up. List everything you take regularly, with or without a prescription, so your team can review it. Do not stop or start anything on your own. Folic acid is usually advised in early pregnancy: if you were advised to take a supplement, keep taking it. If you are not taking one, ask about it now instead of waiting for an appointment that is weeks away.
3. Stop smoking and drinking alcohol, and cut down on coffee, tea and other drinks with caffeine. If that is hard, ask for support at your appointment.
4. For nausea, swap large meals for small, frequent snacks and keep water within reach.
5. Move as much as you can manage. The general recommendation is about 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, and a 10-minute walk is a fair start on very tired days. Check with your care team what suits you.

> Get checked without waiting for your next appointment if you have vaginal bleeding, severe abdominal pain or pain that does not ease, fainting, a fever, pain or burning when you pee, or vomiting so frequent that you cannot keep fluids down. Call your midwife, maternity unit or GP in the UK, or your ob-gyn in the US. If the pain is intense, the bleeding is heavy or you faint, call 999 in the UK or 911 in the US.

The first trimester also affects mood and anxiety. If feeling low or worried is weighing on you, bring it up at your appointment the same way you would a physical symptom.
