**Quick answer:** at 41 weeks your pregnancy has gone past the due date and is called late term. From 42 full weeks it is called post-term. Your baby is usually ready to be born and tends to weigh between 3 and 4 kg. From here on, antenatal care gets closer, with more checks on the baby's wellbeing and a conversation with your care team about waiting or having labour induced.

Weeks are counted from the first day of your last period. "41 weeks" runs from 41 weeks and 0 days to 41 weeks and 6 days. The due date is set at 40 weeks, but only a small share of babies arrive on that exact day, and reaching this point with no sign of labour is more common than it feels.

## Weeks 41 and 42 at a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| Trimester | 3rd |
| Month | past the 9th, which ends at week 40 (approximate count) |
| Compared with the due date | 1 to 2 weeks later |
| Classification | late term from 41w0d to 41w6d; post-term from 42w0d |
| Weight of a term baby | usually between 3 and 4 kg |

These names follow the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG): early term from 37 weeks to 38 weeks and 6 days, full term from 39 weeks to 40 weeks and 6 days, late term in week 41 and post-term from 42 weeks. You may also see "overdue" or "prolonged pregnancy" used for the same stretch of time.

## Your baby at 41 and 42 weeks

According to the NHS week-by-week guide, the fast growth phase is over and your baby has enough fat and maturity to live outside the womb without help. This series does not give week-by-week length and weight after 40 weeks, because the reference charts stop there. What can be said is that a term baby usually weighs between 3 and 4 kg, and each one has its own size.

Babies born after the due date often have red, dry, peeling skin. This happens because they have lost much of the vernix, the greasy layer that protected their skin in the amniotic fluid. The NHS says the dry layer peels off on its own within a few days and that moisturising can make it worse. Ask the maternity team how to care for it.

## Changes in your body

Symptoms are the ones from late in the third trimester. Your bump tightens now and then (Braxton Hicks contractions), and backache, swollen hands and feet, heartburn and poor sleep are common. You may also notice more vaginal discharge and lose the mucus plug, a thick discharge that is sometimes streaked with blood.

The waiting is often the hardest part. People ask every day whether the baby has arrived, and some push for a caesarean or an induction. The due date is an estimate, and in an uncomplicated pregnancy with no sign of a problem, waiting with monitoring is a recognised approach, decided case by case with your care team. Tiredness and frustration are expected now. If anxiety is costing you sleep or appetite, tell your midwife or doctor.

## Antenatal care at this stage

In England, the NHS schedule includes an appointment at 41 weeks for everyone who has not given birth yet. The NHS offers induction if labour has not started on its own by 41 weeks and, if the pregnancy goes beyond 42 weeks and you choose not to be induced, it offers increased monitoring of the baby's wellbeing. In the US, ACOG says that testing to check on the baby and induction may be recommended from 41 weeks. Which tests are done is decided by your care team for your situation. If you have reached 41 weeks and nothing is booked, call your midwife, maternity unit or ob-gyn.

Before induction, the NHS offers a membrane sweep from 39 weeks. It is done with a finger during an internal examination and can cause some cramping and a little bleeding. Inducing labour means starting it artificially, and it happens in a hospital maternity unit, with medicines that soften and open the cervix, mechanical devices that help the cervix open, or breaking the waters. The care team chooses and explains each step. The NHS says the team should explain the reasons, the risks and the alternatives, that the final decision is yours, and lists points to weigh: induced labour is usually more painful than labour that starts on its own, it usually takes many hours to work, it does not restrict your pain relief options and it can make an assisted birth with forceps or ventouse more likely. Ask why induction is being offered, what happens if you wait and what the plan is if it does not work. When induction does not work, the NHS lists trying another method, a caesarean, or waiting a few hours and reassessing.

Hot baths, certain foods, teas and sex appear on many lists of ways to "bring labour on". The NHS says none of them has been shown to work and that herbal supplements could be harmful. Talk to your midwife or doctor before trying anything.

## What to do this week

- Check the plan for the next few days with your care team: when your next check is and from what date induction will be discussed in your case.
- Keep paying attention to your baby's movements. They should not slow down at the end of pregnancy. If your baby moves less or stops moving, contact your maternity unit straight away.
- Keep your hospital bag and pregnancy notes in the car or by the door, and have the baby's car seat ready to fit.
- Line up support for after the birth. Agree who will help with meals, laundry and the house in the first weeks, and freeze a few meals while you still can.
- Know when labour is established: contractions that are strong, regular and getting closer together. Your maternity unit or ob-gyn will tell you when to come in, and the early phase can often be spent at home.

> **Contact your maternity unit or midwife (UK) or your ob-gyn (US) straight away, or call 999 or 911 in an emergency, if:** your baby moves less or stops moving; your waters break, especially if the fluid is green, brown or bloodstained; you have vaginal bleeding; contractions become strong and regular; you have a severe headache, problems with your vision, sudden swelling of the face or hands, a fever or severe tummy pain. Every pregnancy has its own rhythm, but these signs need to be checked immediately.
