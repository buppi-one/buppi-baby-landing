**Quick answer:** at 6 weeks you are in the 2nd month and the 1st trimester. The embryo is **about 6 mm** from head to bottom, roughly the size of a lentil, and the buds of the arms and legs are starting to show. For many women this is the week nausea settles in, and it is a good time to arrange your first appointment.

"Six weeks" means six completed weeks, from 6 weeks and 0 days to 6 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. Conception usually happens around the second week of that count, so the embryo has been developing for about four weeks. The due date is set at 40 weeks, which leaves about 34 to go. If you do not remember the date or your cycles are irregular, the first-trimester scan confirms how far along you are.

## How your baby is developing at 6 weeks

The medical term at this stage is embryo, and it has tripled in length since last week, from about 2 mm to about 6 mm. The body is curved and has a small tail, which is why the NHS week-by-week guide says some people think it looks like a tadpole.

The arms and legs begin as small buds. The head forms a bulge that is large compared with the rest of the body, and it already has the tiny dents where the ears will be. Another bump marks the heart. The liver, the brain and the musculoskeletal system are starting to develop, and a thin layer of transparent skin covers the embryo.

The heart is the most common question this week. According to the NHS, the heartbeat can sometimes be picked up by a vaginal ultrasound scan at 6 weeks. It does not always show this early, and a few days' difference in gestational age changes what the image shows. If you have a scan now and no heartbeat is seen, the sonographer or your care team will tell you whether it needs repeating and when.

## What is changing in your body

Little has changed on the outside, but in many pregnancies symptoms become more obvious around now. The best known is nausea. It is called morning sickness, yet it can come at any hour, and the NHS notes it is usually worse when you first wake up. ACOG says it usually starts before 9 weeks and for most women is gone by 14 weeks; the NHS says it usually clears up by weeks 16 to 20.

A few simple measures often help:

- Keep a dry snack by the bed, such as toast or a plain biscuit, and eat it before you get up.
- Swap large meals for several small ones through the day, with plain food that is low in fat.
- Choose cold food when the smell of hot meals bothers you.
- Sip water little and often.
- Rest whenever you can, because tiredness makes nausea worse.
- Try foods with ginger or sucking ice cubes, which some women find helpful.

If none of this works, talk to your midwife or doctor. There are medicines a doctor can prescribe, and that choice is theirs to make.

Other common symptoms this week are tiredness, sore breasts, needing to pee more often, a sharper sense of smell, a metallic taste in the mouth, going off foods you used to like, bloating, mood swings, headaches and a white, milky discharge (tell your team if it itches, smells strong or changes colour). For headaches, drinking plenty of fluids, sleeping well and resting usually helps. Do not take medicine on your own initiative: ask your midwife, doctor or pharmacist what is safe for you. A severe headache, or one that comes with vision changes, needs checking.

Mild cramps, a bit like period pains, and light spotting are also on the list of early pregnancy symptoms. Bleeding in pregnancy is not always a sign of a problem, but it needs to be assessed by a healthcare professional to find the cause. When a pregnancy is lost at this stage, it is often because of a problem with the way the pregnancy itself was developing.

Not every pregnant woman gets all of this. Some go through this week with no symptoms at all.

## Antenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ by country, so here is what applies in England and what applies in the US.

In England, tell your GP you are pregnant or refer yourself to your local maternity service, which you can usually do through the hospital's website. The first midwife appointment, called the booking appointment, takes place between 8 and 12 weeks and lasts around an hour. You talk through your health and your options, and you are offered blood tests and screening. The NHS plans at least 10 appointments for a first baby and at least 7 if you have given birth before. The first scan, the dating scan, is offered at around 10 to 14 weeks. A scan earlier than that is not routine, and the NHS says you are unlikely to be offered one at 6 weeks unless you have had IVF or there is a clinical reason such as bleeding or pain.

In the US, call your ob-gyn's or midwife's office once you have a positive test. They will tell you when the first prenatal visit and the first ultrasound are scheduled, since timing varies between practices.

The supplements recommended in early pregnancy should not wait for that first appointment. Folic acid supports the formation of your baby's nervous system, which happens very early, and offers some protection against conditions such as spina bifida. If you have not started, ask a pharmacist, midwife or doctor in the next few days what to take and for how long. If you take any regular medication, do not stop it on your own, and tell your doctor about the pregnancy as soon as possible.

On vaccines, the NHS recommends the flu vaccine at any stage of pregnancy. Your team will go through the others with you at the right time.

## What to do this week

1. Arrange your first appointment if you have not yet, and write down the first day of your last period to take with you.
2. Stop smoking and drinking alcohol, and cut back on coffee, tea, cola and other sources of caffeine. Your midwife or doctor can tell you how much caffeine is reasonable for you and can help if stopping smoking or drinking is hard.
3. Plan your eating around the nausea: small meals, water through the day and a dry snack on the bedside table.
4. Keep moving if you feel up to it. The NHS recommends 150 minutes of exercise a week in pregnancy, and 10 minutes of brisk walking a day is a fine way to start.
5. If anxiety or low mood is weighing on you, talk to someone you trust and bring it up at your appointment. Mental health is part of pregnancy care.

> Get checked without waiting for your appointment if you have vaginal bleeding, even a little, pain or burning when you pee, a severe headache or a fever. With nausea, get advice if you cannot keep food or fluids down for 24 hours, your urine is very dark, you have not peed in more than 8 hours, you feel weak or dizzy when you stand up or you have lost weight. In England, call your GP, an early pregnancy unit or NHS 111; in the US, call your ob-gyn. Call 999 or 911 if you have heavy bleeding, severe pain in your tummy (especially low down on one side), pain in the tip of your shoulder, severe dizziness or fainting. These can be signs of an ectopic pregnancy, which needs fast treatment.
