Straight answer: a 1-month-old drinks a total of **600 to 850 ml (20–28 oz) of formula in 24 hours**, across **7 to 9 feeds** — which works out to **60 to 120 ml (2–4 oz) per feed depending on weight**: closer to 60–85 ml for a 3.5–4 kg baby, reaching 90–120 ml for bigger ones. And here's the part the charts don't tell you: those ranges are wide on purpose, and your baby can be perfectly healthy anywhere inside them (or even slightly outside).

This guide covers only the first full month — the weight-based fine-tuning, the growth spurt that usually shakes everything up between weeks 3 and 6, and the signs the amount is right. If you want every age at once, the complete chart is in our [formula amounts by age guide](/en/blog/how-much-formula-by-age/).

## The right math is by weight, not by age

"1 month old" describes 3.5 kg babies and 5.5 kg babies — and they don't drink the same amount. The practical reference used in pediatrics for healthy full-term babies is **about 150 ml of prepared formula per kilogram of body weight per day** (roughly 2.5 oz per pound) — different guidelines quote ranges from ~120 to 180 ml/kg, so treat 150 as a starting average, not a physical constant:

| Baby's weight | Estimated total in 24 h | In practice (7–9 feeds) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3.5 kg (7.7 lb) | ~525 ml (18 oz) | 60–75 ml per feed |
| 4 kg (8.8 lb) | ~600 ml (20 oz) | 65–85 ml per feed |
| 4.5 kg (9.9 lb) | ~675 ml (23 oz) | 75–95 ml per feed |
| 5 kg (11 lb) | ~750 ml (25 oz) | 85–105 ml per feed |
| 5.5 kg (12 lb) | ~825 ml (28 oz) | 90–120 ml per feed |

Two honest caveats about this chart:

- It's a **starting point, not a target**. A baby isn't a fuel tank: some days they drink more, some days less.
- The number that truly matters isn't in it — it's the **weight curve** your pediatrician tracks at the 1-month checkup.

## The typical rhythm at 1 month

By 1 month, the stomach has outgrown the newborn cherry size, and the rhythm changes with it:

- **Interval between feeds**: 2.5 to 4 hours, on demand — offer when your baby shows hunger cues (rooting, sucking on hands, stirring), without waiting for crying, which is the last cue.
- **Overnight**: if birth weight has been regained and gain is on track, **you don't need to wake the baby** at fixed times to feed. Longer night stretches start appearing at this stage — enjoy them. Common sense still applies: at 1 month, repeated very long fasts (beyond about 5 hours, several times a day) deserve a weight-gain check with the pediatrician.
- **Duration**: 15 to 25 minutes per feed is typical with a slow-flow nipple — still the right choice at this age, so the baby controls their own pace.

## The 3–6 week growth spurt: when everything feels like too little

If the baby who used to feed calmly suddenly **empties every bottle, cries at the end and asks again in 90 minutes**, breathe: between weeks 3 and 6 comes one of the most noticeable growth spurts of the first year. It usually lasts **2 to 4 days** (sometimes a week) and is the most common cause of "my 1-month-old isn't satisfied anymore."

What to do during the spurt:

1. **Add 15 to 30 ml per feed** and watch — don't double the volume at once.
2. Accept that they'll feed **more often** for a few days, including overnight.
3. **Don't switch formulas** on your own because it "doesn't fill them up anymore" — the spurt passes; an unnecessary switch just adds variables.
4. Once it's over, intake settles at a slightly higher level. That's the new normal.

## How to know the amount is right

Forget the bottle for a moment — the reliable indicators are these:

- **Diapers**: at least **6 well-soaked diapers a day** from the first week on.
- **Weight**: average gain of **20 to 30 g per day** in the first trimester, tracked on the curve by the pediatrician (not on the home scale every day).
- **Behavior**: a baby who wakes to feed, sucks actively and settles between feeds is getting enough.

And the signs of **too much**, which also exist: large spit-ups after almost every feed, frequent discomfort and gas, gain far above the curve. The fix is usually simple — slightly smaller volumes, respecting the baby's fullness cues, with a slow-flow nipple. If the spit-up and discomfort persist even with appropriate volumes, don't assume it's "just overfeeding": [reflux](/en/blog/baby-reflux-spit-up-when-its-gerd/) and cow's milk protein allergy (CMPA) look similar and belong in a conversation with the pediatrician. Pressuring a baby to "finish the bottle" is the habit to avoid: responsive-feeding research shows that respecting fullness cues early on protects against excessive weight gain.

> **Emergency (ER, now):** forceful, repeated projectile vomiting — between weeks 3 and 6 it can be pyloric stenosis, which needs immediate evaluation —, deep lethargy (baby hard to wake), signs of dehydration (dry mouth, sunken fontanelle, barely any wet diapers). **Pediatrician visit (without waiting for the routine checkup):** fewer than 6 wet diapers a day, refusing several feeds in a row, excessive sleepiness during feeds, weight gain off the curve.

## Water, tea, and getting the dilution right

Three short rules that prevent the most common mistakes at this stage:

- **No extra water**: correctly prepared formula already covers all of a baby's hydration before 6 months. Extra water at this age displaces milk and — in quantity — can cause water intoxication (a dangerous drop in blood sodium).
- **No tea**: no herbal "remedies" for colic or sleep either. It doesn't hydrate better, doesn't treat anything, and displaces the food the baby needs.
- **Dilution is sacred**: follow the label's ratio exactly (generally 1 level scoop per 30 ml of water — water first, then powder). Over-concentrated formula overloads the kidneys; over-diluted formula undernourishes. Never adjust the ratio to "make it last" or "fill them up more."

One detail that fools many parents: formula-fed babies usually **poop less often and with firmer stools** than breastfed babies. That alone isn't constipation — the warning sign is dry, pellet-like stools with pain and straining, which is worth a visit.

## What about combination feeding?

Everything above applies to babies fed **formula only**. With combination feeding (breast + formula), the numbers drop proportionally — and there's no reliable top-up chart, because nobody knows exactly how much breast milk the baby took first. The practical rule: **offer the breast first**, top up with formula only if the baby is still hungry, and let diapers and the weight curve tell you whether the combination is working. More in our guide on [how to tell your baby is getting enough milk](/en/blog/is-baby-getting-enough-milk/).

## Log it and bring it to the 1-month checkup

The 1-month checkup is exactly where these doubts get resolved — and it's far more productive when you arrive with data instead of memory: feeds per day, average volumes, diapers, night intervals. Logging each bottle as it happens (one tap, no spreadsheet) turns "I think he eats too little" into an objective answer from the pediatrician.
