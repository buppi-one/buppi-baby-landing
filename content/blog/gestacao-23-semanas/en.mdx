**Quick answer:** at 23 weeks you are starting your 6th month, still in the second trimester. Your baby is **about 28.9 cm** long from head to heel and weighs **about 560 g**, roughly the size of a small aubergine (eggplant). Kicks are getting stronger, and in England a 20-week scan that had to be delayed or repeated can be completed up to 23 weeks and 0 days.

"23 weeks" means 23 completed weeks, from 23 weeks and 0 days to 23 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. Your due date is set at 40 weeks, so you have about 17 to go. Converting to months is approximate, which is why your care team talks in weeks.

## How your baby is developing at 23 weeks

The length comes from the NHS week-by-week guide, and the weight is the median on the World Health Organization fetal growth charts, estimated by ultrasound. Both are averages, and other tables give slightly different numbers. Your baby's own measurements are the ones on your scan report, read by your midwife or doctor.

Arms and legs are now in proportion with the rest of the body. According to the NHS, around this week your baby is practising breathing movements and settling into patterns of sleeping and waking. Between 21 and 24 weeks kicks get stronger and the sucking reflex develops. Hearing, which began to develop between 13 and 16 weeks, keeps maturing.

Over the next few weeks you may see your bump move from the outside. First movements are usually felt between 16 and 24 weeks. There is no set number of movements per day, and the NHS advice is to get to know your baby's own rhythm, which is easier to notice when you are sitting still or lying down. If you reach 24 weeks without having felt anything, tell your midwife or doctor, who will check your baby's heartbeat and movements.

## What changes in your body

The typical complaint at this stage is pain on the sides of the bump, known as round ligament pain. The ligaments that hold the womb stretch as it grows, which can cause a twinge or sharp cramp on one side of the lower tummy, sometimes running down into the groin. The NHS says mild pain that goes away when you change position, rest, do a poo or pass wind is probably nothing to worry about. Severe pain needs checking promptly, and so does milder pain that is still there after 30 to 60 minutes of rest.

You may also notice your bump going hard for a few seconds and then relaxing. These are practice contractions, or Braxton Hicks, which can start any time after the first trimester. They are short, irregular, usually painless, and they do not become more frequent. Many women never notice any. Tightenings that come at regular intervals, with cramps, a constant dull ache low in your back or pressure in your pelvis, can be a sign of preterm labour and need to be assessed.

Also common this week:

- Heartburn and indigestion, caused by hormones and the womb pressing on your stomach. It helps to eat smaller meals more often, sit up straight while you eat, stop eating three hours before bed, prop up your head and shoulders in bed, and cut down on caffeine and on rich, fatty or spicy food. Take medicine for heartburn only on the advice of your midwife or doctor.
- Discomfort in your ribs and feeling a little breathless, which the NHS puts down to the rib cage expanding and the growing womb. Severe or sudden breathlessness, or breathlessness with chest pain, needs immediate care. So does bad or persistent pain just below the ribs.
- Colostrum, the first milk, leaking from your breasts. It happens to some women and not to others.
- More vaginal discharge that is thin, clear or milky white and does not smell unpleasant. If it turns yellow or green, smells strong, itches or comes with pain when you pee, book an appointment.

How strongly you feel any of this varies from one pregnancy to the next. You will probably be hungrier, but the NHS points out that in general you do not need extra calories until the third trimester. Advice for your own situation comes from your care team.

## Prenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ by country, so here is England and the United States side by side.

In England (NHS), the routine appointments around now are at 16 weeks and at 28 weeks, with an extra one at 25 weeks if this is your first baby. The 20-week screening scan is usually done between 18 and 21 weeks, and in some cases it can be completed up to 23 weeks and 0 days. If yours has not happened or is waiting to be repeated, call your midwife straight away. The whooping cough vaccine is usually offered around 20 weeks and can be given from 16 weeks, ideally before 32. If you have not had it, ask now. The flu vaccine can be given at any stage, and the RSV vaccine is offered from 28 weeks. A glucose tolerance test for gestational diabetes is done between 24 and 28 weeks, only for women who have risk factors.

In the United States (ACOG and CDC), glucose screening for gestational diabetes is done for everyone between 24 and 28 weeks, so it is likely to be ordered at your next visit. The whooping cough vaccine (Tdap) is given in every pregnancy between 27 and 36 weeks, and the RSV vaccine between 32 and 36 weeks, during the RSV season. Your ob-gyn or midwife will tell you what applies to you.

## What to do this week

1. In England, check that your 20-week scan has been completed. If not, call your midwife today.
2. Ask about the whooping cough vaccine: in England you can have it now, in the US it comes from 27 weeks.
3. Cut down on caffeine, which makes heartburn worse. Coffee, tea and cola all count.
4. Start pelvic floor exercises, which help prevent leaks when you cough or laugh. Your midwife, doctor or a pelvic health physiotherapist can teach you the technique.
5. Ask when your glucose test will be, if one is planned for you, so you can fit it in between 24 and 28 weeks.

> Get medical advice without waiting for your next appointment if you have vaginal bleeding, fluid leaking from your vagina, contractions or cramps at regular intervals, a constant dull ache low in your back, pressure in your pelvis, tummy pain that is severe or does not ease with rest, bad pain just below the ribs, a high temperature, pain or burning when you pee, a severe headache that will not go away, changes in your vision, or sudden swelling of your face, hands or legs. A birth at this stage would be extremely preterm. A baby's chances improve with each week between 23 and 25 weeks, so any sign of labour needs to be assessed straight away, at any time of day or night. If you already feel your baby move every day and the movements slow down or stop, that also needs checking the same day. Call your maternity unit or midwife (UK) or your ob-gyn (US). In an emergency, call 999 or 911.
