One year! The baby who fit in your arms now pulls up to stand, "chats" in their own way, points at everything, and explores the world with endless curiosity. Twelve months closes the first year and opens the door to toddlerhood — with changes in feeding, movement, and communication. This guide covers what a baby typically does at this age, by developmental area, what changes at mealtime, how to encourage development, and — most important — when it's worth talking to the pediatrician.

## What milestones are (and aren't)

Milestones are skills **most babies** (about 75%) already show by a certain age. They're a **reference**, not a deadline. Two reminders apply to any list:

- **Every baby has their own pace.** The range of normal is wide, and arriving a bit earlier or later is usually natural variation.
- **What matters is the progress.** Gaining new skills over the weeks is the best sign, even without ticking every item by the exact date.

For **premature** babies, use **corrected age** (subtracting the weeks of prematurity) for the first 2 years.

## Milestones at 12 months, by area

According to the CDC's revised milestones, around 12 months many babies already:

| Area | What tends to appear at 12 months |
|---|---|
| **Social and emotional** | Plays games with you, like peek-a-boo |
| **Language and communication** | Waves bye-bye; calls a parent by a special name ("mama," "dada"); understands "no" (pauses briefly when you say it) |
| **Cognitive** | Puts something into a container (e.g., a block in a cup); looks for objects they saw you hide (e.g., a toy under a cloth) |
| **Movement/physical** | Pulls up to stand; walks holding on to furniture; drinks from a lidless cup (with you holding it); picks up small things with the pincer grasp (thumb and index finger) |

Remember: these are **examples of what most do** — not a required checklist.

## Standing, cruising, and walking alone

The big motor theme of the first birthday is standing and getting around. In practice:

- **Pulls up to stand** on furniture or your hands — sometimes not knowing how to get back down at first
- **Walks from furniture to furniture** ("cruising"), holding on for support
- **Walking alone** has a **wide window**: many babies take their first steps between **12 and 15 months**, but walking only closer to **18 months** is still within normal (the WHO describes a wide range for independent walking)
- **Crawling** varies a lot — some babies scoot, "bum-shuffle," or skip it entirely and go straight to walking. What matters is that the baby **moves around somehow** and uses both sides of the body similarly

In other words: **not walking at 12 months usually isn't cause for concern**. What deserves attention is the baby who, at 12 months, doesn't stand with support, doesn't bear weight on their legs, or moves one side of the body very differently from the other.

## First words and gestures

Communication takes a leap around age 1:

- **First words**: many babies say **1 to 3 little words** besides "mama/dada" (with meaning) — but the variation is huge, and some talk a bit later
- **Gestures** are as important as words: **waving bye-bye**, **pointing** at what they want, shaking the head. Gestures show communication is developing
- **Understands more than they say**: recognizes their own name, understands "no," follows simple requests ("give it to me")
- **Object permanence**: looks for things they saw you hide — a sign memory and reasoning are advancing

If at 12 months the baby **makes no gestures** (no waving, no pointing) and **says no words at all**, it's worth mentioning to the pediatrician — the absence of gestures usually weighs more than the absence of words.

## What changes at mealtime at 12 months

The first birthday brings practical feeding changes:

- **Whole cow's milk** can be introduced from age 1 (before that, it's not recommended as the main drink). **Breastfeeding can continue** as long as the family wishes. But **don't overdo it**: more than about **500 ml (17 oz) of cow's milk a day** hinders iron absorption and can lead to anemia
- Solids take more space: the baby now eats more **family food** (adapted, **with no salt and no added sugar** — the recommendation is zero sugar until age 2 —, safely cut) and practices **self-feeding** with hands and the pincer grasp
- It's the stage to **gradually swap the bottle for a cup** — the goal is to move off the bottle over the second year
- With the **first teeth**, oral hygiene begins: brush with a tiny amount of fluoride toothpaste and see a pediatric dentist around age 1

As always, the pace and choices of this transition are individual: confirm with your pediatrician.

## How to encourage development (through play)

At 12 months, the best stimulation is still interaction:

- **Peekaboo games** — they reinforce object permanence
- **Naming everything** and responding to babbles and gestures as if they were a conversation
- **Reading together** every day, letting the baby turn pages and point at pictures
- **Stacking, nesting, putting objects in and out** of containers — works coordination and the pincer grasp
- **Giving safe space to move**: a clear floor to crawl, stand, and cruise beats any baby walker (which, in fact, isn't recommended)
- **No screens**: the recommendation (AAP and pediatric societies) is to avoid screens before age 2 — what truly develops the baby is human contact

## When to talk to the pediatrician

Milestones are flexible, but some signs deserve attention. According to the CDC, mention it to the pediatrician if, at 12 months, the baby:

- **Doesn't move around** in any way (no crawling, no scooting, no cruising)
- **Doesn't stand** when supported
- **Doesn't look** for objects they saw you hide
- **Doesn't say** any little word like "mama" or "dada"
- **Doesn't learn gestures**, like waving or shaking the head
- **Doesn't point** at things
- **Doesn't respond** to their own name or look you in the eye consistently
- **Has lost skills** they once had

> **Important:** losing a skill the baby had mastered is always a reason to talk to the pediatrician promptly. And trust your instinct: if something in the development worries you, even without a specific item from the list, bring it to the visit. Checking early opens doors — when stimulation or follow-up is needed, starting soon makes a big difference.

## What to expect next

The second year is an explosion: first steps turn into running, vocabulary grows fast, the first tantrums appear (a normal part of developing autonomy), and the child wants to do everything "by myself." **Separation anxiety** tends to peak between about 10 and 18 months and eases through the second year as the child gains confidence.

For now, at 12 months, celebrate: closing the first year is quite a milestone — for the baby and for you. Follow the progress more than the exact date of each achievement, and count on the pediatrician to confirm everything is on track.
