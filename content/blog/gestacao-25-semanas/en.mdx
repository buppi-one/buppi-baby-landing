**Quick answer:** at 25 weeks you are in the 6th month and close to the end of the 2nd trimester. Your baby is **about 34.6 cm** from head to heel and weighs **about 780 g**, roughly the size of a cauliflower. This is the window for the glucose test, and a good time to keep an eye on swelling and blood pressure.

"Twenty-five weeks" means twenty-five completed weeks, from 25 weeks and 0 days to 25 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. The due date is set at 40 weeks, so there are about 15 to go. Converting weeks to months is approximate, which is why your care team always talks in weeks.

## How your baby is developing at 25 weeks

The length of about 34.6 cm is measured from head to heel and comes from the NHS week-by-week guide. The weight of about 780 g is the median on the World Health Organization fetal growth charts, estimated by ultrasound. Both are averages, and your own baby's measurements are the ones in your scan report.

The NHS describes this as an active time. A loud noise can make your baby jump and kick, which does not hurt but may catch you off guard. Some women also notice short, rhythmic twitches, which are hiccups.

Your baby pees into the amniotic fluid. By now most of the fluid around them is urine, which cushions their movements and helps keep the temperature steady.

Between 25 and 28 weeks, according to the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG), the eyelids begin to open and the lungs start making surfactant, the substance that later lets them fill with air. The lungs still have a lot of maturing to do before the end of pregnancy. The World Health Organization classes a birth before 28 weeks as extremely preterm, which is why the signs of early labour in the warning box below need prompt attention.

## What is changing in your body

Your uterus has grown and now presses on your stomach and bowel. That explains feeling full after a small meal, the burping, the heartburn and the bloating. The NHS suggests smaller meals, sitting up straight when you eat, cutting back on rich, fatty and spicy food and going easy on caffeine. Medicine for heartburn or constipation should only be taken on the advice of your midwife or doctor.

Your face, hands and feet may get a little puffy. According to the NHS this is most likely water retention, but only a blood pressure check can confirm that. Mention it at your appointment even if the swelling is mild. Pre-eclampsia is a form of high blood pressure that can appear from 20 weeks and is more common in the third trimester, and swelling that comes on suddenly is one of its signs. The others are in the warning box below.

Other common symptoms at this stage:

- Sharp twinges at the sides of the bump or in the groin, caused by the ligaments that support the uterus being stretched. They tend to come when you stand up quickly, cough or turn over in bed, and they pass quickly. Pain that is severe, does not ease or comes at regular intervals needs checking.
- Backache, which tends to be worse after a long time on your feet.
- Swollen gums that bleed when you brush. Keep brushing and flossing, and book a dentist.
- Constipation and piles (haemorrhoids).
- Leg cramps, feeling hot, dizziness and broken sleep.
- Dark patches on the face and stretch marks.

Not every pregnant woman gets all of these, and the intensity changes from one pregnancy to the next. Burning when you pee, or discharge that smells strong or itches, is not part of the expected picture and should be checked without waiting for your next routine appointment.

## Antenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ by country, so here is what applies in England and what applies in the US.

In England, the NHS plans at least 10 appointments for a first baby and at least 7 if you have given birth before. There is an appointment at 25 weeks if this is your first baby. Otherwise the next one is at 28 weeks. In the US, your ob-gyn or midwife sets the visit schedule.

The main test at this stage is the glucose test, done between 24 and 28 weeks. It looks for gestational diabetes, which usually causes no symptoms. In the US it is a routine test for all pregnant women. In England it is offered only to women with a higher chance of gestational diabetes, so ask your midwife whether it applies to you. You drink a very sweet liquid and have blood taken afterwards. Your care team interprets the result and explains the next steps if it comes back raised.

For the whooping cough vaccine, the two countries differ. The NHS usually offers it around week 20; it can be given from week 16 and works best before week 32, so if you have not had it yet, ask now. In the US, the CDC and ACOG recommend it between 27 and 36 weeks, as early in that window as possible. Both repeat it in every pregnancy. The flu vaccine is recommended during flu season at any stage of pregnancy in both countries. The RSV vaccine comes later: from 28 weeks in England, and at 32 to 36 weeks in the US, where it is seasonal.

## What to do this week

1. Check whether a glucose test is planned for you and how to prepare. The window runs to 28 weeks.
2. Find out which maternity unit or hospital you would go to in an emergency, how to get there and which number to call. Save it on your phone.
3. If you work in the UK and have not told your employer yet, do it now. The NHS gives 15 weeks before the due date as the deadline, which is about this week.
4. Start on anything that depends on delivery times, such as furniture or painting the room. Clothes can wait, and our [checklist for the baby's arrival](/en/blog/preparing-for-baby-arrival/) helps you sort out what is essential.
5. Keep moving. The general recommendation is about 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, and walking counts. Pelvic floor exercises may help prevent leaks when you cough or sneeze. Check with your care team what suits you.

> Get checked without waiting for your next appointment if you have vaginal bleeding, fluid leaking from the vagina, cramps or contractions that come at regular intervals, severe abdominal pain, a fever or pain when you pee. A severe headache that does not go away, blurred vision or seeing spots, pain just below the ribs and sudden swelling of your face, hands or legs can be signs of high blood pressure in pregnancy and need assessment the same day, and so does a blood pressure reading of 140/90 or higher. If you notice your baby moving less than usual or not moving, get assessed too, without waiting until the next day. Call your midwife or maternity unit in the UK, or your ob-gyn or labor and delivery unit in the US. For a seizure, fainting, trouble breathing or heavy bleeding, call 999 in the UK or 911 in the US.
