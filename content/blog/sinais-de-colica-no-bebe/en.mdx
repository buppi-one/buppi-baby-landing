It's 3 a.m. The baby has been crying for over an hour, you've already tried the breast, the diaper, holding, motion — nothing. The first question that comes to mind is the hardest: **is this colic, or is something wrong?** This guide was written to answer exactly that. Short, direct, with bullets, so you can read it in half-light.

The good news: in most cases it's infant colic — a common, self-limiting phase with no identifiable medical cause. But there are specific signs that point to other things, and knowing how to tell them apart is what gives you the peace to wait it out (most nights) or to head to the ER (the few when you really need to).

## The Rome IV criteria — the definition pediatricians use today

In 2016, an international consensus in pediatric gastroenterology (called **Rome IV**) updated the definition of infant colic. Four criteria need to be present together:

- Baby **under 5 months** when symptoms start and end
- **Recurrent and prolonged** episodes of crying, fussing or irritability that occur **without obvious cause** and that caregivers cannot prevent or soothe
- **No evidence** of failure to thrive, fever or illness
- (For research diagnosis) crying or fussing for **3 or more hours/day** on **3 or more days** within a week

If you've heard of the **"rule of 3"** (3h/day, 3 days a week, for 3 weeks), that comes from a 1954 study by Dr. Morris Wessel. It's still cited, but Rome IV is simpler and more widely used in clinical practice today: the key point became **inconsolable crying, without apparent cause, in an otherwise healthy baby**.

## The classic behavioral signs

Colic isn't just "a baby crying a lot". There's a cluster of signs that usually appear together:

- **Intense, high-pitched crying, louder than usual** — many parents describe it as "different", almost pain-like
- **Legs drawn up toward the abdomen**, in a pedaling motion or stretched out tightly
- **Tense belly**, sometimes visibly distended during the crisis
- **Clenched fists**, rigid arms, arched back
- **Reddened face**, furrowed brow
- **Passing gas** during or shortly after the episode
- **Hard to soothe** even at the breast, in arms, with motion or a pacifier — the baby may seem to settle for a few seconds and start crying again

These signs don't all have to be present at the same time. The typical cluster is **tense posture + inconsolable crying + no clear trigger** (not hunger, not the diaper, not sleep, not visible pain).

## The time-of-day pattern: why it's almost always late afternoon/evening

One of the most consistent hallmarks of colic is its **timing**:

- Episodes usually appear **between late afternoon and early evening** — typically from 4 p.m. to midnight
- Babies with colic generally have **long stretches of calm** during the day
- They feed well between crises, gain weight, and have normal diapers
- Parents learn to "expect" the start of the crisis almost by the clock

If your baby's crying doesn't follow this concentrated pattern — if it's spread throughout the day with no calm windows — it's worth a conversation with the pediatrician to check for another cause.

## Onset, peak, and when it ends

The typical timeline:

| Age | What to expect |
|---|---|
| **0–2 weeks** | Usually not yet. Intense crying at this stage warrants evaluation. |
| **2–6 weeks** | Most common onset of episodes |
| **~6–8 weeks** | Peak intensity and duration |
| **3–4 months** | Spontaneous resolution in the vast majority of cases |
| **>5 months** | No longer classified as infant colic; another cause should be investigated |

Prevalence is between **17% and 25%** of healthy babies — nearly **1 in 4**. It's common, self-limiting, and leaves no sequelae.

## Colic vs normal crying: the quick table

Every baby cries — on average, **2 to 3 hours a day** between weeks 2 and 6, even without colic. The differences that help:

| Feature | Colic | Normal crying |
|---|---|---|
| **Time of day** | Concentrated late afternoon/evening | Distributed through the day |
| **Trigger** | No obvious cause | Responds to hunger, diaper, sleep, cold |
| **Posture** | Legs drawn up, tense belly, clenched fists | More relaxed |
| **Response to soothing** | Hard to calm even with breast/arms | Calms when the cause is addressed |
| **Episode duration** | 1–3 hours | Minutes |
| **Between episodes** | Calm baby, feeding well | Variable |

## Warning signs: when it's NOT colic

Colic is a diagnosis of exclusion. The following signs are **not part of the picture** and require medical evaluation — some urgently:

**See the pediatrician or ER urgently if:**

- **Fever ≥38°C (100.4°F)** rectal in a baby under 3 months — any fever at this age is an immediate evaluation per AAP and SBP guidelines
- **Projectile vomiting** or repeated vomiting (different from mild spit-up)
- **Blood in stool** or persistent diarrhea
- **Lethargy**: floppy baby, weak, hard to wake
- **Refusal to feed** for more than one consecutive feeding
- **Persistent abdominal distension** — a hard, swollen belly that doesn't relax even between crying episodes
- **Crying with a tone clearly different from usual**, or a sudden change in crying pattern
- **No weight gain** or weight loss between visits
- **Fewer than 6 wet diapers a day** after the first week of life
- **Breathing difficulty**, pallor, or cyanosis (bluish lips/extremities) during crying
- **Signs of trauma**: recent fall, unexplained bruising

These signs may indicate infection, pathological gastroesophageal reflux, cow's milk protein allergy, intussusception, incarcerated hernia, urinary tract infection, or other causes that need investigation. **None of them are typical of colic.**

> **Practical rule**: colic is intense crying in a baby who is **healthy in every other respect**. If there's fever, weight loss, repeated vomiting, blood or lethargy — don't assume it's colic. See the pediatrician.

## What to try before assuming it's colic

When the baby starts crying inconsolably, it's worth a quick check of the usual suspects:

1. **Hunger** — when was the last feeding? A few short feedings in a row may mean they're still hungry.
2. **Diaper** — wet or soiled, even if changed recently
3. **Temperature** — a baby who's hot or cold cries; feel the back of the neck or chest (not the hands) to check
4. **Position** — tight clothing, a scratchy tag, a hair wrapped around a finger (yes, this happens and it really hurts)
5. **Sleep** — an overtired baby past their sleep window often cries from exhaustion
6. **Visible pain** — check skin, diaper area, fingers, ears, mouth

If none of that helps and the baby keeps crying intensely, with the behavioral signs described above, in the typical time-of-day pattern, in a healthy baby with good weight gain — it's most likely colic.

## What to track for the pediatrician

Two weeks of records are worth more than any from-memory description at 3 a.m. What makes a difference at the appointment:

- **Start and end times** of each intense crying episode
- **Total crying time** per day (summing the episodes)
- **Time-of-day pattern** — concentrated in any window?
- **Posture** during the crisis — legs drawn up? tense belly?
- **What was tried** and what (if anything) worked: breast, holding, motion, warm bath
- **Feedings**: number, duration, interval, whether the baby seems satisfied
- **Diapers**: number of wet and stooled per day, color and consistency of stools
- **Weekly weight gain** (a pharmacy scale works, always same clothes/time)
- **Any out-of-pattern symptom** (even if it seems small): spit-ups, gas, change in crying

Baby tracker apps (like Buppi) make this logging effortless — you log with one tap as it happens and bring the two-week chart to the appointment. No memory required.

## Why confirming the diagnosis matters

It may sound counterintuitive, but **correctly identifying colic is a relief**, not a sentence. Recognizing the pattern changes three things:

1. **Reduces parental anxiety** — knowing it's not a serious illness, that it's a phase, that it passes, that it's common, lowers the emotional guard. And calmer parents care better.
2. **Avoids unnecessary interventions** — switching formula without indication, restrictive maternal diets, teas or unprescribed medications usually make things worse rather than better.
3. **Keeps your radar on for change** — if what looked like colic starts to evolve with warning signs, you notice faster because you know your baby's "normal" pattern.

## If you feel you're losing control

Inconsolable crying is one of the leading triggers of **shaken baby syndrome (abusive head trauma)** — a severe brain injury that can cause permanent damage or death even after just a few seconds. If you or another caregiver feels close to the limit:

1. **Place the baby somewhere safe** — in the crib on their back, with no pillow, loose blankets or objects nearby.
2. **Leave the room for 5 to 10 minutes.** Breathe. A baby crying alone in a safe crib is safer than a caregiver at the breaking point.
3. **Call someone** — partner, grandparent, friend, neighbor, support line. Don't go through it alone.
4. **NEVER shake the baby.** Even a light shake can cause brain injury.

Asking for help isn't weakness — it's part of caring well. If feelings of despair, extreme exhaustion, or disconnection from the baby persist beyond the crises, talk to your obstetrician, pediatrician or a mental health service: postpartum depression is common, treatable, and recovery is usually quick with support.

## When it ends, and what to expect after

For the vast majority of babies, colic **resolves spontaneously between months 3 and 4**, as the nervous system and digestive tract mature. Colic **does not impair growth or neurodevelopment** — babies who had colic do not differ from others in development, bonding, or long-term weight gain.

Until it passes, the best you can do is **share the load**, ask for help, keep the log, and care for the caregivers. The most useful sentence of these weeks, obvious as it may be: **this will pass**.
