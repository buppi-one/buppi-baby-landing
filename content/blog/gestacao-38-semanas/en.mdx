**Quick answer:** at 38 weeks you are in the 9th month, in the third trimester, and your baby is early term, so no longer preterm if born now. Your baby is **about 49.8 cm** long from head to heel and weighs **about 3.2 kg**, roughly the length of a leek. Meconium, which will be the first poo, is building up in the bowel, and your body may start showing signs that the birth is near.

"Thirty-eight weeks" means 38 completed weeks, from 38 weeks and 0 days to 38 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. Your due date is set at 40 weeks, so about 2 weeks remain. In practice, labour can start before or after that date.

## Your baby at 38 weeks

The length comes from the NHS week-by-week guide (the NHS is the UK's public health service) and is measured from head to heel. The weight is the median of the World Health Organization fetal growth charts. On those charts, 2 in 10 babies fall outside the 2.7 kg to 3.7 kg range, and a different estimated weight on a scan does not mean a problem on its own. Your prenatal team is the one to interpret it.

Under ACOG's definition, a pregnancy from 37 weeks 0 days to 38 weeks 6 days is early term, and full term runs from 39 weeks to 40 weeks 6 days. A baby born now is not preterm, but ACOG notes that the lungs and brain keep maturing right up to the final weeks.

According to the NHS, the lanugo, the fine downy hair that covered the skin in the second trimester, has mostly gone. Some babies are still born with a few patches.

The bowel is storing meconium, a sticky dark green substance made of what the baby swallows in the womb, such as amniotic fluid and shed hairs. It normally comes out after the birth as the first poo. If the baby passes meconium during labour, the NHS explains that it can be a sign of distress, and the team will monitor the baby closely. So if your waters break, check the colour of the fluid and contact your maternity unit or hospital promptly, telling them what you saw.

Movements feel different because there is less space, but they should not decrease. The Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists explains that movements increase until about 32 weeks and then stay about the same until birth. What matters is your baby's usual pattern.

## Changes in your body

The NHS mentions a new symptom this week: frustration or boredom with the wait. It is common. If anxiety or low mood starts to weigh on your days, talk to your midwife or doctor.

Other things the NHS lists for this stage:

- Braxton Hicks (practice) contractions, which tighten the bump and are usually painless.
- Trouble sleeping, backache and pains on the side of the bump.
- Heartburn, indigestion, constipation and piles.
- Leg cramps, feeling hot and dizziness. A severe headache that does not go away is a warning sign (see the box further down).
- Swollen hands and feet. Swelling that appears suddenly, especially in the face, needs checking straight away.
- Sore breasts or leaking a little colostrum, the first milk.
- Swollen, bleeding gums and dark patches on the face.
- Urine or vaginal infections. Burning when you pee or a change in discharge should be checked.

For any pain or discomfort, do not take medicines on your own: in pregnancy, only with advice from whoever provides your prenatal care.

Before labour itself, tightenings often become more frequent while staying irregular and short, and the mucus plug may come away. That is not established labour yet. The NHS advises calling your midwife or maternity unit when contractions are regular and come about every 5 minutes or more often. If your pregnancy is high risk, you have a planned caesarean or you live far from the hospital, agree with your team in advance on when to leave home. Every pregnancy has its own pace, and labour may start this week or not until after 40 weeks.

## Prenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ by country, so here are England and the United States separately.

In England, the NHS schedule includes an appointment at 38 weeks, and another at 40 weeks for a first baby. The NHS says that at this appointment your midwife or doctor measures your bump with a tape measure, checks your blood pressure and tests your urine for protein, which could point to pre-eclampsia. If you have a planned caesarean, it is usually booked at 39 weeks or later to give the baby's lungs the best chance to develop. You will also talk about what happens if you go past 41 weeks, when you may be offered an induction. The RSV vaccine, which protects babies against bronchiolitis, is offered from 28 weeks and can still be given up until labour: if you have not had it, ask your midwife or GP.

In the United States, ACOG notes that testing may be recommended at 41 weeks and induction may be recommended at that point too. Screening for group B strep is done for everyone between 36 weeks 0 days and 37 weeks 6 days: if you have not had the swab, ask your ob-gyn. The maternal RSV vaccine is only given up to 36 weeks 6 days, so if you did not get it, your baby can be protected with an infant immunisation after birth instead.

## What to do this week

1. Keep your hospital bag by the door, with your maternity notes and birth plan. The NHS suggests taking your notes with you whenever you go out.
2. Agree with your birth partner who will drive you and how. The NHS warns that it is not safe to drive yourself in labour.
3. Save in your phone, and on paper, the number and address of your maternity unit or hospital.
4. If you are still working, plan when you stop. Rules on leave differ between the UK and the US, so check what applies to you.
5. Rest when you can, and go for gentle walks if you feel well.

> Contact your maternity unit without waiting for your next appointment if you have regular contractions about every 5 minutes or more often, fluid leaking from your vagina (note the colour), vaginal bleeding, a fever, or pain or burning when you pee. Call straight away if your baby is moving less than usual or the pattern of movements changes. A severe headache that does not go away, blurred vision or flashing lights, severe pain just below the ribs and sudden swelling of the face, hands or legs need immediate assessment. Call your midwife or maternity unit in the UK, or your ob-gyn in the US. For a seizure, fainting, severe breathlessness, chest pain or heavy bleeding, call 999 in the UK or 911 in the US.
