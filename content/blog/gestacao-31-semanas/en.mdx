**Quick answer:** at 31 weeks you are in the last week of the 7th month, in the 3rd trimester. Your baby is **about 41.1 cm** long from head to heel and weighs **about 1.7 kg**, roughly the size of a coconut. Your baby is putting on fat every day, your bump tightens now and then with practice contractions, and at your appointments the care team starts checking which way your baby is lying.

"Thirty-one weeks" means 31 completed weeks, from 31 weeks and 0 days to 31 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. The due date is set at 40 weeks, so there are about 9 to go. It is an estimate, and the birth may come a little earlier or later.

## How your baby is developing at 31 weeks

The length comes from the NHS week-by-week guide and is measured from head to heel. The weight is the median of the World Health Organization fetal growth charts. Both are reference values, and a scan estimate outside the 1.5 kg to 2.0 kg range does not point to a problem by itself: your care team looks at how the measurements change between scans and decides whether closer follow-up is needed.

According to the NHS, your baby is getting plumper day by day and the skin looks less wrinkled. Another growth spurt is expected in a couple of weeks.

Your baby moves a lot, sucks their fingers and turns the odd somersault. The amount of amniotic fluid around them is increasing, and the NHS points out that the baby's pee adds to it.

The NHS also says your baby is starting to recognise voices from outside the womb, which is why the guide suggests that your partner and any other children talk to your bump.

Movements tend to become more frequent until around 32 weeks and then stay about the same, without dropping off at the end of pregnancy. With less room they may feel different, but your baby's usual pattern is still the reference. If your baby moves less than usual, contact your maternity unit or ob-gyn the same day.

Under the World Health Organization classification, a baby born between 28 and just under 32 weeks is very preterm and needs care in a neonatal unit.

## What is changing in your body

Your bump may go hard for 20 to 30 seconds and then relax. These are practice contractions, also called Braxton Hicks, which the NHS describes as normal and harmless and which should not hurt. If they hurt or start coming at regular intervals, even without pain, they could be a sign of early labour. In that case, call your maternity unit or ob-gyn to be assessed without waiting for your next appointment.

Climbing the stairs without getting out of breath takes more effort. The NHS describes this mild breathlessness at weeks 29 and 30, explains that the baby is pushing against your lungs and says it tends to ease once the baby moves down into the pelvis. Breathlessness that is severe, comes on suddenly or arrives with chest pain is a warning sign and needs immediate care.

For sleep, the NHS recommends lying on your side, with one pillow under your bump and another between your legs.

Other symptoms the NHS lists for this week:

- Heartburn, indigestion, bloating and constipation.
- Backache, leg cramps and pains at the side of your bump, caused by your expanding womb.
- Swollen hands and feet, feeling hot and dizziness.
- Urine and vaginal infections, which always need checking. Tell your care team if it hurts or burns when you pee or if your discharge changes.
- Headaches, poor sleep, piles and bleeding gums.

How strong these are varies from one woman to another and from one pregnancy to the next.

## Antenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ by country, so here is what applies in England and what applies in the US.

In England, the NHS schedules an antenatal appointment at 31 weeks if this is your first baby. The next ones are at 34 and 36 weeks for everyone. According to the NHS, at this stage your midwife or doctor will probably measure your bump with a tape, from the top of the pubic bone to the top of the bump, to estimate the size of your womb. They also gently feel your belly to work out which way up your baby is. Some babies are already head down. A baby who is still head up has time to turn, and some only engage in the pelvis when labour starts. If your baby stays bottom-down later on, or if the placenta is blocking the way out, your midwife or doctor may recommend a caesarean and will go through the risks and benefits with you before you decide.

On vaccines in England, the RSV vaccine is offered from 28 weeks in every pregnancy and can still be given later, up to labour. RSV is a common cause of bronchiolitis. The whooping cough vaccine is usually offered around week 20 and is best had before 32 weeks. You can still have it later, but if you have not had it yet, speak to your midwife or GP this week.

In the US, the CDC and ACOG recommend the whooping cough (Tdap) vaccine in every pregnancy between 27 and 36 weeks, as early in that window as possible. The maternal RSV vaccine is a single dose given between 32 and 36 weeks, so the window opens next week. It is seasonal and is not repeated in later pregnancies, so ask your provider whether it applies to your dates. Screening for group B strep comes later, between 36 and 37 weeks.

The flu vaccine is recommended at any stage of pregnancy in both countries. If a vaccine has been missed, talk to your care team about catching up now, because the antibodies need time to reach your baby through the placenta.

## What to do this week

1. Take your vaccination record to your appointment and go through what is still due: whooping cough and RSV in England, Tdap now and RSV from next week in the US, plus flu.
2. Ask which way your baby is lying at your next check.
3. Look into antenatal classes if you have not booked any. The NHS suggests asking your midwife or doctor, who may be able to recommend one, and inviting your partner.
4. Start packing your hospital bag. Ask your maternity unit or hospital what to bring, because the list changes from one place to another.
5. Decide where your baby will sleep. The NHS advises a crib, carry cot or Moses basket with a mattress that is clean, waterproof and fits snugly with no gaps, and no pillows or duvets, which can make a baby overheat or suffocate.

> Get checked without waiting for your next appointment if you have vaginal bleeding, fluid leaking from your vagina, contractions that hurt or come at regular intervals, strong pressure low in your belly, a fever, or pain or burning when you pee. Call straight away if your baby is moving less than usual or the pattern of movements changes. A severe headache that does not go away, blurred vision or flashing lights, severe pain just below the ribs and sudden swelling of the face, hands or legs are signs of high blood pressure in pregnancy and need immediate assessment. Call your midwife or maternity unit in the UK, or your ob-gyn in the US. For a seizure, fainting, severe breathlessness, chest pain or heavy bleeding, call 999 in the UK or 911 in the US.
