**Quick answer:** at 18 weeks you are starting the 5th month, in the middle of the 2nd trimester. Your baby is **about 14.2 cm** from head to bottom and weighs **about 220 g**, roughly the size of a bell pepper. Many women feel the first movements around now, and this is the week to check the date of your mid-pregnancy scan.

"Eighteen weeks" means eighteen completed weeks, from 18 weeks and 0 days to 18 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. The due date is set at 40 weeks, so there are about 22 to go. Converting weeks to months is approximate, and your care team always talks in weeks.

## How your baby is developing at 18 weeks

The length comes from the NHS week-by-week guide and is measured from head to bottom because the legs are curled up. The weight is the median on the World Health Organization fetal growth charts. Both figures are averages, and the reference for your own baby is the estimate from your scan, read by the team looking after you.

According to the NHS, hearing, the sense of touch and the swallowing and sucking reflexes are developing this week. Between 17 and 20 weeks the body also becomes covered in fine, downy hair called lanugo, and your baby has recognisable features on a scan.

Your baby moves a lot, bending and stretching arms and legs. Now that the baby is bigger, you may start to notice.

### The first movements

Women usually describe the feeling as bubbles or a light flutter inside the belly, easy to mistake for wind. First movements are generally felt between 16 and 24 weeks. In a first pregnancy they often come after week 20, and women who have had a baby before tend to recognise them earlier.

Feeling nothing at 18 weeks is expected. At this stage there is also no pattern to follow and no movements to count. If you reach 24 weeks without having felt any, tell your midwife or doctor.

## What changes in your body

Your bump is growing and shifting your balance, so it is common to feel clumsier. Your breasts may have gone up a size, especially in a first pregnancy.

Back pain affects many pregnant women. The NHS explains that the ligaments in your body soften and stretch to prepare for labour, which puts a strain on the joints of your lower back and pelvis. A few simple habits usually help:

- Bend your knees and keep your back straight when you pick something up, and avoid lifting heavy things.
- Wear flat shoes, which spread your weight more evenly.
- Sit with your back supported, and turn your whole body by moving your feet instead of twisting your spine.

If the pain is bad enough to get in the way of your day, bring it up at your appointment: your midwife or doctor can suggest exercises or refer you to a physiotherapist. Back pain that comes with a high temperature, bleeding, pain when you pee or pain in your side under the ribs needs to be checked the same day. So does new back pain with cramps, pressure in your pelvis or tightening of your bump.

Dizziness when you stand up is another complaint at this stage. Your blood pressure tends to be a little lower than before pregnancy, and jumping up from the sofa or the bed can make your vision go dark. Get up slowly, hold on to something and sit back down if you feel dizzy. If it keeps happening, mention it at your appointment. Fainting needs urgent medical attention.

You may also notice a dark vertical line down the middle of your belly, the linea nigra. It is skin pigmentation, it does not point to a problem and it usually fades a few months after the birth. Brown patches on the face are also common in pregnancy.

Other common symptoms at 18 weeks:

- Sharp twinges at the side of your belly, from the stretching of the ligaments that support the womb.
- Leg cramps and feeling hot.
- Swollen, bleeding gums and nosebleeds. In England, NHS dental care is free during pregnancy.
- Slightly swollen hands and feet, more so at the end of the day. Swelling that comes on suddenly in your face, hands or legs needs to be checked.

You will probably feel hungrier, and the NHS points out that there is no need to eat for two. No single number of kilos or pounds fits everyone at 18 weeks: expected weight gain depends on your weight before pregnancy, and your care team gives advice that fits you.

## Prenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ by country, so here is what applies in England and what applies in the US.

In England, the routine appointment of this period is the one at 16 weeks. The next is at 25 weeks if this is your first baby, or at 28 weeks if you have given birth before. Between them comes the 20-week screening scan, also called the anomaly scan. The screening programme offers it between 18 weeks and 20 weeks and 6 days, and in some cases it can be completed up to 23 weeks and 0 days. It looks at your baby's anatomy in detail, including the head, spine, heart, abdomen and limbs. It picks up a range of conditions, though not all of them, and anything found leads to a conversation with your midwife or doctor about next steps. The sonographer can often see the sex, depending on the baby's position and the hospital's policy.

In the US, your ob-gyn or midwife sets the timing of the mid-pregnancy ultrasound, so ask at your next visit when yours is planned.

On vaccines, the NHS usually offers the whooping cough vaccine around week 20 and can give it from week 16. In the US, the CDC and ACOG recommend it between 27 and 36 weeks of every pregnancy. The flu vaccine is recommended in pregnancy at any stage. Further ahead, between 24 and 28 weeks, comes the glucose test for gestational diabetes: in the US it is offered to everyone, and in England only to women with risk factors.

## What to do this week

1. Check the date of your mid-pregnancy scan. In England the screening window opens this week and runs to 20 weeks and 6 days. If you have no appointment letter yet, call your maternity unit. In the US, ask your ob-gyn when the scan is planned.
2. Ask about antenatal classes. The NHS suggests asking your midwife or doctor which ones are available near you or online, and they are worth doing even if you have had a baby before.
3. Keep moving. The general recommendation is about 150 minutes of moderate activity a week. Avoid contact sports and activities with a risk of falling, and check with your care team what suits your situation.
4. Start pelvic floor exercises. Squeeze the muscles you would use to stop the flow of pee, hold for a few seconds and release. These exercises can help prevent leaks when you cough, sneeze or laugh.

> Get medical advice without waiting for your next appointment if you have vaginal bleeding, fluid leaking from your vagina, strong abdominal pain, cramps or tightening of your bump that keep coming back, a high temperature, pain or burning when you pee, a severe headache or changes in your vision. Call your maternity unit or midwife (UK) or your ob-gyn (US). Fainting, shortness of breath, chest pain, heavy bleeding or loss of feeling in your legs are an emergency: call 999 or 911.
