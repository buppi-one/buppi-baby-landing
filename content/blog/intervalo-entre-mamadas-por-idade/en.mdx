Quick answer: a **breastfed newborn** usually nurses **8 to 12 times or more in 24 hours**, about **every 2 hours** on average with plenty of variation, and a **formula-fed newborn** eats **every 2 to 3 hours**. With a bottle, the interval tends to lengthen steadily: 3 to 4 hours around 2 months, 3.5 to 4.5 hours near 6 months. At the breast, spacing is slow and very individual. Between 1 and 6 months, healthy babies nurse anywhere from 6 to 18 times a day.

This guide is about **frequency**: how often and how many times a day. If your question is **how many ounces or ml** per feed, the right place is our [formula-by-age guide](/en/blog/how-much-formula-by-age/).

Intervals are counted **from the start of one feed to the start of the next**, and they are averages, not a timetable to follow.

## Breastfeeding: frequency by age

With breastfeeding, the standard is **feeding on demand**: your baby nurses when they show hunger, not by the clock. It still helps to know what to expect:

| Age | Feeds in 24 h | Approximate interval |
|---|---|---|
| **First month** | 8 to 12 or more | about every 2 h on average, with variation |
| **1 to 6 months** | 11 on average, normal range 6 to 18 | varies widely from baby to baby |
| **6 to 12 months** | on demand, alongside solid food | meals gradually take up more room |

Why isn't there a row for every month? Because at the breast, spacing is slow and individual. In a study of 71 exclusively breastfed babies aged 1 to 6 months, the average was **11 feeds a day, but some healthy babies fed 6 times and others 18**. The number of feeds also had no relationship with the total amount of milk the mother produced. Every mother-baby pair finds its own rhythm, and nursing often at 4 months doesn't mean you're short on milk.

## Formula: interval by age

With a bottle, the interval tends to lengthen more steadily, because the volume per feed grows. Even so, your baby's hunger cues are in charge, not the clock: the table shows what's common, not a time to wait for.

| Age | Typical interval | Feeds in 24 h |
|---|---|---|
| **0 to 2 weeks** | every 2 to 3 h | 8 to 12 |
| **1 month** | every 2.5 to 4 h | 7 to 9 |
| **2 months** | every 3 to 4 h | 6 to 8 |
| **3 to 4 months** | every 3 to 4 h | 5 to 7 |
| **5 to 6 months** | every 3.5 to 4.5 h | 4 to 6 |
| **6 to 12 months** | between meals | 4 to 5 at 6 months; 3 to 5 in the second half of the year |

The feeds-per-day count already allows for the night stretch usually being longer than daytime intervals. That's why it doesn't always match 24 hours divided by the interval. For the volume of each feed, see the [formula-by-age guide](/en/blog/how-much-formula-by-age/).

Babies on **combination feeding** (breast and formula) usually land between the two tables. Tracking diapers and weight matters more than fitting your baby into one column.

## Why breast and bottle have different rhythms

Three things explain the difference:

- **Digestion:** breast milk is digested faster than formula, so hunger comes back sooner.
- **Variable volume:** at the breast, the amount per feed changes with the time of day, the side, and the previous feed. Sometimes your baby takes a little and comes back soon; sometimes they take a lot and sleep longer.
- **Nursing isn't only eating:** at the breast, babies also calm down, warm up and settle into sleep. Some short feeds are about comfort, and that's part of breastfeeding.

That's why the AAP describes spacing at the breast as a slow process. Comparing your breastfed baby with a friend's baby who takes a bottle every 4 hours only adds stress.

## How to count the interval (and why it feels like they eat all the time)

The right way to count is **from the start of one feed to the start of the next**. For example: your baby started at 2:00 pm, fed until 2:40 and asked again at 4:00. The interval was **2 hours**, not 1 hour 20.

Counting from the end of the feed makes the interval look much shorter. It's one of the most common reasons parents feel their baby "eats all day." Writing down the start time of every feed for a day or two usually reveals a pattern a tired memory can't see.

## Should I wake my baby to feed?

It depends on the stage:

- **Until your baby is back to birth weight** (usually between day 10 and day 14): yes. Don't let more than **4 hours** pass without a feed, overnight included, unless your pediatrician advises a different interval. Sleepy newborns sometimes "skip" a feed they still need. The typical 2 to 3 hours in the table is normal; 4 hours is the limit.
- **After that, with weight gain confirmed by the pediatrician:** you generally don't need to wake them on a fixed schedule. If they stretch out a longer sleep at night, enjoy it.
- **Exceptions:** babies born premature or small, with jaundice, or with borderline weight gain. In these cases the pediatrician sets the maximum interval, and it's worth following what they advise.

## What about nights?

Night feeds stay normal for many months, especially at the breast. In the study mentioned above, they were common and made up an important share of the day's milk. For breastfeeding, they also help maintain supply.

What usually changes with age:

- **0 to 2 months:** night looks like day, with several feeds at intervals similar to daytime.
- **2 to 4 months:** many babies start stretching one block of 4 to 6 hours, more often on formula than at the breast.
- **4 to 6 months and beyond:** one or two feeds a night are still common and don't signal a problem. Breastfed babies may keep more than that.

If nights are rough, our [sleep-by-age guide](/en/blog/how-long-should-baby-sleep-by-age/) helps separate hunger from normal night waking.

## Hunger and fullness cues

Watching your baby works better than watching the clock. **Early hunger cues** are rooting (turning the head toward the breast), opening the mouth, licking the lips, bringing a hand to the mouth and getting restless. Crying is a late cue: a baby already crying from hunger has a harder time latching or taking the bottle.

**Fullness cues** are letting go of the breast or nipple, turning the face away, closing the mouth, relaxing the hands and getting drowsy. The same goes for bottles: feed with your baby more upright, pause during the feed and stop when they show they're satisfied, even if milk is left over. This is called responsive feeding, and the AAP recommends it for formula too.

## When they ask every hour

Very close feeds are alarming, but most of the time there's an explanation:

- **Cluster feeding:** blocks of back-to-back feeds, almost always in the late afternoon and evening. It's common in the first weeks and during growth spurts. Our [cluster feeding guide](/en/blog/cluster-feeding-explained/) explains it in detail.
- **Hungrier phases:** every so often your baby asks for more for a few days and then goes back to their rhythm. They're usually described in the first weeks and around 3 months, but the timing varies from baby to baby.
- **Comfort sucking:** not every cry is hunger. Holding, a diaper change or a burping break settles some of it.

With bottles, very close feeds deserve an extra look for **overfeeding**: a baby who drinks too fast doesn't always show they're full. A slow-flow nipple and pauses during the feed help them notice fullness.

> **See the pediatrician** if your baby wants to feed every hour all day for several days, **or** if any of these show up: fewer than 6 well-wet diapers a day, weak feeds, pain while breastfeeding, or weight gain below expectations. It may be a latch or milk-transfer problem, and a breastfeeding assessment usually sorts it out. Also seek care if your baby sleeps too much and is hard to wake for feeds in the first weeks. A very sleepy baby with a dry mouth or almost no pee needs to be seen the same day.

## The right interval is the one that keeps your baby growing

The table is a starting point. Three signs confirm the rhythm is working, the same ones in our ["is my baby getting enough milk?" guide](/en/blog/is-baby-getting-enough-milk/):

- **Diapers:** at least 6 well-wet diapers a day after the first week, and regular dirty diapers in the first weeks (our [wet diapers per day guide](/en/blog/wet-diapers-per-day/) has the table).
- **Weight:** gain along the growth curve, checked by the pediatrician at visits.
- **Behavior:** a baby who is active during feeds and settled for a while afterward.

If all three are going well, your baby's rhythm is probably fine, even if it doesn't match the table. When in doubt, the pediatrician makes the call.

## After 6 months

As solids begin, meals gradually take up more room. At the breast, breastfeeding continues on demand, and the WHO recommends continuing to 2 years or beyond. On formula, it's about 4 to 5 feeds a day at 6 months, and the number tends to drop as meals progress, fitted in between them. To plan this period, see our [starting solids guide](/en/blog/blw-vs-purees-6-months/).

## Track the start time, not just the amount

At a checkup, "they eat all the time" tells the pediatrician very little. Something like "10 feeds a day, every 2 h 20 on average, with a 4-hour stretch at night" helps much more. When you log each feed (time, side or volume), the average interval is ready for you, and you can see when the rhythm really changed.
