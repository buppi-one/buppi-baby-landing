At 7 months, your baby's sleep has a clearer skeleton: a longer night stretch, a handful of more organized naps, and wake windows much longer than in the early months. But it's also a stage of new developments that can shake up nights — the **3-to-2 nap transition** and the arrival of **separation anxiety**. This guide covers how much a 7-month-old usually sleeps, how naps are going, wake windows, a flexible schedule, why sleep can wobble again, and what changes in safe sleep at this age.

## How much a 7-month-old sleeps

In total, a 7-month-old sleeps about **12 to 15 hours a day**, counting night and naps, with the average around **14 hours**. Most of it now happens **at night** — many babies do a long night stretch — and daytime sleep concentrates into fewer but more structured naps. As always, it's a wide range of normal: the best sign that things are fine isn't hitting a number, but a baby who eats well, gains weight, and is alert and curious when awake. (For the full picture across ages, see the [how much sleep by age table](/en/blog/how-long-should-baby-sleep-by-age/).)

## Wake windows at 7 months

The **[wake window](/en/blog/wake-windows-by-age/)** — how long your baby can comfortably stay awake between sleeps — is usually **2.5 to 3.5 hours** at 7 months, much longer than in the early months. The **last window of the day** (before night sleep) tends to be the longest. Rule of thumb: if your baby is on **2 naps**, windows are closer to 3 hours; if still on **3 naps**, closer to 2.5.

Going much beyond that tends to leave the baby **overtired**: more wound up and, paradoxically, harder to settle. Keep **reading sleepy cues** — yawning, rubbing their eyes, going quieter or fussier, losing interest in toys — instead of just timing.

## Naps at 7 months: 2 or 3?

Seven months sits **right in the middle of the 3-to-2 nap transition**, which usually happens between 6 and 9 months. So at 7 months it's equally common to find:

- **Babies already on 2 longer naps** (morning and early afternoon), with windows of ~3 hours;
- **Babies still on 3 naps**, the third being a short late-afternoon nap to "bridge" to bedtime.

Both are normal. The sign it's time to move to 2 naps is usually the **third nap getting harder and harder** (the baby resists it, or it pushes bedtime too late). During the transition, expect a few days of a slightly tired baby by evening — bringing bedtime a bit earlier helps you get through it.

## A flexible one-day schedule

There's no fixed schedule at 7 months. This example uses **2 naps and windows of ~2h45 to 3.5 hours** — but if your baby still does **3 naps**, add a short third nap in the late afternoon (around 4:30–5:00 pm) and shorten the windows to ~2.5 hours. Both routines are valid; follow your baby's. Use it as an elastic reference:

| Time (example) | What happens |
|---|---|
| 7:00 | Wakes and feeds |
| 9:45–11:00 | 1st nap |
| 11:00 | Milk + solids |
| 14:00–15:15 | 2nd nap |
| 15:30 | Milk, awake time |
| 17:30 | Dinner (solids) |
| 18:30 | Milk, calm routine |
| 19:00–19:30 (varies) | Start of night sleep |
| Overnight | 0–2 wakings |

Notice: **solids** now enter the picture (complementary feeding starts around 6 months), but **milk — breast or formula — stays the base of nutrition** through this first year. Food doesn't replace milk feeds yet.

## Sleep at night

At 7 months, many babies do a **long night stretch**, and some spend most of the night without asking to feed. Even so, **waking 0 to 2 times stays within normal** — the frequency varies a lot, and breastfed babies tend to feed more often. It's not the time to cut night feeds on your own; talk to your pediatrician if you want to adjust.

What tends to **disrupt** nights at 7 months often isn't hunger, but development (see below), a tooth coming in, or a mistimed wake window leaving the baby overtired. Still, in this solids-introduction phase daytime intake can fluctuate — so **real hunger can also wake the baby**, and it's worth considering before ruling out.

## Why sleep can wobble again

Two new things at this stage stir up sleep, and neither is a "true regression":

- **Separation anxiety.** Between 6 and 8 months, your baby develops **object permanence** — they understand you still exist out of sight. But they don't yet know you *always come back*, so they protest at being left, including at bedtime and during night wakings. It's a developmental milestone. What helps: a predictable routine, short and calm goodbyes (don't sneak off), and playing peekaboo during the day, which teaches that you come back.
- **Motor skills.** Sitting steadily, rolling side to side, and crawling are achievements your baby wants to **practice** — sometimes in the crib, sometimes waking at night to "train." It's temporary; give plenty of room to practice while awake during the day.

To **tell the causes apart**: teething pain usually comes with drooling, swollen gums, and daytime fussiness too, and doesn't ease just because you appear; separation anxiety, on the other hand, calms when you show up and comfort. In either case, what helps is **staying consistent** (a light routine, wake windows, safe sleep) rather than inventing new habits you don't want to keep. And remember: if the worsening comes with fever, inconsolable crying, or signs of persistent pain, think of a physical cause and see your pediatrician.

## Safe sleep at 7 months

At 7 months your baby already rolls and sits steadily — and that changes part of the guidance.

> If your baby already **rolls and moves on their own**, you can leave them in whatever position they choose during sleep; no need to keep flipping them all night. What matters is always **placing them on their back to sleep**, in a **crib with a firm, flat mattress** and **nothing loose** — pillows, cushions, bumpers, or blankets. Once your baby **can sit or push up on hands and knees** — and even more so when they pull to stand — **lower the crib mattress** to its lowest level, so they can't fall out. To keep your baby warm, use a **sleep sack** instead of a blanket. Room-sharing (not bed-sharing) with parents is advised for at least the first 6 months, ideally up to a year.

## What helps sleep at 7 months

- **Keep a light, predictable routine** before sleep (bath, milk, low light, a song), in the same order — predictability soothes separation anxiety
- **Respect wake windows** and the last window of the day, so your baby doesn't reach night overtired
- **Put your baby down drowsy but still awake** when you can — the ability to fall asleep on their own helps them resettle at the wakings between cycles (which have several causes — hunger, teeth, development — so it's one piece, not the only one)
- **Give plenty of awake motor practice** during the day (free floor time, room to sit and crawl), so your baby "spends" the urge to train outside the crib

On **sleep training**: from 4–6 months some more structured methods can be considered, and the decision is the family's — worth discussing with your pediatrician. None of it is mandatory.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Sleep at 7 months still varies, but it's worth talking to your pediatrician if:

- Your baby is **very sleepy**, hard to wake, or **isn't gaining weight** as expected
- Your baby **snores loudly**, pauses in breathing, or seems to work hard to breathe while asleep
- The sleep trouble comes with **extreme irritability**, fever or other signs something's wrong
- Your baby **doesn't sit without support**, or doesn't interact/respond as expected (worth checking development)
- You're **exhausted to the point of not coping** — asking for help is also caring for your baby

## What to expect next

Seven months is a stage of more structured sleep, but with new bumps — the nap transition and separation anxiety. In the following months, around 8 and 9 months, a rougher patch is common as separation combines with big motor leaps (crawling, pulling to stand) — and that passes too. The trick stays the same as in the [6-month guide](/en/blog/baby-sleep-6-months/): go at your baby's pace, with wake windows, a light routine, safe sleep and patience.
