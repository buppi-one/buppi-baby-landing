At 6 months, your baby's sleep looks very different from the chaos of the early months: nights are longer and more organized, naps start to have set times, and the baby stays awake and curious for much longer. It's also a stage of decisions — night feeds, their own room, and whether (and how) to work on sleep. This guide covers how much a 6-month-old typically sleeps, how many naps they take, the longer wake windows, a flexible schedule, and what to weigh in these choices.

## How much a 6-month-old sleeps

In total, a 6-month-old sleeps about [**12 to 15 hours a day**](/en/blog/how-long-should-baby-sleep-by-age/), counting night sleep and naps, with the average around **14 hours**. The big difference from the early months is that **most of the sleep now happens at night**, in longer blocks, and daytime naps are more defined.

At 6 months, the **circadian rhythm** is well established, the baby produces melatonin at night, and can manage more consolidated stretches of night sleep. Add to that the start of **solids** (complementary feeding), which usually happens around this age and is part of the new routine.

## Wake windows and naps

At 6 months, the **[wake window](/en/blog/wake-windows-by-age/)** (the comfortable awake time between sleeps) is around **2 to 3 hours** — much longer than in the early months. It's usually shorter in the morning (the first window of the day) and longer before night sleep.

**Naps** enter an important transition: between 6 and 9 months, many babies move from **3 to 2 naps** that are longer and more predictable — usually one mid-morning and one early afternoon. It's gradual: some days the baby still wants a short third nap in the late afternoon, and that's fine. On transition days, when the baby arrives **exhausted** in the late afternoon, an **earlier bedtime** helps make up for it and avoids "overtired."

Keep **watching for sleepy cues** — yawning, a distant stare, going quieter or crankier, losing interest in play — alongside the clock. At 6 months the two work well together.

## A flexible one-day schedule

There's no single agenda, but an example with **2 naps** helps you picture the 6-month rhythm. Use it as an elastic reference, not a rule:

| Time (example) | What happens |
|---|---|
| 7:00 | Wakes and feeds |
| 8:30 | Play and **1 solid meal** (solids start with 1 a day) |
| 9:30–11:00 | 1st nap |
| 11:00 | Feed |
| 13:00 | Feed |
| 13:30–15:00 | 2nd nap |
| 15:30 | Feed |
| 16:30–17:00 | Short third support nap (on days it's needed) |
| 18:30 | Feed |
| 19:00–19:30 | Wind-down and night sleep |
| Overnight | 0–2 wake-ups (variable) |

Notice: it's **2 to 3 naps** (the 3rd still shows up often at 6 months), a longer night sleep, and **just 1 solid meal** — because solids start slowly. Night wakings are now **less frequent**, but still normal.

## Night sleep

At 6 months, many babies do **long night blocks**, and some already sleep through. But **still waking 1 or 2 times is common and normal** — every baby has their own rhythm. Factors like **teething**, the start of **separation anxiety** (which usually appears around 6 to 8 months), and developmental leaps can disrupt nights temporarily.

On **night feeds**: at 6 months, many babies could go through the night without feeding, but that's **individual**. Whether and when to reduce the overnight feeds is a conversation with your pediatrician, especially considering weight gain and breastfeeding. There's no rush and no single rule.

An important myth to bust: **solids don't make babies sleep better**. Until age 1, **milk (breast milk or formula) is still the main source of nutrition** — solids complement, they don't replace it. Filling the baby with purée (or putting cereal in the bottle) hoping to "get through the night" doesn't work and isn't recommended.

## The 6-month decisions

### Own room?

Guidelines recommend **room-sharing** (baby in their own crib, in the parents' room) for **at least the first 6 months**. After that, moving the baby to their own room is a **family decision** — what doesn't change are the safe-sleep rules.

### Working on sleep?

From around **4 to 6 months**, behavioral sleep methods become an **option** for families who want them — always gently and respecting the baby. To be clear: it's **not mandatory** and not the only way to build healthy sleep. Many babies improve just with a consistent routine, appropriate wake windows, and time. It's a family choice, ideally discussed with your pediatrician.

What helps on any path:

- A **consistent sleep routine** (bath, feed/dinner, dim light, a song)
- **Respecting the wake windows** and sleepy cues
- **Putting the baby down still awake**, so they practice falling asleep on their own
- A **supportive environment**: dark room, comfortable temperature, white noise if it helps

> **Safe sleep, always:** put the baby to sleep **on their back**, on a **firm, flat mattress**, with **no** pillows, cushions, bumpers, or loose blankets. At 6 months, many babies roll on their own: **keep laying them down on their back**, but if the baby rolls over during sleep, you don't need to keep repositioning them all night. Swaddling should have stopped well before this — **at the first sign the baby was trying to roll** (around 2 to 4 months) — because a swaddled baby who rolls onto their front is at risk of suffocation.

## When to talk to the pediatrician

Sleep at 6 months still has ups and downs, but it's worth talking to the pediatrician if:

- The baby **snores loudly**, has pauses in breathing, or seems to work hard to breathe while asleep
- Sleep **suddenly gets worse** along with signs of illness (fever, ear-pulling, cries of pain, congestion) — it could be an ear infection, a virus, or another acute cause that deserves a check
- There's a **major, persistent worsening** of sleep, with no improvement, or a lot of irritability
- The baby **isn't gaining weight** well, or you have questions about reducing night feeds
- Sleep deprivation is heavily affecting your physical or mental health — asking for help is also caring for your baby

## What to expect next

Six months is often a relief after the 4-month regression: more predictable naps, longer nights, and a more interactive baby who sits and explores. There will still be hard nights — teeth, motor milestones, separation anxiety — but the foundation is firmer. The trick stays the same: routine, wake windows, safe sleep, and patience. And in this stage's decisions (night feeds, room, sleep), remember there's **no single right way** — there's what works for your family.
