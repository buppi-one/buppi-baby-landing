**Quick answer:** at 15 weeks you are in the 4th month and the early part of the 2nd trimester. Your baby is **about 10.1 cm** from head to bottom and weighs **about 110 g**, roughly the size of an apple. This week the eyes react to light and hearing starts to work.

"Fifteen weeks" means fifteen completed weeks, from 15 weeks and 0 days to 15 weeks and 6 days, counted from the first day of your last period. The due date is set at 40 weeks, so there are about 25 to go. Converting weeks to months is approximate, which is why your care team always talks in weeks.

## How your baby is developing at 15 weeks

The length of about 10.1 cm runs from head to bottom and leaves out the legs, which are curled up and hard to measure. The figure comes from the NHS week-by-week guide. The weight of about 110 g is the median on the World Health Organization fetal growth charts, estimated by ultrasound. If your scan report shows different numbers, your care team will interpret them.

This week your baby's body is being covered in lanugo, a layer of fine, soft hair. Eyebrows and eyelashes are starting to grow. The eyes are already sensitive to light.

According to the NHS, hearing starts to work around this week, and your baby begins to pick up sounds such as your voice, your heartbeat and the sounds of your digestive system.

Your baby has been moving for some time, even though you cannot feel it yet. For now the movements are too slight for you to feel. Most women first notice them between 16 and 24 weeks, and in a first pregnancy often after 20. Feeling nothing at 15 weeks is what you would expect.

## What changes in your body

For many women the sickness and tiredness of the first trimester have eased, and a bump is starting to show. In some women the nausea lasts longer, and bump size varies a lot from one pregnancy to another. The most common symptoms at this stage:

- Jabbing pains on the sides of your bump when you stand up, cough or turn over in bed. They usually come from the ligaments that support your womb being stretched, and resting with your feet up helps. Severe pain, or pain that does not go away, needs to be checked.
- More vaginal discharge, clear or white and without a strong smell, which is expected in pregnancy. If it changes colour, smells bad, looks like cottage cheese or comes with itching and soreness, book an appointment, because it may be a vaginal infection.
- Mildly itchy skin, mostly on your bump. The NHS suggests an unperfumed moisturiser, loose cotton clothing and a cool or warm bath. Very itchy skin, itching that gets worse at night or itching on the palms of your hands and soles of your feet can be a sign of a liver condition and needs urgent medical advice, without waiting for your next appointment.
- A blocked nose, the occasional nosebleed and gums that bleed when you brush.
- Headaches, dizziness, heartburn, constipation, leg cramps and feeling hot. A severe headache, or one that does not go away, needs to be checked.

As your bump grows you also become less steady on your feet, which matters most on buses and trains. If there is no free seat, ask someone for theirs.

If you sleep on your front, you may start to find it uncomfortable. For now, any position you find comfortable is fine. The NHS advises going to sleep on your side, left or right, and the research behind that advice concerns the period after 28 weeks. You can start practising now, with a pillow between your knees.

Urine infections are common in pregnancy and, left untreated, can cause problems for you and your baby. The signs are pain or burning when you pee, needing to pee often while passing little, cloudy or bloodstained urine and pain low in your tummy. Drinking water through the day and not holding on when you need to go help prevent them. Any of these signs, even a mild one, calls for prompt medical advice, and the treatment is decided by whoever sees you. With a high temperature or pain in your back, get seen the same day.

## Prenatal care at this stage

Schedules differ by country, so here is what applies in England and what applies in the US.

In England, the next routine appointment is at 16 weeks. The NHS plans at least 10 appointments if this is your first baby and at least 7 if you have given birth before, plus 2 ultrasound scans. No routine scan falls in week 15. The next one is the 20-week screening scan, which usually takes place between 18 and 21 weeks, so it is worth checking that yours is booked.

In the US, the timing of second-trimester visits and scans is set with your ob-gyn or midwife. Ask at your next visit what is planned and when.

On vaccines, the flu vaccine is recommended in England at any stage of pregnancy during the flu season. For whooping cough, the NHS usually offers the vaccine around week 20 and can give it from week 16, while in the US the CDC and ACOG recommend it between 27 and 36 weeks.

## What to do this week

1. Check the date of your next appointment. In England it is the 16-week one.
2. Make sure your mid-pregnancy scan is booked. In England it usually takes place between 18 and 21 weeks.
3. Start pelvic floor exercises. The NHS points to this stage as a good time to begin, because they help prevent leaks when you cough, laugh or sneeze. Your midwife or doctor can show you the technique.
4. Keep moving. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) recommends at least 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity a week, avoiding contact sports and activities with a risk of falling. Check with your care team what suits your situation.
5. If you have not told your employer, think about when to do it. In the UK, once you tell them you have maternity rights, can attend antenatal appointments in paid work time and can ask for a risk assessment of your workplace.

> Get medical advice without waiting for your next appointment if you have vaginal bleeding, fluid leaking from your vagina, strong or persistent abdominal pain, cramps that come at regular intervals, a high temperature, pain or burning when you pee, a severe headache or vomiting that stops you keeping food or fluids down. Call your maternity unit or midwife (UK) or your ob-gyn (US). Fainting, shortness of breath or chest pain are an emergency: call 999 or 911.
